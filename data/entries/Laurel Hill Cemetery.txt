# Laurel Hill Cemetery

**Laurel Hill Cemetery** is a historic rural cemetery located on the east bank of the Schuylkill River in the East Falls neighborhood of Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Founded in 1836, it was the first rural cemetery established in Philadelphia and the second in the United States, following Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge, Massachusetts (1831). Positioned roughly four miles northwest of the city's original boundaries, Laurel Hill was developed as an elite, nonsectarian alternative to overcrowded urban churchyards, combining picturesque landscape design with monumental funereal sculpture.

Conceived during the height of the 19th-century Romantic movement, the cemetery was designed not merely as a resting place for the deceased, but as a public cultural institution, arboretum, and outdoor art gallery. Spanning more than 90 acres following mid-19th-century expansions, the cemetery sits elevated 100 feet above the Schuylkill River, offering dramatic topographic vistas and manicured garden terraces. Its prominent features include monumental architecture by John Notman, mausoleums along "Millionaire's Row," and thousands of sculptured monuments executed by notable American and European artists.

Throughout its history, Laurel Hill served as the preferred burial site for Philadelphia’s political, military, financial, and artistic leadership. Early cemetery managers actively cultivated an "American pantheon" by reinterring key figures of the American Revolution on its grounds. It also became a burial site for dozens of Civil War officers, including General George Gordon Meade, as well as prominent Gilded Age industrial tycoons. In addition to its primary funerary function, Laurel Hill drew tens of thousands of tourists annually during the mid-19th century, directly influencing the creation of public park systems in North America, most notably Fairmount Park.

In the mid-20th century, the site experienced period-specific decline due to regional industrialization and shifting demographic patterns. However, grass-roots preservation efforts initiated in the late 1970s led to comprehensive physical restoration and public reactivation. In 1998, Laurel Hill was designated a National Historic Landmark in recognition of its architectural, landscape, and cultural historical significance.

## History

### Origins and Founding (1835–1836)

Between 1800 and 1830, the population of Philadelphia expanded rapidly, growing by 133 percent from 81,000 to 189,000 residents within the narrow bounds between Vine and Cedar (South) Streets. This rapid urbanization severely strained city infrastructure, particularly church burial grounds. Older interments, such as the Friends' Burial Ground at Arch and Fourth Streets—in continuous use since 1701—contained nearly 20,000 bodies squeezed into less than half a city block. These urban yards suffered from spatial exhaustion, poor sanitary conditions, and commercial redevelopment pressures. Most Philadelphians were buried based on socio-economic standing: the wealthy in private estate vaults like the Logan Burial Ground at Stenton, and religious minorities or the indigent in urban Potter's Fields that were later converted into public squares (including Washington, Franklin, and Logan Squares).

The concept for Laurel Hill originated in 1835 with John Jay Smith (1798–1881), a Quaker librarian, editor, and horticulturist. After Smith was unable to locate the grave of his young daughter at the Arch Street Meeting House following structural additions to the premises, he resolved to establish an extra-urban, nonsectarian cemetery modeled on European predecessors like Père Lachaise Cemetery outside Paris. In late 1835, Smith organized a founding committee consisting of prominent local figures: former Philadelphia mayor Benjamin W. Richards, druggist Frederick Brown, and merchant Nathan Dunn. In early 1836, the group formally incorporated as the Laurel Hill Cemetery Company.

### Site Selection and Early Growth

In early 1836, the company purchased "Laurel Hill," a 32-acre country estate formerly owned by businessman Joseph Sims, situated along the east bank of the Schuylkill River near the Falls of Schuylkill. Rising 100 feet above the river, the site offered well-drained soil and elevated topography ideal for subterranean interments. Located roughly four miles northwest of Vine Street (then Philadelphia's northern corporate border), the countryside site was isolated from urban industrial growth and expected to retain its picturesque character indefinitely.

The company divided the estate into individual family burial lots averaging 120 square feet, priced between $50 and $150. These costs intentionally limited burial access primarily to affluent citizens. The lot sizes permitted multi-generational family interments alongside the erection of substantial stone monuments and private family mausoleums. To establish national prestige, cemetery managers aggressively pursued an "American pantheon" initiative, persuading families and institutions to exhumed and reinter historic Revolutionary War figures at Laurel Hill. Among those relocated were Charles Thomson (1729–1824), Secretary of the Continental Congress, and General Hugh Mercer (1726–1777), a hero of the Battle of Princeton whose remains were disinterred from Christ Church in Center City and transported up the river on a ceremonial funeral barge.

### Mid-to-Late 19th Century Expansion

```
[1836: North Laurel Hill] ──(1849)──> [South Laurel Hill Added]
                                                │
                                             (1861)
                                                ▼
                                    [Central Laurel Hill Added]
                                                │
                                                ▼
                                   [Bridge over Nicetown Lane]
```

To meet burgeoning demand from elite families, the cemetery managers expanded its physical footprint through land acquisitions between 1849 and 1861:

*   **South Laurel Hill:** In 1849, the company purchased an estate situated south of the original grounds.
*   **Central Laurel Hill:** In 1861, the company acquired the intermediate property separating the North and South parcels.
*   **Connecting Infrastructure:** A stone-and-iron bridge was constructed over Nicetown Lane (now Hunting Park Avenue) to link the Central and South tracts.

These additions expanded the site to over 90 acres. During and after the American Civil War, Laurel Hill became the primary resting place for numerous military leaders, most notably General George Gordon Meade (1815–1872), commander of the Union Army of the Potomac at Gettysburg. Following the war, Gilded Age industrial leaders constructed opulent mausoleums along "Millionaire's Row" in Central Laurel Hill. Prominent figures buried here included saw manufacturer Henry Disston (1819–1878), *Public Ledger* publisher George W. Childs (1829–1894), and streetcar traction magnate P.A.B. Widener (1834–1915).

### Decline and Preservation Movement

By 1900, Laurel Hill was fully surrounded by expanding industrial districts to the north and east and Fairmount Park to the south. Saturated burial lots and shifting residential preferences led many families toward suburban garden cemeteries, such as West Laurel Hill Cemetery, founded by John Jay Smith in Lower Merion Township in 1869. 

Following World War II, Laurel Hill entered an extended period of financial instability, institutional neglect, and physical degradation. Vandals damaged numerous carved Victorian monuments, including Alexander Milne Calder's sculpture on the Warner family plot. 

In 1978, a recovery effort was launched through the creation of the Friends of Laurel Hill Cemetery, founded by Drayton Smith (a direct descendant of John Jay Smith), his wife Jane Smith, and historian John Francis Marion. The Friends organization instituted public heritage tours, secured private capital for structural stabilization, and repaired damaged statuary and masonry. The site was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1977 and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1998.

## Architecture

### Site Layout and Landscape Design

The site layout of Laurel Hill combines early 19th-century English landscape gardening with formal Classical planning. The original ground plan—attributed jointly to Scottish architect John Notman and local surveyor Philip M. Price—was loosely modeled on Kensal Green Cemetery outside London.

The spatial organization progresses from formal design near the main entrance to picturesque, naturalistic landscapes along the river bluff:

```
[Ridge Avenue Entrance] ──> [Formal Medallion Gardens] ──> [Wooded Naturalistic Slopes] ──> [Schuylkill River Terraces]
```

1.  **Entrance & Gardens:** Adjacent to the Ridge Avenue gatehouse, formal gravel paths frame symmetrical shrubbery plots and the circular "Medallion Garden."
2.  **Picturesque Core:** Moving west toward the Schuylkill River, the paths follow natural contours around rock outcrops, mature canopy trees, and sloping hillsides.
3.  **River Terraces:** The landscape culminates in artificial stone terraces cut directly into the hillside, descending 100 feet down to the Schuylkill River shoreline.

To establish the cemetery as a public botanical resource, founder John Jay Smith personally supervised the planting of more than 800 native and exotic trees and ornamental shrubs.

### Gatehouse and Chapel

```
└──────────────────────────────┘              └──────────────────┘
```

In 1836, the board of directors held an architectural competition for the cemetery's main entrance on Ridge Avenue. Prominent Philadelphia architects William Strickland and Thomas Ustick Walter submitted entries utilizing the Egyptian Revival style—a popular aesthetic choice for early 19th-century cemeteries symbolising eternity. However, the commission was awarded to John Notman, a recently arrived 26-year-old Scottish immigrant builder. Notman’s winning design was a Classical Roman Doric entrance structure. Displeased by the board's selection of an unknown artisan, Strickland resigned from the cemetery board.

Notman's completed gatehouse consists of a massive central Roman archway flanked by classical colonnades and crowned with a decorative urn. Shortly after, Notman designed an on-site cemetery chapel in the Gothic Revival style. Because many urban clergy initially refused to travel four miles to officiate services at Laurel Hill—or objected to the loss of burial fees at their own parish yards—an on-site chapel was essential for nonsectarian funeral services. Notman's chapel stood inside the gatehouse entrance until the 1880s, when it was demolished to clear additional ground for lucrative burial lots.

### Monuments, Sculpture, and "Millionaire's Row"

Laurel Hill functions as an open-air archive of 19th- and early 20th-century American sculptural styles, containing Classical Revival obelisks, Victorian Gothic shrines, Egyptian pylons, and Art Nouveau vaults.

*   ***Old Mortality* Statuary Group:** Installed directly inside the gatehouse courtyard within a Tudor-style enclosure designed by Notman, this sandstone sculpture group was carved by Scottish sculptor James Thom. Purchased by cemetery managers in the late 1830s, the life-size statuary depicts author Sir Walter Scott conversing with "Old Mortality" (Robert Paterson), a legendary 18th-century itinerant re-carver of Scottish Covenanter tombstones, alongside his pony. It served as a moral exemplar to visiting lot holders regarding the duty of memorializing the dead.
*   **The Warner Lot:** Located in Central Laurel Hill, this family plot features a bronze monument carved by Alexander Milne Calder (1846–1923), sculptor of the statuary on Philadelphia City Hall. The sculpture depicts a shrouded female figure lifting a heavy sarcophagus lid to symbolize spiritual resurrection.
*   **"Millionaire's Row":** Situated along the high ridgeline of Central Laurel Hill parallel to Hunting Park Avenue, this avenue features concentrated Classical, Egyptian, and Renaissance Revival mausoleums designed for post-Civil War industrialists. Highly detailed structures include the private family vaults of publisher George W. Childs, manufacturer Henry Disston, and financier P.A.B. Widener.

## Cultural Significance

### Tourism and Public Recreation

During the 19th century, Laurel Hill developed into one of Philadelphia's primary tourist attractions, drawing roughly 30,000 visitors per year by the mid-1840s. In an era preceding municipal public art museums, civic parks, or public botanical gardens, the cemetery served as a multi-purpose cultural destination. Guidebooks and magazines like *Godey’s Lady’s Book* praised the site as a necessary stop for regional travelers, noting that it housed "our most responsible families in every walk of life."

```
                 [Transportation Routes to Laurel Hill]
                                   │
         ┌─────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                                   ▼
[Ridge Avenue Horse-Car & Carriage]        [Schuylkill River Steamboats]
                                                             │
                                                             ▼
                                               [Landing at Hunting Park Ave.]
```

To reach the grounds, visitors traveled either along Ridge Avenue by private carriage and horse-car, or via scenic steamboat lines departing from the Fairmount Water Works up the Schuylkill River directly to the Laurel Hill steamboat landing at Hunting Park Avenue. Due to immense public crowds, cemetery managers established a ticketed admission system. Furthermore, managers closed the grounds to the general public on Sundays, restricting Sunday entry exclusively to lot-holders and their pass-bearing guests—a policy that effectively excluded working-class Philadelphians whose only day off was Sunday.

### Influence on Fairmount Park and Urban Planning

The commercial success and popularity of Laurel Hill triggered a regional "rural cemetery boom." Between 1840 and 1876, more than twenty similar landscape cemeteries opened across Greater Philadelphia, often catering to specific demographic, religious, or racial groups:

*   **The Woodlands** (West Philadelphia, 1840): Primary competitor for Philadelphia's Protestant elite.
*   **Cathedral & New Cathedral Cemeteries** (1849): Established for Catholic interments.
*   **Lebanon & Olive Cemeteries**: Established for African American burials.
*   **Mount Sinai & Montefiore Cemeteries**: Established for Jewish interments.
*   **Harleigh Cemetery** (Camden, New Jersey, 1885): Final resting place of poet Walt Whitman.

Laurel Hill’s popular success as a picturesque retreat directly catalyzed the urban public park movement in Pennsylvania. When attorney Eli Kirk Price (1797–1881) advocated for the state legislation creating Fairmount Park in 1867, he cited the overwhelming public overcrowding at rural cemeteries as proof that the city required vast, municipally owned green spaces. Price, who served simultaneously as a founding manager of The Woodlands and an original Fairmount Park Commissioner, sought to relieve urban pressure on rural cemeteries by establishing public parkland. Landscape designer James Clark Sidney incorporated spatial layout techniques developed at South Laurel Hill into his original master plan for Fairmount Park.

### Notable Interments

Laurel Hill contains the graves of prominent military, political, scientific, and cultural figures:

*   **Military Figures:** General George Gordon Meade (victor at Gettysburg), along with more than 40 Civil War general officers and hundreds of Union veterans.
*   **Revolutionary Leaders:** Charles Thomson (Secretary of the Continental Congress) and General Hugh Mercer.
*   **Industrialists and Financiers:** Henry Disston (founder of Keystone Saw Works), George W. Childs (publisher and philanthropist), and P.A.B. Widener (traction and steel magnate).
*   **Artists, Architects, and Cultural Figures:** Thomas Sully (portrait painter), Frank Furness (Victorian architect), Alexander Milne Calder (sculptor), and Harry Kalas (veteran play-by-play broadcaster for the Philadelphia Phillies).

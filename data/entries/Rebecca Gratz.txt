## Lead section

**Rebecca Gratz** (March 4, 1781 – August 27, 1869) was an American educator, philanthropist, and communal leader who played a pivotal role in shaping early nineteenth-century American Jewish life and non-sectarian social reform in Philadelphia. Operating at a time when public charitable work by women was expanding in the Early Republic, Gratz established and directed several groundbreaking institutions that addressed poverty, orphan care, and religious education. Her initiatives provided critical welfare services to Philadelphia's growing population and served as models for Jewish benevolent organizations throughout the United States.

Among her most notable achievements was the establishment of the Female Hebrew Benevolent Society in 1819, the first independent non-synagogue-affiliated Jewish women's charitable organization in North America. Recognizing the need for structured religious instruction for youth, Gratz founded the Hebrew Sunday School Society in 1838, pioneering the Sunday school model within American Judaism. In addition to her efforts within the Jewish community, Gratz actively participated in broader civic institutions, co-founding the Philadelphia Orphan Asylum in 1815 and serving as its secretary for more than forty years.

Gratz's intellectual engagement and high standing in Philadelphia's cultural society earned her the friendship of prominent figures of the era, including author Washington Irving and painter Thomas Sully, who painted several celebrated portraits of her. Literary tradition long held that Irving's descriptions of Gratz inspired Sir Walter Scott to create the heroine Rebecca in his famous 1819 historical novel *Ivanhoe*. 

Throughout her long life, Gratz maintained a deep commitment to orthodox Jewish practices while navigating the cultural landscape of early nineteenth-century Philadelphia. Her career demonstrated how women of faith could utilize organizational benevolence to negotiate civic identity, preserve religious heritage, and establish permanent charitable infrastructure in the dynamic urban environment of Philadelphia.

## Early life and education

Rebecca Gratz was born in Philadelphia on March 4, 1781, into a prominent and prosperous merchant family. She was the seventh of twelve children born to Michael Gratz and Miriam Simon Gratz. Her father, Michael, had emigrated from Langendorf, Silesia, in 1759, establishing a successful trading firm with his brother Barnard Gratz. Her mother, Miriam, was the daughter of Joseph Simon, a pioneer land speculator and merchant from Lancaster, Pennsylvania. The Gratz family belonged to Philadelphia's Congregation Mikveh Israel, one of the oldest Jewish congregations in the United States, and maintained a devoutly observant Jewish household.

Growing up in the nation’s temporary capital during the post-Revolutionary era, Gratz was exposed to the political, intellectual, and cultural elites of early America. The Gratz home on Market Street served as a gathering place for leading citizens, merchants, and visiting scholars. Gratz received an unusually thorough private education for a woman of her era, studying English literature, French, history, and classical humanities alongside traditional Jewish texts and ethical teachings. 

The political and social ideals of the American Revolution profoundly influenced Gratz during her formative years. She developed a firm belief in Republican Motherhood and civic duty, combining these American national ideals with the Jewish imperative of *tzedakah* (charity and righteousness). Following the death of her mother in 1808 and her father's subsequent decline in health, Gratz assumed primary responsibility for managing the household and overseeing the upbringing of her younger siblings, establishing her role as a central unifying figure within her extended family.

## Career

Gratz’s public career as a philanthropist began in the early 1800s, during a period marked by rapid urban growth and economic instability in Philadelphia. In 1801, at the age of twenty, she assisted in establishing the Female Association for the Relief of Women and Children in Reduced Circumstances. This non-sectarian organization sought to alleviate the suffering of destitute women and children who fell outside traditional municipal relief systems. Her work with the association exposed her to the systemic poverty affecting urban families and laid the foundation for her lifelong dedication to social welfare.

In 1815, following a devastating yellow fever outbreak and economic hardship, Gratz co-founded the Philadelphia Orphan Asylum alongside other prominent Philadelphia women. She was elected as the institution's secretary, a executive role she held continuously until 1859. In this capacity, Gratz drafted annual reports, managed institutional correspondence, supervised daily administrative operations, and raised crucial funding. Under her guidance, the asylum grew into one of the region's premier child-welfare institutions, establishing standards for orphan care that influenced municipal policies across Pennsylvania.

Recognizing that the Jewish population of Philadelphia required specialized charitable institutions that respected dietary laws and religious customs, Gratz turned her administrative talents toward Jewish communal development. In 1819, she founded the Female Hebrew Benevolent Society (FHBS). Created to assist poor Jewish women, widows, and families, the FHBS operated independently of the male-dominated synagogue governance structure, granting women unprecedented autonomy in managing community relief funds.

Gratz's most enduring legacy in education emerged in 1838 with the founding of the Hebrew Sunday School Society. Modeled structurally on Christian Sunday schools but dedicated entirely to Jewish religious instruction, the school provided free education in Bible history, the Hebrew language, and Jewish moral traditions. Gratz served as the school's superintendent and primary curriculum author, producing instructional materials adapted for American youth. The school offered religious instruction to children of all socio-economic backgrounds, particularly benefiting girls and children of less affluent immigrants who lacked access to private tutors. The model spread rapidly to major cities across the nation, establishing a foundation for modern Jewish supplementary education in the United States.

In her later career, Gratz actively supported the creation of the Jewish Foster Home and Asylum in 1855, ensuring that Jewish orphans received care within a environment consistent with their religious heritage. Her written correspondence reveals a extensive national network, through which she advised women in other cities—such as Charleston, Baltimore, and New York—on how to establish similar charitable and educational societies.

## Personal life

Rebecca Gratz remained unmarried throughout her life, a decision that allowed her to dedicate her complete energies to civic work, family management, and intellectual pursuits. According to long-standing Philadelphia account and family biography, Gratz had been courted by Samuel Ewing, a non-Jewish lawyer and member of a prominent Philadelphia family. Despite mutual affection, Gratz declined to marry outside her Jewish faith, prioritizing her religious commitment and heritage over personal inclination. She remained on friendly terms with Ewing until his death in 1825.

Gratz resided in Center City Philadelphia, maintaining a home that served as a salon for leading writers, artists, jurists, and political figures. She maintained an active correspondence with major literary figures, most notably Washington Irving, who was a close friend of her close companion Matilda Hoffman as well as the Gratz family. Irving frequently visited the Gratz home and deeply admired Rebecca's intellect, character, and devotion to her community.

Gratz was also deeply involved in the life of her extended family. When her sister Rachel Gratz Moses died in 1823, Rebecca took nine of her orphaned nieces and nephews into her home, raising them to adulthood while continuing her demanding public duties. Her surviving letters demonstrate a woman of keen political insight, deep theological reflection, and intense personal devotion to her friends and family.

## Legacy

Rebecca Gratz died in Philadelphia on August 27, 1869, at the age of 88. She was buried in the historic Mikveh Israel Cemetery in Center City Philadelphia. At the time of her death, she was widely eulogized as one of the nation's premier female philanthropists and a pioneer of American Jewish institutional life.

Gratz's historical legacy operates across multiple domains:

* **Pioneer of Female Philanthropy:** She demonstrated that nineteenth-century American women could organize, finance, and direct major benevolent institutions, establishing a template for female social activism in the Early Republic.
* **Architect of American Jewish Education:** The Hebrew Sunday School Society revolutionized Jewish religious education in North America, establishing an accessible organizational framework for educating youth outside of full-time parochial schools.
* **Literary Iconography:** Historical tradition holds that Washington Irving described Gratz's character, beauty, and religious devotion to Sir Walter Scott during a visit to Abbotsford in 1817. Scott subsequently modeled the noble character of Rebecca of York in his famous 1819 novel *Ivanhoe* on Gratz.
* **Artistic Portraiture:** Artist Thomas Sully painted several portraits of Gratz, capturing her prominent place in the elite social and cultural circles of early Philadelphia.

Today, institutions founded by Gratz continue to influence American social work and Jewish communal organization, and her life remains a subject of study regarding the intersection of gender, faith, and civic duty in early American history.

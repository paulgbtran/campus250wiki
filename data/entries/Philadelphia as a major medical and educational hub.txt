## 2. Lead section

Philadelphia's narrative as a premier medical and educational center is an enduring pillar of its cultural and economic identity. Broadly categorized under the regional designation "City of Medicine" and reinforced by its educational institutions, the Philadelphia metropolitan area stands as one of the oldest and most concentrated academic and healthcare networks in the United States. Originating in the colonial era with the founding of the nation's first hospital and its first medical school, the region established an early reputation for scientific rigor, clinical innovation, and institutionalized care that attracted scholars and practitioners from across North America and Europe.

Alongside its medical vanguard, Greater Philadelphia developed a rich ecosystem of higher education institutions driven by a diverse array of religious and civic movements. Second only to Greater Boston in its concentration of independent colleges and universities, the region became a focal point for Quaker, Roman Catholic, and Protestant educational initiatives. Institutions such as Haverford College, Swarthmore College, Bryn Mawr College, Villanova University, Saint Joseph's University, and La Salle University were established to provide both specialized rigorous instruction and "guarded" moral education. Concurrently, groundbreaking medical institutions such as the Woman’s Medical College of Pennsylvania—the first degree-granting medical school for women in the world—and the Homeopathic Medical College of Pennsylvania (later Hahnemann Medical College) broadened access to medical training and expanded clinical paradigms.

In the post-World War II and late twentieth-century eras, as manufacturing declined across the American Rust Belt, Philadelphia underwent a structural economic transformation. The city leveraged its historic medical and academic infrastructure to forge a post-industrial economic model centered on healthcare, biomedical research, and higher education—a sector collectively termed "eds and meds." Municipal initiatives, such as the establishment of the Philadelphia Hospitals and Higher Education Authority and the strategic urban redevelopment of the former Philadelphia General Hospital site, facilitated multi-billion-dollar expansions of major academic medical centers, including the Health System of the University of Pennsylvania (HUP) and Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP).

By the twenty-first century, healthcare and higher education emerged as the largest employment sector in the city, generating billions in patient revenue and driving urban redevelopment across West Philadelphia, Center City, and North Philadelphia. Although this sector has faced challenges regarding tax-exempt land use, labor unionization, and health system consolidations, Philadelphia’s dual identity as a center of learning and healing remains a defining narrative of its urban resilience and cultural heritage.

## 3. Origins

The foundation of Philadelphia’s medical leadership was forged within the challenging disease environment of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. As a burgeoning Atlantic port city, colonial Philadelphia experienced frequent outbreaks of infectious diseases—including smallpox, yellow fever, dysentery, and malaria—introduced through international trade networks. Sanitation challenges, urban crowding, and higher mortality rates among newly arrived immigrants created an urgent demand for systematic medical care. In response, residents relied on a combination of domestic medical guides (such as John Tennent's *Every Man His Own Doctor* and William Buchan's *Domestic Medicine*), botanical remedies derived from Native American traditions, and local midwives and herbalists. 

```
[1751: Pennsylvania Hospital Founded] 
       │
       ▼
[1765: College of Phil. Medical Dept.] ──► [European Humoral Theory (Morgan, Rush)]
       │
       ▼
[1775–1782: Revolutionary Inoculations]
```

Formal institutional care began in 1751 when civic leader Benjamin Franklin and physician Thomas Bond secured a charter to establish Pennsylvania Hospital. Founded to care for the "sick-poor and insane of Philadelphia," it was the first hospital established in the British North American colonies. Its iconic Pine Building, designed by master builder Samuel Rhoads and modeled after the Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh, opened its doors to patients in 1756. The hospital pioneered early humane approaches to psychiatric care, established the nation's first medical pharmacy, built a vast historical medical library containing over 13,000 volumes, and in 1804 erected the nation's first Surgical Amphitheatre, where pioneering operations were observed by generations of students.

Formal American medical education was inaugurated in Philadelphia in 1765 with the opening of the Medical Department of the College of Philadelphia (which later became the University of Pennsylvania Perelman School of Medicine). Spearheaded by Dr. John Morgan and Dr. William Shippen Jr., the institution established a structured academic curriculum modeled on the University of Edinburgh. In his seminal publication, *A Discourse Upon the Institution of Medical Schools in America* (1765), Morgan argued that the complexities of the human body demanded formal university training rather than informal apprenticeships. Early faculty members, including Dr. Benjamin Rush, integrated European theoretical frameworks—such as Herman Boerhaave's balance of body fluids and solids, and William Cullen's miasmatic theories—into clinical practice, relying heavily on therapeutics such as bloodletting, blistering, and purges. 

During the American Revolution, Philadelphia served as the strategic heart of military medicine. The city witnessed widespread smallpox inoculation programs ordered by General George Washington to safeguard Continental troops. Through its early hospitals, medical school, and the formation of professional bodies in neighboring regions (such as the New Jersey Medical Society in 1766), colonial Philadelphia established itself as the intellectual center of medical education and clinical care in early North America.

## 4. Development

Throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Philadelphia's institutional landscape expanded through the growth of religiously affiliated higher education and pioneering medical colleges. The region became a laboratory for diverse educational philosophies. Following the 1827 Hicksite-Orthodox schism within the Religious Society of Friends, Orthodox Quakers founded Haverford College in 1833 to provide an enlarged, "guarded" liberal education. Hicksite Quakers subsequently established Swarthmore College in 1864 as a coeducational institution situated in a rural environment, while Bryn Mawr College was established in 1885 under the leadership of M. Carey Thomas, providing women with an academic curriculum equal to that of elite male universities.

```
[19th C. Religious & Specialized Ed]
 ├── Quaker Colleges: Haverford (1833), Swarthmore (1864), Bryn Mawr (1885)
 ├── Catholic Higher Ed: Villanova (1842), St. Joseph's (1851), La Salle (1863)
 └── Medical Pioneers: Hahnemann (1848), Woman's Medical College of PA (1850)
```

Simultaneously, Roman Catholic orders established educational networks to protect youth from secular and anti-Catholic influences, a movement later reinforced by Archdiocesan directives under Cardinals Dennis Dougherty and John O'Hara. The Augustinians established Villanova College in 1842 on the suburban Main Line; the Jesuits founded Saint Joseph's College in 1851; and the Christian Brothers chartered La Salle College in 1863. To serve Catholic women, religious orders founded single-sex institutions in the 1920s, including Chestnut Hill College, Rosemont College, and Immaculata College. Over the course of the twentieth century, these institutions adapted to broader societal shifts, adopting coeducation and expanding into comprehensive regional universities.

Philadelphia also spearheaded progressive and specialized medical instruction. In 1848, the Homeopathic Medical College of Pennsylvania was established to teach Samuel Hahnemann’s system of homeopathic medicine, eventually evolving into Hahnemann Medical College, a premier center for cardiovascular surgery and subspecialty medicine. In 1850, visionary reformers founded the Female Medical College of Pennsylvania (later the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania, or MCP), the world's first degree-granting medical school for women. MCP produced generations of female clinicians and international researchers, breaking systemic barriers in medicine.

```
[Late 20th C. Economic Shift & Consolidation]
 ├── 1974: Municipal Hospitals & Higher Education Authority Created
 ├── 1977: Philadelphia General Hospital (PGH) Closed
 ├── 1980s–1990s: PGH Site Redeveloped into Center for Health Care Sciences
 └── 1996–2002: AHERF Collapse ──► MCP + Hahnemann Merge into Drexel Univ. College of Medicine
```

By the late twentieth century, structural economic pressures precipitated institutional consolidation and municipal interventions. In 1974, the city established the Hospital Authority (later the Hospitals and Higher Education Authority of Philadelphia) to issue tax-exempt revenue bonds for facility expansion. Following Mayor Frank Rizzo’s controversial closing of the public Philadelphia General Hospital (PGH) in 1977, municipal leaders partnered with private institutions to redevelop the 21-acre West Philadelphia site. The resulting Philadelphia Center for Health Care Sciences enabled massive physical expansions for the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania (HUP), Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP), and Children's Seashore House. Following the 1998 bankruptcy of the Allegheny Health, Education and Research Foundation (AHERF)—which had acquired MCP and Hahnemann—Tenet Healthcare acquired the clinical assets, while Drexel University assumed management of the merged academic medical schools, creating the Drexel University College of Medicine in 2002.

## 5. Impact

By the early twenty-first century, the expansion of higher education and healthcare—widely known as the "eds and meds" sector—had reshaped Philadelphia's physical environment, employment structure, and civic policy. Data from the 2012 U.S. Economic Census established healthcare as the single largest employment sector in Philadelphia. By 2013, nine of the city’s sixteen largest private employers were major hospitals or health systems, including HUP, CHOP, Thomas Jefferson University Hospital, Temple University Health System, and Albert Einstein Medical Center. While industrial manufacturing declined, employment in the local health and education sector expanded by 18 percent in the first decade of the 2000s alone.

```
[Post-Industrial Economic Shift]
  Manufacturing Economy Decline  ──►  Rise of "Eds & Meds" Sector
                                      ├── Top City Employment (2012 Census)
                                      ├── Multi-Billion Dollar Revitalizations
                                      └── Institutional Anchor Expansions
```

This sector became a primary driver of urban redevelopment. Facilities such as Penn’s Perelman Center for Advanced Medicine, CHOP’s Colket Translational Research Center, and Cooper University Health Care's expanded campus in Camden, New Jersey, transformed former public and industrial lands into research corridors. These physical institutional expansions spurred surrounding retail, transit, and residential growth, establishing districts like University City as regional economic engines.

However, the dominance of tax-exempt medical and educational mega-institutions also created civic and financial tension. Because hospitals and universities operate as non-profit entities, their land acquisitions removed valuable real estate from municipal tax rolls. Controversies surrounding Payments-in-Lieu-of-Taxes (PILOTs) highlighted ongoing disputes between city leaders seeking tax revenue for public services and institutions arguing that their economic contributions, job creation, and uncompensated clinical care offset their tax-exempt status.

Labor dynamics have also played a central role in the sector's history. Organizations such as District 1199C of the National Union of Hospital and Health Care Employees, led for decades by labor leader Henry Nicholas, organized thousands of service, maintenance, and technical workers across Philadelphia’s medical centers. Landmark labor milestones—including the tragic 1972 death of organizer Norman Rayford during a campaign at Metropolitan Hospital—underscored the intense struggles over wages, working conditions, and equity within the healthcare economy. 

Despite these challenges, Philadelphia's legacy as a medical and educational hub remains foundational. By fusing historic academic roots with contemporary clinical and scientific research, the region continues to serve as an international center for medical training, patient care, and higher education.

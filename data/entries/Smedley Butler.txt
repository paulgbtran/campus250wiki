## Lead section

Major General Smedley Darlington Butler (July 30, 1881 – June 21, 1940), nicknamed "Old Gimlet Eye" and "The Fighting Quaker," was a senior officer in the United States Marine Corps, a prominent civic reformer in Philadelphia, and one of the most decorated soldiers in American military history. Over a military career spanning more than three decades, Butler participated in major foreign conflicts across Cuba, the Philippines, China, Central America, the Caribbean, and Europe. He remains one of only nineteen individuals—and one of just two Marines—to be awarded the Medal of Honor twice for separate military actions.

In addition to his distinguished combat record, Butler played a significant role in Philadelphia regional history during the 1920s. Upon the request of Philadelphia Mayor-elect W. Freeland Kendrick, President Calvin Coolidge authorized a temporary leave of absence for Butler from the Marine Corps to serve as Philadelphia's Director of Public Safety from January 1924 through December 1925. In this municipal role, he launched a fierce, militarized campaign against bootlegging, illegal speakeasies, political corruption, and organized crime during the height of Prohibition, fundamentally reshaping local law enforcement practices while engaging in public clashes with the city's entrenched political machine.

Following his retirement from active military service in 1931, Butler emerged as a prominent anti-war advocate and outspoken critic of American foreign policy and military interventionism. He authored the influential 1935 book *War Is a Racket*, in which he argued that foreign military interventions primarily served the commercial interests of Wall Street bankers and corporate conglomerates at the expense of enlisted service members and taxpayers.

Butler achieved further national attention in 1934 when he testified before the U.S. House of Representatives regarding the "Business Plot," an alleged fascist conspiracy by wealthy industrialists to overthrow President Franklin D. Roosevelt and install a military dictatorship. Butler’s unique path from highly decorated imperial military commander to municipal law enforcement director and anti-imperialist reformer makes him an enduring figure in both Philadelphia civic lore and national military history.

## Early life and education

Smedley Darlington Butler was born on July 30, 1881, in West Chester, the county seat of Chester County, Pennsylvania. He was raised in a prominent Quaker family with deep political roots in the Philadelphia suburbs. His father, Thomas S. Butler, was a respected attorney who later served as a Republican member of the U.S. House of Representatives for over thirty years, eventually becoming Chairman of the House Naval Affairs Committee. His mother, Maud Darlington, was the daughter of Smedley Darlington, a wealthy businessman and former U.S. Congressman from Pennsylvania.

Butler received his primary and secondary education in Chester County and Montgomery County, attending The Haverford School in Haverford, Pennsylvania. He excelled academically and captained the school’s baseball team while competing on its football squad. Despite his family’s strict Quaker tradition of pacifism, Butler displayed an early ambition for military service. 

In April 1898, following the outbreak of the Spanish–American War, the sixteen-year-old Butler lied about his age to obtain a direct commission in the United States Marine Corps. Leveraging his family’s political standing, he secured a commission as a second lieutenant in May 1898, leaving Haverford prior to graduation, though the school subsequently awarded him his high school diploma.

## Career

### Military Service and Foreign Campaigns

Butler’s early military service took him to Cuba during the Spanish–American War and subsequently to the Philippines during the Philippine–American War. In 1900, during the Boxer Rebellion in China, First Lieutenant Butler participated in the Battle of Tientsin, where he rescued a wounded comrade under heavy fire and sustained a gunshot wound to his thigh. For his bravery, he was promoted to captain at age nineteen and received the Marine Corps Brevet Medal.

During the era of the "Banana Wars," Butler led Marine expeditions across Central America and the Caribbean:
* **Veracruz, Mexico (1914):** Major Butler was awarded his first Medal of Honor for his leadership during the United States occupation of Veracruz, where he conducted dangerous reconnaissance missions inside the city under hostile fire.
* **Haiti (1915):** During the U.S. occupation of Haiti, Butler led an assault on Fort Rivière, a stronghold of Caco rebels. For personally leading the breach through a narrow drain hole under direct gun fire, he received his second Medal of Honor.

During World War I, Butler was promoted to brigadier general and placed in command of Camp Pontanezen at Brest, France, the primary debarkation camp for the American Expeditionary Forces. He transformed a chaotic, mud-filled campsite into a highly efficient logistical center handling hundreds of thousands of troops. For his administrative success, he was awarded both the Army Distinguished Service Medal and the Navy Distinguished Service Medal.

### Director of Public Safety in Philadelphia (1924–1925)

In late 1923, Philadelphia Mayor-elect W. Freeland Kendrick sought to fulfill a campaign promise to eliminate police corruption and curb rampant violations of the Eighteenth Amendment. Kendrick appealed to President Calvin Coolidge to grant Brigadier General Butler a leave of absence from the Marine Corps to serve as Philadelphia’s Director of Public Safety. Butler assumed office in January 1924, taking control of both the Philadelphia Police Department and the Philadelphia Fire Department.

Butler approached municipal administration with military intensity. Within his first 48 hours, he organized citywide raids on more than 900 illegal speakeasies, gambling parlors, and bootlegging dens across Center City, North Philadelphia, and South Philadelphia. He introduced sweeping structural reforms to the police force:
1. **Reorganization:** Shifted police operations into mobile armored units and established checkpoint posts along major transit corridors.
2. **Professionalization:** Replaced old uniforms with distinctive dress uniforms, mandated rigorous physical conditioning, and dismissed dozens of officers implicated in bribery.
3. **Equitable Enforcement:** Insisted that high-end social clubs and hotel bars frequented by political elites be raided with the same zeal as neighborhood saloons.

Butler’s refusal to spare politically connected establishments alienated ward leaders and city council members affiliated with Philadelphia’s dominant Republican political machine. Mayor Kendrick faced intense pressure from party leadership to restrain his safety director. Butler publicly denounced political interference in law enforcement, declaring that domestic bootlegging and municipal graft posed a severe threat to public order. In December 1925, at the end of his two-year leave, Mayor Kendrick declined to extend Butler’s tenure, forcing his resignation and return to the military. Reflecting on his time in Philadelphia, Butler noted that cleaning up corruption in the city was harder than any military campaign he had ever waged.

### Retirement, Anti-War Advocacy, and the Business Plot

Butler was promoted to Major General in 1929, becoming the youngest major general in Marine Corps history. After being passed over for the position of Commandant of the Marine Corps in 1930, he retired from active duty in 1931.

In retirement, Butler embarked on nationwide lecture tours. In 1935, he published his famous work *War Is a Racket*. Later that same year, in an article published in the socialist journal *Common Sense*, Butler wrote of his military career:
> *"I spent 33 years and four months in active military service... And during that period I spent most of my time being a high class muscle man for Big Business, for Wall Street and for the bankers. In short, I was a racketeer, a gangster for capitalism."*

In November 1934, Butler revealed details of a political conspiracy to the McCormack–Dickstein Committee of the U.S. House of Representatives. Butler testified that representatives of wealthy financial interests had approached him to command a private army of 500,000 military veterans in a coup d'état to overthrow President Franklin D. Roosevelt and establish a fascist military dictatorship. While media outlets initially expressed skepticism, the congressional committee's final report confirmed that key elements of Butler’s account were credible.

## Personal life

On June 30, 1905, Butler married Ethel Peters Parker of Philadelphia, the daughter of a prominent local family. The couple had three children: Ethel, Smedley Darlington Jr., and Thomas.

Throughout his career, Butler maintained a residence in the Philadelphia area, eventually settling in Newtown Square in Delaware County, Pennsylvania. He was known for his direct speaking style, colorful vocabulary, and deep empathy for enlisted men, who affectionately referred to him as "Old Gimlet Eye" due to his piercing gaze and intense demeanor. Despite his high military rank and public profile, Butler retained his personal Quaker values, frequently writing about the horrors of war and the duty of public leaders to protect human life.

In the late 1930s, Butler's health began to decline. In June 1940, he fell ill and was admitted to the Naval Hospital at the Philadelphia Navy Yard in South Philadelphia. He died of cancer on June 21, 1940, at the age of 58. He was buried near his birthplace at Oaklands Cemetery in West Chester, Pennsylvania.

## Legacy

Smedley Butler remains one of the most complex figures in American military history and Philadelphia municipal lore. He is remembered simultaneously as a highly decorated hero of American overseas expansion and as a formidable critic of economic imperialism and foreign warfare.

In Philadelphia, Butler’s tenure as Director of Public Safety is remembered as an intense, militarized effort at municipal police reform. His uncompromising campaign against Prohibition violations exposed the extent of political machine influence in the city's police department, establishing a historical precedent for civil rights discussions and police reform efforts in 20th-century Philadelphia.

Within the United States Marine Corps, Butler is honored as a tactical pioneer and leader. Military installations have been named in his honor, including Camp Smedley D. Butler in Okinawa, Japan. His writings, particularly *War Is a Racket*, continue to be studied by historians, political scientists, and anti-war advocates examining the relationship between corporate interests, military power, and domestic governance.

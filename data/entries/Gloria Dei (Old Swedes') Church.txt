### 2. Lead section

**Gloria Dei (Old Swedes') Church** is a historic sanctuary situated in South Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Constructed between 1698 and 1700, it holds the distinction of being the oldest church building in Pennsylvania. Built by Swedish immigrants who settled in the Delaware Valley decades prior to William Penn's arrival, the church stands as the primary surviving material link to the colony of New Sweden.

Originally consecrated as a Swedish Lutheran sanctuary, Gloria Dei served as the religious and community center for Swedish settlers for nearly 150 years. As the local Swedish population assimilated into the broader English-speaking culture of Philadelphia, the congregation voted to affiliate with the Protestant Episcopal Church in 1845. Throughout its centuries of continuous operation, the sanctuary has maintained an active ministry while preserving its original early American architectural character and interior artifacts.

In 1942, Gloria Dei Church was designated a National Historic Site, establishing a federal preservation partnership six years before the creation of Independence National Historical Park and the federal recognition of Independence Hall. Under a unique cooperative agreement, the private Gloria Dei congregation retains full ownership and maintenance responsibilities for the historic sanctuary, its active ministry, and immediate parish buildings, while the National Park Service owns and maintains surrounding parkland to buffer the historic precinct from urban encroachment.

Set within pastoral, tree-lined grounds near the Delaware River waterfront, the site encompasses the brick church building, a historic bell tower added in 1703, and an expansive colonial burial ground. The cemetery serves as the final resting place for Swedish pioneers, Revolutionary War patriots, prominent early citizens, and ordinary Philadelphians, offering an enduring record of the region's cultural and civic evolution.

### 3. History

#### New Sweden and Colonial Beginnings
The origins of Gloria Dei are tied to the colony of New Sweden, established in 1638 when the Swedish vessels *Kalmar Nyckel* and *Fogel Gryp* landed in the Delaware Valley. Swedish settlers established agrarian homesteads and trading posts along the Delaware River long before King Charles II granted William Penn his charter for Pennsylvania in 1681. Early Swedish Lutheran services were conducted at various fortified structures and timber chapels, including a log blockhouse constructed at Wicaco—a riverfront settlement located in present-day South Philadelphia.

By the late 17th century, the growing congregation at Wicaco required a permanent structure. Construction on the present brick building began in 1698 under the leadership of Reverend Andrew Rudman. Built using local materials, the sanctuary was formally dedicated on July 2, 1700, and given the name *Gloria Dei* ("Glory to God"). In 1703, a brick entry tower and porch (topped by a timber belfry and spire) along with side porches were added to buttress the church's walls and house a church bell, completing the primary structural silhouette that survives today.

#### The American Revolution and Nils Collin
During the 18th century, Gloria Dei remained an influential institution within the expanding urban environment of colonial Philadelphia. One of its most notable pastors was the Reverend Nils Collin, who arrived from Sweden in the 1770s and served as rector for over forty years. Ministering during the turbulent era of the American Revolution, Collin navigated the complexities of wartime Philadelphia, maintaining the church as a spiritual sanctuary for both the Swedish-descended population and the broader Revolutionary community.

Collin was a prominent intellectual in early national Philadelphia, maintaining friendships with figures such as Benjamin Franklin and participating in the city's vibrant scientific circles. Following the American Revolution, the formal financial and clerical support previously provided by the Crown of Sweden gradually waned, setting the stage for the congregation's eventual institutional transition.

#### 19th-Century Transition and Modern Preservation
As the Swedish language faded from common usage in the Delaware Valley during the early 19th century, the congregation conducted services primarily in English. In 1845, after nearly a century and a half as a Swedish Lutheran parish, Gloria Dei formally joined the Episcopal Diocese of Pennsylvania. The transition allowed the church to maintain its historic liturgical style while integrating into the established ecclesiastical framework of the region.

Recognizing the exceptional historical and architectural value of the property, the United States federal government designated Gloria Dei Church a National Historic Site on November 17, 1942. This designation predated the federal historical recognition of Independence Hall (1948). To protect the churchyard's pastoral environment against surrounding industrial and residential development in South Philadelphia, the National Park Service subsequently acquired adjacent parcels of land, creating a protective green buffer around the churchyard and cemetery.

### 4. Architecture

Gloria Dei Church represents an extraordinary example of early American colonial ecclesiastical architecture, incorporating traditional Swedish construction influences with English colonial bricklaying techniques.

#### Exterior Structure and Masonry
The exterior walls of Gloria Dei are constructed in Flemish bond masonry, utilizing deep red clay bricks interspersed with dark, vitrified black header bricks. This bricklaying pattern produces a checkered geometric design characteristic of high-status late 17th-century structures in the mid-Atlantic region. The church features a rectangular nave topped by a steeply pitched gabled roof covered in wood shingles.

The west elevation is dominated by the historic brick tower and entry porch, topped by a timber belfry and spire, added in 1703. The square base of the tower extends into an octagonal spire topped by a weather vane. The side elevations are pierced by arched multi-pane windows that allow natural light into the main assembly hall.

#### Interior Layout and Sacred Relics
The interior of Gloria Dei reflects the modest aesthetic of colonial Protestant houses of worship. The simple layout centers on an elevated central altar at the eastern end. Suspended from the vaulted ceiling of the nave are two hand-carved scale models of the *Kalmar Nyckel* and *Fogel Gryp*, commemorating the two sailing vessels that carried the original Swedish settlers to North America in 1638.

The sanctuary's large arch-topped windows feature clear leaded glass panes that fill the interior with natural light. Among the interior's notable historical artifacts are two carved wooden cherubim brought from the 1640s Swedish chapel at Tinicum Island, as well as a historic marble baptismal font brought from Sweden.

### 5. Cultural significance

Gloria Dei Church serves as a primary physical testament to the pre-English European settlement of the Delaware Valley. While early Pennsylvania history is often defined by William Penn's Quaker colony established in 1682, Gloria Dei provides visible evidence of the Swedish colonial footprint that laid the agricultural, legal, and social foundations of the region decades earlier.

The surrounding burial ground forms an integral component of the site's cultural landscape. Dating back to the late 17th century, the cemetery contains grave markers spanning more than three centuries. Interred within the churchyard are original Swedish settlers, Revolutionary War officers and soldiers, mariners, civic leaders, and generations of neighborhood residents. The burial ground functions both as a historical archive and as an urban park setting within South Philadelphia.

The administrative arrangement governing Gloria Dei is also significant within American historic preservation. As a dual-managed site, the self-supporting Gloria Dei congregation maintains control over its active religious services, historical structures, and internal affairs, while collaborating with the National Park Service to maintain the open parkland surrounding the sanctuary. This model balances the preservation of an active place of worship with the public access goals of a national landmark.

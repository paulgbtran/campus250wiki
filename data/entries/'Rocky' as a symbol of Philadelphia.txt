### Lead section

***Rocky*** is a monument and iconic cultural symbol located in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, centered around the fictional boxer Rocky Balboa created and portrayed by actor Sylvester Stallone. Originally conceptualized for the *Rocky* film franchise, the character’s triumphant morning training run through the streets of Philadelphia—culminating in an ascent of the 72 stone steps of the Philadelphia Museum of Art—established an enduring visual and narrative connection between the fictional fighter and the city's identity. In 1980, Stallone commissioned sculptor A. Thomas Schomberg to cast an eight-foot-six-inch bronze statue of Balboa with his arms raised in victory, which was subsequently gifted to the City of Philadelphia in 1982.

As a symbol, *Rocky* embodies the spirit of the underdog, representing the values of hard work, grit, determination, and hustle associated with Philadelphia’s working-class culture. Stallone viewed the character's ascent to the top of the museum steps as emblematic of the city itself—a location where ordinary individuals can overcome formidable obstacles to achieve champion status. Over the decades, the statue and the steps have evolved into one of the city's most visited public landmarks, attracting millions of tourists and sports fans who participate in the ritual of running the steps and posing beside the monument.

The installation of the sculpture sparked intense debates within Philadelphia’s artistic, municipal, and cultural communities. While local citizens and film enthusiasts embraced the bronze statue as a celebration of popular culture and civic pride, museum officials and art critics questioned its artistic dignity, arguing that a commercial movie prop should not stand permanently near a world-class fine art institution. Consequently, the sculpture was relocated several times, spending over two decades at the Spectrum sports complex in South Philadelphia before returning to the grounds of the Philadelphia Museum of Art in 2006.

Since 2006, the sculpture has stood permanently in a landscaped area at the base of the museum steps, solidifying its dual status as a popular cultural icon and a recognized monument in American public art history.

---

### Origin and creation

The origin of *Rocky* as a Philadelphia cultural symbol stems from the release of the 1976 feature film *Rocky*, written by and starring Sylvester Stallone, a former Philadelphian. Set against the backdrop of South Philadelphia's working-class neighborhoods, the film told the story of Rocky Balboa, an unheralded club fighter given an improbable shot at the world heavyweight championship. The cinematic sequence showing Balboa running through local markets, industrial waterfronts, and residential streets before bounding up the granite steps of the Philadelphia Museum of Art became an immediate touchstone of 1970s American cinema, transforming the museum steps into a stage for personal triumph.

In 1980, during pre-production for *Rocky III* (1982), Stallone sought to translate this cinematic symbol into a tangible, permanent monument. He commissioned Colorado-based sculptor A. Thomas Schomberg (born 1943) to create a larger-than-life bronze representation of Balboa. Schomberg was already an accomplished figurative sculptor specializing in sports subjects, whose athletic monuments had been commissioned for major American sports arenas including the Houston Astrodome, the New Orleans Superdome, and Yankee Stadium in New York City.

Schomberg sculpted the statue to capture Balboa at his peak moment of athletic victory, depicting the boxer clad in trunks and boxing shoes, arms held triumphantly aloft with clenched gloves. Cast in solid bronze, the figure measures 8 feet 6 inches in height, 4 feet in width, and 2 feet in depth, resting atop a brick and concrete base measuring 1 foot 6 inches high by 3 feet 6 inches square. In the storyline of *Rocky III*, the statue is unveiled in the courtyard of the Philadelphia Museum of Art during a fictional civic ceremony—a plot point that soon spilled over into real-world municipal policy and urban art debate.

### History and relocations

The physical life of the *Rocky* statue was marked by decades of relocation, public controversy, and debate over the definition of public art. Following the completion of filming for *Rocky III* in 1982, Sylvester Stallone formally presented the bronze sculpture as a gift to the City of Philadelphia. However, the proposal to keep the statue permanently installed on the museum’s plaza touched off a fierce debate among city leaders, art historians, museum trustees, and the general public.

```
Timeline of Statue Relocations:
[1980] Sculpted by A. Thomas Schomberg
  │
[1982] Gifted by Stallone; Installed at The Spectrum (South Philadelphia)
  │
[1990] Relocated temporarily to Art Museum Steps for 'Rocky V' filming
  │
[1990] Returned to The Spectrum
  │
[2006] Moved permanently to base of Art Museum steps (Kelly Dr. & Ben Franklin Pkwy)
```

Opponents of the museum location, including museum directors and art purists, argued that the sculpture was fundamentally a commercial film prop rather than a work of fine art, contending that its permanent presence at the museum’s entrance compromised the institution’s aesthetic integrity. Conversely, working-class Philadelphians, movie enthusiasts, and tourism advocates passionately defended the work, viewing it as an accessible monument celebrating endurance, hope, and the city’s underdog identity. After prolonged civic discourse, the city accepted the gift but chose to site it away from the museum, moving it to the Spectrum sports arena complex in South Philadelphia.

For eight years, the statue stood outside the Spectrum, becoming a favorite pre-game gathering point for Philadelphia sports fans attending hockey and basketball games. In 1990, film producers reached an agreement with the city to temporarily relocate the sculpture back to the top of the Art Museum steps for the filming of *Rocky V*. The move reignited the debate over its permanent home; once filming concluded, public outcry failed to overturn the city's previous decision, and the sculpture was returned to the Spectrum.

The monument remained in South Philadelphia until 2006, when municipal leaders and arts organizations reassessed its cultural and economic value to the city. Recognizing its status as one of Philadelphia's primary tourist attractions, the city moved the statue back to the Art Museum grounds, permanently installing it on a concrete base in a landscaped area at the bottom of the steps, near the intersection of Kelly Drive and the Benjamin Franklin Parkway.

### Cultural impact and public engagement

While *Rocky* is a fictional character and a bronze monument, the symbol has maintained a profound "personal life" within the daily rhythms and popular culture of Philadelphians and global visitors. The site of the sculpture and the adjacent steps function as an interactive civic space. Visitors routinely emulate Balboa’s training regimen by sprinting up the 72 stone steps, raising their arms above their heads at the summit, and posing for photographs next to Schomberg’s bronze casting.

The symbol deeply resonates with the blue-collar, neighborhood-centric character of Philadelphia, particularly in South Philadelphia communities where the fictional Balboa originated. Local figures have long championed the statue's civic significance. Prominent Philadelphia lawyer and former Pennsylvania Boxing Commissioner James "Jimmy" Binns emerged as a key advocate for the statue’s preservation and prominence, emphasizing its positive impact on youth, sports culture, and tourism.

The Association for Public Art (aPA)—formerly the Fairmount Park Art Association—has extensively documented the sculpture’s impact on urban art history. Under the leadership of long-time Executive Director Penny Balkin Bach (who served from 1980 to 2022 and authored *Public Art in Philadelphia*), the organization contextualized *Rocky* within the broader landscape of Philadelphia's public monuments. The Association incorporated the sculpture into its *Museum Without Walls: AUDIO* program, providing educational audio segments featuring perspectives from Schomberg, Binns, and cultural historians to explain the complex dialogue between public art, cinema, and urban identity.

---

### Legacy

The legacy of *Rocky* as a symbol of Philadelphia represents a pivotal case study in modern public art, urban branding, and popular culture. Schomberg’s sculpture bridged the gap between fine art traditions and pop-culture fandom, challenging mid-20th-century assumptions regarding what constitutes a legitimate public monument. While traditional statues often honor military commanders, political figures, or industrialists, *Rocky* stands as one of the world's most famous monuments dedicated to a fictional character and the working-class ethos he represents.

The symbol has extended far beyond cinema into the identity of Philadelphia sports teams—such as the Eagles, Phillies, 76ers, and Flyers—who frequently employ the *Rocky* theme song ("Gonna Fly Now") and imagery to mobilize fans and frame their competitive pursuits around the underdog narrative. 

Furthermore, the statue’s ultimate integration onto the grounds of the Philadelphia Museum of Art symbolizes a historic shift in institutional art curation. By placing the sculpture permanently at the base of the museum steps, the City of Philadelphia and its art institutions acknowledged that public significance and emotional resonance are key components of enduring monumental art.

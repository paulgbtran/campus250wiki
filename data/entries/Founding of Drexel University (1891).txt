### Lead Section

The founding of Drexel University occurred in December 1891 in West Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, when American financier and philanthropist Anthony J. Drexel established the Drexel Institute of Art, Science and Industry. Conceived during the height of the American Gilded Age and the late Industrial Revolution, the institution was designed to address the burgeoning technical and industrial workforce demands of Philadelphia—then recognized as the "Workshop of the World." Anthony J. Drexel invested more than $3 million of his personal fortune—equivalent to roughly $78 million in modern valuation—to build the campus and establish its founding endowment.

From its inception, the Drexel Institute broke sharply from the contemporary norms of late-nineteenth-century American higher education. At a time when university education was largely restricted to wealthy, upper-class men preparing for traditional professions in law, medicine, or the Christian ministry, Drexel instituted an open-access policy. The institution mandated no restrictions based on religion, race, gender, or social class. Operating as a nonsectarian, coeducational center for practical instruction, the institute aimed to equip young men and women with applied skills tailored to rapid technological change and commercial growth.

The original institute expanded dramatically over the following century, evolving from a non-degree-granting trade and technical school into a comprehensive doctoral research institution. Organizing its initial eighteen departments into specialized faculties, the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania granted the institute degree-conferring privileges for Bachelor of Science degrees in 1914, Master of Science degrees in 1927, and Doctor of Philosophy degrees in 1965. Reflecting its expanding scope and focus on industrial research, the school was renamed the Drexel Institute of Technology in 1936 and attained university status as Drexel University in 1970.

A central hallmark of the university's identity, the formalization of its Cooperative Education (Co-op) program in 1919 embedded mandatory professional workplace experience directly into the undergraduate academic curriculum. In the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, the university continued its history of academic innovation by becoming the first U.S. institution to require microcomputers for all undergraduate students in 1983 (distributing Macintoshes in early 1984), creating the nation's first fully wireless campus in 2000, establishing a cooperative law school in 2006, and affiliating with the Academy of Natural Sciences in 2011.

### Background

During the late nineteenth century, Philadelphia experienced unprecedented industrial expansion. The city became an international leader in heavy manufacturing, locomotives, textiles, metallurgy, and financial commerce, earning the moniker "Workshop of the World." This rapid transformation created a high demand for skilled technical professionals, engineers, draftsmen, applied scientists, and commercial managers. However, traditional American higher education systems were largely unequipped to meet these needs, maintaining rigid classical curricula focused on ancient languages, philosophy, and liberal arts aimed at elite male demographics.

Anthony J. Drexel (1826–1893), one of the prominent financiers of the nineteenth century, recognized this institutional disconnect. Entering his father’s currency exchange trading house as a teenager, Drexel gained firsthand appreciation for practical work experience, financial stewardship, and applied skills. Throughout his career, Drexel played a pivotal role in shaping the modern economic landscape of the United States. In partnership with his mentee John Pierpont Morgan, he co-founded the merchant bank Drexel, Morgan & Co., financed the construction of America’s transcontinental railway system, underwrote payroll for the Union Army during the American Civil War, and helped stabilize national economic panics.

Reflecting on his career, Anthony J. Drexel envisioned an educational institution that would combine rigorous academic coursework with applied training. He believed that upward economic mobility should be accessible to working-class men and women alike, provided they were granted rigorous, career-focused instruction. Rather than establishing a traditional university or leaving an unrestricted bequest, Drexel resolved to personally fund and construct an innovative technical institute in Philadelphia while he was still living, ensuring its charter reflected his egalitarian and pragmatic educational philosophy.

### Course of Events

In the late 1880s and early 1890s, Anthony J. Drexel finalized his plans for the new educational facility, committing a personal fortune exceeding $3 million to construct a flagship building and establish an operational endowment. Drexel selected a site in West Philadelphia at 3141 Chestnut Street, strategic for its proximity to major rail transport lines connecting the city's commercial core to expanding industrial zones. Construction began on a monumental, Italian Renaissance Revival building—now known as Main Building—designed by the architectural firm Wilson Brothers & Company.

In December 1891, the institution was officially chartered and dedicated as the Drexel Institute of Art, Science and Industry. The dedication ceremony brought together leading civic, financial, and educational figures of the era to celebrate a radical blueprint for industrial schooling. The founding charter enshrined three core organizational principles:
1. Complete nonsectarianism and coeducation.
2. Open admissions without barriers based on race, gender, or socioeconomic background.
3. A focus on practical arts, science, and technical education designed for direct employment in modern industries.

When classes officially commenced, the Drexel Institute functioned as a non-degree-granting institution organized into 18 distinct academic departments. The curriculum offered specialized instruction in mechanical engineering, electrical science, applied chemistry, architecture, domestic arts, business practices, and technical drawing. The campus centered around Main Building's grand court, housing lecture halls, specialized laboratories, workshop facilities, and a public library and art gallery, reflecting Anthony Drexel's belief that technical training should be enriched by culture and the arts.

### Aftermath

Following its opening, the Drexel Institute expanded rapidly to meet regional and national industrial demands. To accommodate growing enrollment and advancing academic standards, the institute systematically structured its programs into formal degree tracks:

* **School Reorganization and Degree Conferral (1914–1927):** In 1914, the 18 founding departments were consolidated into four primary schools. That same year, the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania authorized the institute to confer its first four-year Bachelor of Science (B.S.) degrees. In 1927, degree-granting privileges were expanded to include Master of Science (M.S.) graduate degrees.
* **Formalization of Cooperative Education (1919):** Inspired by Anthony Drexel’s emphasis on real-world training, the institute formalized its Cooperative Education (Co-op) program in 1919. This educational model alternated periods of academic study with full-time professional employment, giving students practical industry experience prior to graduation.
* **Name Changes and Doctoral Authorization (1936–1970):** In 1936, the school changed its name to the *Drexel Institute of Technology* to reflect its specialized emphasis on engineering, physical science, and industrial research. In 1965, the institution received state authorization to award Doctor of Philosophy (Ph.D.) degrees. Recognizing its growth into a comprehensive multi-college entity, the institution officially became *Drexel University* in 1970.

In the modern era, Drexel University has maintained its focus on academic technology and experiential education:

* **Microcomputer Requirement (1983–1984):** Drexel became the first higher education institution in the United States to mandate personal microcomputers for all undergraduate coursework in 1983, distributing Apple Macintosh 128K units to incoming students in early 1984.
* **Campus Networking (2000):** In 2000, Drexel became the first major university in the nation to feature a fully wireless Internet network across its entire campus footprint.
* **Law School and Institutional Expansion (2006–2011):** In 2006, the university opened the Drexel University College of Law (later renamed the Thomas R. Kline School of Law)—the first new law school established at a major research university in 25 years—incorporating a cooperative education format. In 2011, Drexel affiliated with the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia, combining two of the region's historic scientific institutions.

Today, Drexel University stands as a major urban doctoral research institution in West Philadelphia's University City neighborhood, retaining the founding values of innovation, inclusivity, and practical education established by Anthony J. Drexel in 1891.

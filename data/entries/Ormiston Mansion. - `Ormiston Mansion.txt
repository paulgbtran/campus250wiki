## Lead section

Ormiston Mansion is a historic late Georgian and early Federal-style country house located atop a scenic bluff overlooking the Schuylkill River in East Fairmount Park, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Constructed around 1798, the mansion was established by Edward Burd, a prominent Philadelphia lawyer, Continental Army veteran, and longtime Prothonotary of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania. Burd named the estate "Ormiston" after his family's ancestral seat in East Lothian, Scotland, following a tradition among 18th-century Philadelphia gentility of creating suburban villas named after European homelands or rural retreats.

The property represents a well-preserved example of the country seats constructed by Philadelphia's political and economic elite during the late 18th century. Situated along the Schuylkill River corridor, Ormiston was designed to afford panoramic views of the river valley while providing a summer refuge from the densely populated urban core of Center City Philadelphia. Architectural highlights of the two-and-a-half-story red-brick mansion include a hipped roof, heavy molded cornices, symmetrical dormer windows, and an expansive wooden porch facing the river that highlights the building's scenic site.

In the mid-19th century, Ormiston was incorporated into the expanding Fairmount Park system. Following the Consolidation Act of 1854 and subsequent legislative acts by the Pennsylvania General Assembly in 1867 and 1868, the newly formed Fairmount Park Commission expropriated private river estates to protect the city’s drinking water supply drawn from the Schuylkill River and to create public recreational parklands. Unlike many contemporary urban estates that were demolished during municipal development, Ormiston was retained by park authorities due to its scenic contours and historic architectural integrity.

Today, Ormiston Mansion stands alongside neighboring historic river properties—such as Mount Pleasant, Woodford, Lemon Hill, and Strawberry Mansion—as a preserved historic landmark within East Fairmount Park. Managed in partnership between Philadelphia Parks & Recreation and volunteer preservation groups, including the Royal Heritage Society of Delaware Valley, the estate serves as an educational and cultural venue highlighting colonial and early American life, architectural history, and the evolution of urban municipal parklands in Philadelphia.

## Early life and education

The origin of Ormiston Mansion is closely bound to the early life, lineage, and education of its founder, Edward Burd (1749–1833). Born in Lancaster, Pennsylvania, Burd was the son of Colonel James Burd, a high-ranking officer in the provincial forces of Pennsylvania during the French and Indian War, and Sarah Shippen Burd, a member of the influential Shippen family of Philadelphia. Raised in an environment defined by civic governance and colonial administration, young Edward received a rigorous classical education designed to prepare him for leadership in legal and public affairs.

Burd pursued higher education at the College of Philadelphia (now the University of Pennsylvania), graduating in 1769. Following his academic coursework, he read law under the tutelage of his uncle, Edward Shippen IV, who later served as Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania. Admitted to the bar in 1772, Burd quickly established a reputation for legal precision and scholarly acumen within the judicial circles of colonial Philadelphia and Lancaster County.

The outbreak of the American Revolutionary War significantly interrupted Burd’s early professional establishment. Committing himself to the Patriot cause, he volunteered for military service and was appointed a captain in Colonel William Montgomery’s battalion of Pennsylvania troops within the Flying Camp. In August 1776, during the Battle of Long Island, Captain Burd was captured by British forces. He remained a prisoner of war until his parole and exchange, after which he returned to Pennsylvania to resume his legal career and public service during the formative years of the new American republic.

Burd’s deep appreciation for his Scottish heritage directly influenced his personal aesthetic and vision for his family estate. Drawing upon ancestral ties to the village of Ormiston in Scotland, he envisioned a pastoral seat that mirrored the manor houses of Great Britain while embodying the architectural restraint favored in post-Revolutionary Pennsylvania. This combination of classical education, Revolutionary service, and ancestral identity shaped the establishment of Ormiston Mansion in the late 1790s.

## Career

The operational career of Ormiston Mansion unfolded alongside Edward Burd’s distinguished legal tenure and the subsequent growth of the City of Philadelphia. In 1778, Burd was appointed Prothonotary of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, a pivotal judicial administrative post he held with distinction for twenty-seven years until 1805. During his tenure, Burd maintained meticulously detailed judicial records and managed administrative proceedings during a transformative period in American jurisprudence, when Philadelphia functioned as both the capital of Pennsylvania and the nation's capital.

During the yellow fever epidemics that devastated Philadelphia in the 1790s, wealthy residents sought refuge in the elevated, rural surroundings west of the city limits. Burd selected a high parcel of land along the east bank of the Schuylkill River to serve as his primary summer residency. Constructed around 1798, Ormiston Mansion functioned as an active agricultural estate and elite country villa, where Burd managed rural land holdings while continuing his duties at the Supreme Court. The estate was outfitted with formal gardens, outbuildings, and orchard plantings designed to operate self-sufficiently during seasonal retreats.

Following Burd’s death in 1833, the property remained in private hands for several decades, maintaining its dual character as a residential homestead and agricultural parkland. However, by the mid-19th century, the rapid industrialization of Philadelphia threatened the Schuylkill River watershed. In response, Philadelphia City Councils initiated a massive public acquisition campaign under the Consolidation Act of 1854 and the Fairmount Park Acts of 1867 and 1868. Ormiston was expropriated by the Fairmount Park Commission (FPC) as part of an effort to preserve green buffer zones along the Schuylkill to guard the municipal water supply managed by the Fairmount Water Works.

Under the administration of the Fairmount Park Commission, Ormiston transitioned into a public asset. While some river estates were converted into administrative facilities or demolished to make way for roadways, Ormiston was preserved in place due to the commission's policy of maintaining the scenic contours of historic river properties. Over the 20th and 21st centuries, the building has hosted civic organizations, educational programming, and heritage preservation initiatives under the jurisdiction of Philadelphia Parks & Recreation.

## Personal life

Edward Burd’s personal life was tied to the political and social networks of Revolutionary-era Philadelphia. In 1778, he married his first cousin, Elizabeth Shippen (1754–1828), daughter of Chief Justice Edward Shippen IV. This marriage reinforced his connections to one of Pennsylvania’s most prominent families, making him a brother-in-law (as well as first cousin) to Margaret "Peggy" Shippen, who married Benedict Arnold. Despite the political scandals that affected branches of the Shippen family during the Revolution, Burd and his immediate household retained their respected status through steadfast legal service and public loyalty.

At Ormiston Mansion, Edward and Elizabeth Burd established a domestic life centered on family, legal scholarship, and hospitality. The couple had several children, including Elizabeth, Sarah, and Edward Shippen Burd, the latter of whom became a notable legal figure and philanthropist in Philadelphia. The house was designed to accommodate both intimate family gatherings and formal entertainments for leaders of Philadelphia society, legal professionals, and visiting dignitaries who traveled along the Schuylkill riverfront.

The domestic economy of Ormiston relied on a staff of household servant laborers, farmhands, and groundskeepers who maintained the estate’s extensive gardens, stables, and agricultural fields. Letters and journals from the period describe Ormiston as an idyllic rural refuge, praised for its cool river breezes, clean spring water, and elevated views of the Schuylkill River valley—a striking contrast to the crowded and unhygienic conditions of urban Philadelphia in the late 18th century.

Elizabeth Shippen Burd passed away at the estate in 1828, followed by Edward Burd in 1833. Both were interred in Philadelphia, leaving behind a substantial legal and physical legacy anchored by their preserved Schuylkill residence.

## Legacy

Ormiston Mansion remains an enduring physical testament to late 18th-century architectural design and early municipal conservation in Philadelphia. As one of the surviving historic country houses of Fairmount Park, Ormiston contributes to a rare collection of preserved 18th-century residential structures in an urban parkland setting, exemplifying the transitional style between Georgian symmetry and early Federal architectural ornament.

The preservation of Ormiston was largely made possible by the early vision of the Fairmount Park Commission, which sought to maintain the natural topography and historic estates along the Schuylkill River. Today, the property is maintained through a public-private partnership between Philadelphia Parks & Recreation (PPR) and non-profit stewardship bodies. The Royal Heritage Society of Delaware Valley has played a primary role in restoring the interior spaces, furnishing the house with period-appropriate decorative arts, and maintaining authentic Scottish-style garden plantings.

Beyond its architectural significance, Ormiston represents an important chapter in Philadelphia's environmental history. The acquisition of Ormiston and its neighboring estates in the late 1860s laid the foundation for the modern Fairmount Park system—one of the largest urban park networks in the United States. The estate continues to host seasonal public tours, educational events, and heritage celebrations, preserving the historical legacy of Edward Burd and the early national heritage of Philadelphia.

### Lead section

Richard Allen (February 14, 1760 – March 26, 1831) was an American minister, educator, writer, and one of the most influential African American leaders of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Born into slavery in Philadelphia, Allen purchased his freedom in 1780 and emerged as a prominent religious leader and civil rights organizer in the early Republic. He is best known as the founder of the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church, the first independent Black denomination in the United States, and served as its first consecrated bishop.

In 1787, Allen co-founded the Free African Society alongside Absalom Jones. This mutual aid organization provided economic, social, and spiritual support to free African Americans residing in Philadelphia. Facing growing racial discrimination and forced segregation within the white-dominated St. George’s Methodist Episcopal Church, Allen led Black congregants in a historic walkout around 1792, establishing a dedicated space of worship that laid the groundwork for independent Black religious institutions across the nation.

During the devastating Yellow Fever Epidemic of 1793 in Philadelphia, Allen and Jones organized Black volunteers to nurse the sick and bury the dead when many city officials and residents fled. In response to public mischaracterizations of Black nurses, Allen and Jones co-authored *A Narrative of the Proceedings of the Black People, During the Late Awful Calamity in Philadelphia* (1794), the first copyrighted publication by African American authors in the United States.

Throughout his life, Allen remained an ardent abolitionist, community organizer, and champion of Black self-determination. He operated a key station on the Underground Railroad out of his home and church, published anti-slavery pamphlets, and presided over the first National Negro Convention in 1830. Today, Allen is remembered as a foundational figure in African American history whose legacy of religious independence, civil rights activism, and institutional development reshaped the social landscape of Philadelphia and the broader United States.

### Early life and education

Richard Allen was born into slavery on February 14, 1760, on the Philadelphia estate of Benjamin Chew, a prominent lawyer and Chief Justice of Colonial Pennsylvania. While Allen was still a young child, Chew sold his mother and her four children (including Richard and three siblings) to Stokley Sturgis, a farmer living near Dover, Delaware. 

As a youth in Delaware, Allen exhibited a deep religious inclination and taught himself to read and write. Around the age of seventeen, he experienced a profound spiritual conversion after hearing itinerant Methodist preachers who traveled through the region. Methodism, which was in its formative stages in North America during the late eighteenth century, proved attractive to many enslaved and free Black Americans due to its emotional preaching style, emphasis on personal salvation, and early opposition to the institution of slavery.

Allen joined a local Methodist society and began holding religious meetings on the Sturgis plantation. His devotion and moral conduct impressed Sturgis, who eventually permitted Methodist itinerant preachers—including the celebrated evangelist Freeborn Garrettson—to preach at his home. Moved by Garrettson’s sermons on the sinfulness of slaveholding, Sturgis experienced a crisis of conscience and agreed to allow Allen and his brother to purchase their freedom. 

To earn the required sum of $2,000 in Continental currency (equivalent to approximately £60 in gold or silver specie), Allen engaged in arduous extra labor. During the American Revolutionary War, he worked as a brick maker, woodcutter, and wagon driver, hauling supplies for the Continental Forces. Through diligence and thrift, Allen fully paid for his manumission and attained his freedom by 1780, adopting the surname "Allen" to signify his independent status.

### Career

Following his manumission, Allen traveled extensively throughout Delaware, Maryland, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania as an itinerant lay preacher. His powerful oratorical skills attracted both Black and white listeners. In 1786, Allen returned to Philadelphia and was invited to preach early morning services to Black congregants at St. George’s Methodist Episcopal Church. Under his leadership, the Black membership at St. George's grew substantially, causing tension among the white church leadership, who sought to maintain social hierarchy and control.

Tensions culminated around 1792 when white trustees implemented strict racial segregation within the sanctuary, requiring Black parishioners to sit in the newly constructed balcony gallery. During a Sunday morning service, while praying, Absalom Jones and other Black members were physically pulled off their knees by white church officials and told to move to the back of the gallery. Led by Allen and Jones, the Black congregants walked out of St. George’s en masse, refusing to accept second-class status in their house of worship.

In 1787, prior to the walkout, Allen and Jones had co-founded the Free African Society (FAS), a non-denominational mutual aid organization. The FAS provided financial assistance to widows, orphans, and the sick, offered burial support, and established a framework for moral and social self-governance among Philadelphia’s growing free Black population. 

When the Yellow Fever Epidemic struck Philadelphia in the summer of 1793, municipal services collapsed, and thousands of residents fled. Physician Benjamin Rush appealed to Allen and Jones for assistance, believing incorrectly that Black citizens were immune to the disease. Allen, Jones, and members of the Free African Society stepped forward to serve as nurses, gravediggers, and relief workers. Following the crisis, when publisher Mathew Carey published a pamphlet accusing Black volunteers of extortion, Allen and Jones published *A Narrative of the Proceedings of the Black People, During the Late Awful Calamity in Philadelphia* (1794), offering a factual defense of the Black community's sacrifices.

In 1794, Allen founded the Bethel African Church on a lot he had purchased at the corner of Sixth and Lombard Streets in Philadelphia—the site of a former blacksmith shop. The church was dedicated by Methodist Bishop Francis Asbury. However, white Methodist leaders continued to assert property rights and administrative control over Bethel. Allen fought a series of legal battles to secure the congregation's independence, culminating in a historic 1816 ruling by the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania that granted the church full legal autonomy.

In April 1816, Allen convened a convention of Black Methodist congregations from Philadelphia, Baltimore, New Jersey, and surrounding regions. The delegates resolved to unite into a single national body: the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church. Allen was consecrated as the denomination’s first bishop on April 11, 1816. Under his leadership, the AME Church expanded rapidly, serving as a sanctuary, educational hub, and political center for Black Americans.

Beyond his pastoral duties, Allen was a pioneer of the early abolitionist movement. He operated a safe house for freedom seekers on the Underground Railroad at Mother Bethel and funded anti-slavery efforts. In September 1830, Allen hosted and presided over the first National Negro Convention at Bethel Church, bringing together delegates from across the free states to address the abolition of slavery, oppose African colonization schemes, and advocate for civil rights and educational opportunities for Black Americans.

### Personal life

Richard Allen married his first wife, Flora, on October 19, 1790. Flora shared Allen’s commitment to religious service and community assistance, assisting in early church operations and offering shelter to traveling preachers. She suffered from failing health for several years and passed away on March 11, 1801.

In 1802, Allen married Sarah Bass, a formerly enslaved woman from Virginia who had purchased her freedom and moved to Philadelphia. Sarah Allen was an indispensable partner in Allen’s ministry and civil rights activism. She organized church relief societies, provided food and clothing to freedom seekers traveling on the Underground Railroad, and helped establish auxiliary organizations within the AME Church.

Together, Richard and Sarah Allen had six children: Richard Jr., James, John, Peter, Sara, and Ann. To support his large family and maintain financial independence from white church authorities, Allen operated several successful businesses over his lifetime, including a chimney-sweeping service, a shoemaking shop, and real estate investments in Philadelphia. He died at his home on March 26, 1831, at the age of 71, and was buried at Mother Bethel A.M.E. Church.

---

### Legacy

Richard Allen’s contributions left an indelible mark on Philadelphia and American history. By founding the African Methodist Episcopal Church, he established a self-governing Black institution that grew into an international denomination with millions of members. The site of Mother Bethel A.M.E. Church on Sixth and Lombard Streets in Philadelphia holds the distinction of being the oldest parcel of land continuously owned by African Americans in the United States.

Allen’s leadership during the 1793 Yellow Fever Epidemic and his co-authoring of the 1794 pamphlet established a model for Black civic leadership and defense of African American citizenship rights. His organization of the 1830 National Negro Convention laid the structural foundation for the nineteenth-century Black Convention Movement, which championed abolition, education, and equal voting rights leading up to the Civil War.

Allen is commemorated across numerous public spaces, academic institutions, and historical markers. In Philadelphia, the Richard Allen Homes housing complex, historical markers at Mother Bethel Church, and public parks bear his name. In 2016, on the occasion of the 200th anniversary of the founding of the AME Church, the United States Postal Service issued a commemorative Black Heritage forever stamp in his honor. Allen remains a symbol of religious liberty, community empowerment, and uncompromising commitment to human equality.

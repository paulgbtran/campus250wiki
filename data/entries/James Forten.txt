## Lead section

James Forten (September 2, 1766 – March 4, 1842) was an African American sailmaker, Revolutionary War veteran, entrepreneur, philanthropist, and prominent abolitionist leader in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Born free in colonial Philadelphia, Forten came of age during the political turbulence of the American Revolution, attending public readings of the Declaration of Independence as a young child and subsequently serving aboard an American privateer ship as a teenager during the Revolutionary War. 

Following the war, Forten rose from an apprentice sailmaker to become the sole proprietor of one of Philadelphia’s most prosperous sailmaking lofts. Employing an integrated workforce of Black and white craftsmen, he accumulated significant financial wealth through business acumen and technological innovations in sail production. Forten leveraged his financial fortune and social standing to become a primary benefactor and organizer of early nineteenth-century abolitionism, civil rights campaigns, and civic institutions for African Americans.

Forten was a central figure in resisting the American Colonization Society's efforts to resettle free Black Americans in Africa, arguing forcefully that African Americans were entitled to full citizenship, legal protections, and voting rights in the United States. He financed anti-slavery publications—most notably providing essential seed money and ongoing funding for William Lloyd Garrison’s landmark abolitionist newspaper, *The Liberator*—and collaborated closely with religious and community leaders such as Bishop Richard Allen of Mother Bethel AME Church.

Through his marriage to Charlotte Vandine Forten, James Forten established a formidable family dynasty dedicated to civic activism. His children and descendants played leading roles in the anti-slavery struggle, the underground railroad, women’s suffrage, and the American Civil War. Recognized as one of the quintessential "Black Founders" of the United States, Forten's life illustrates the vital contributions of African Americans to the founding of the nation and the persistent struggle to fulfill its founding ideals of liberty and equality.

## Early life and education

James Forten was born a free person of African descent in Philadelphia on September 2, 1766. His father, Thomas Forten, was a free Black sailmaker who worked in the loft of Robert Bridges, and his mother, Margaret, was also free. Growing up in the maritime center of colonial Philadelphia during the 1770s, Forten experienced firsthand the sights, sounds, and intellectual ferment of the revolutionary movement against British rule. In July 1776, as a nine-year-old boy, Forten was among the crowd gathered in the yard of the Pennsylvania State House (now Independence Hall) to hear the first public reading of the Declaration of Independence.

Forten received his early formal education at the African School operated by the Quaker abolitionist Anthony Benezet. Benezet’s school provided Black children with rigorous instruction in reading, writing, arithmetic, and moral philosophy at a time when formal education for African Americans was severely restricted. Following his father's early death in an accident, Forten took on various odd jobs to help support his mother and sister, eventually securing work as an apprentice in Robert Bridges' sail loft.

In 1781, at age fourteen, Forten enlisted in the Continental cause during the Revolutionary War, serving as a powder boy aboard the privateer ship *Royal Louis*, commanded by Captain Stephen Decatur Sr. Aboard the vessel, Forten engaged in naval combat against British ships in the Atlantic. During its second voyage, the *Royal Louis* was captured by the British warship HMS *Amphion*. Impressed by Forten's character and intelligence, the British captain offered to send him to England to be educated alongside his own son. Forten refused the offer, asserting his loyalty to the newly declared American republic by declaring that he would never betray his country. Consequently, Forten was imprisoned as a prisoner of war for seven months aboard the notorious British prison ship HMS *Jersey*, anchored in Wallabout Bay off Brooklyn, New York, where thousands perished under brutal conditions. Forten survived the ordeal and returned on foot to Philadelphia following a prisoner exchange in 1782.

## Career

Upon returning to Philadelphia, Forten resumed his apprenticeship at Robert Bridges’ sail loft on the Philadelphia waterfront. Forten demonstrated exceptional skill in sail design, drafting, and lofting—the complex art of laying out full-sized sail patterns on the floor of the loft. Recognizing Forten's extraordinary talent, administrative capability, and integrity, Bridges appointed him foreman of the loft in 1786. When Bridges retired in 1798, he arranged for Forten to purchase the sail loft business outright, loaning him the necessary capital, which Forten repaid rapidly.

Under Forten’s management, the sail loft at the corner of Wharves and Water streets became one of the most successful and respected maritime enterprises in the region. Forten invented a sail-handling device that greatly simplified the hoisting and manipulation of large, heavy sails, though he chose not to patent it. His loft supplied sails to vessel owners throughout the Delaware Valley and transatlantic shipping trade. Forten maintained a diverse, fully integrated workforce of Black and white sailmakers, fostering racial cooperation and paying equal wages based on skill and tenure.

Forten used his substantial business profits to fund civic causes, reform organizations, and civil rights campaigns. In 1817, when the American Colonization Society (ACS) proposed transporting free African Americans to West Africa, Forten co-organized a historic mass meeting of over 3,000 African Americans at Mother Bethel AME Church in Philadelphia. The assembly overwhelmingly rejected state-sponsored colonization, affirming that Black Americans were born in the United States and were determined to claim their rights on American soil. 

Forten’s patronage was instrumental to the broader abolitionist movement. In 1831, when white journalist William Lloyd Garrison sought to establish *The Liberator*, an uncompromising anti-slavery newspaper in Boston, Forten provided the crucial initial financial capital to launch the paper and continuously purchased subscriptions for community members. Forten also helped found the American Anti-Slavery Society in 1833 and served as an active leader in the Pennsylvania Abolition Society, constantly petitioning the state legislature to repeal discriminatory laws and maintain voting privileges for Black Pennsylvanians.

## Personal life

James Forten was married twice. His first wife, Martha Beatte, died in 1804 shortly after their marriage. In 1805, he married Charlotte Vandine (1785–1884), with whom he shared a deep commitment to family, faith, and social justice. The couple raised nine children in their spacious home near Fourth and Lombard Streets in Philadelphia's Southwark neighborhood. Their residence served as a central salon and meeting place for prominent Black and white abolitionists, reformers, musicians, and intellectuals.

The Forten family maintained strong connections to Philadelphia’s cultural and religious institutions. They were major patrons of Mother Bethel AME Church, founded by Richard Allen, as well as St. Thomas' African Episcopal Church. The family also championed the flourishing Black arts scene in antebellum Philadelphia, frequently hosting and supporting figures such as Black composer, bandleader, and musician Francis Johnson.

Education and handcrafts were deeply emphasized within the household. Children were trained in literature, politics, and classical arts; for example, in 1827, Forten’s youngest daughter, Mary Isabella Forten, completed an intricate needlework sampler at the age of eight, demonstrating the family's dedication to refined education. Forten’s daughters—Harriet, Margaretta, and Sarah—co-founded the Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society in 1833 alongside Quaker women such as Lucretia Mott, playing a pioneering role in intersectional abolitionist and women's rights advocacy. His son, Robert Bridges Forten, became an active anti-slavery orator and soldier, while his granddaughter, Charlotte Forten Grimké, became an acclaimed diarist, poet, and educator of freed slaves during the Civil War.

## Legacy

James Forten died in Philadelphia on March 4, 1842, at the age of 75. His funeral was attended by thousands of Philadelphians of all racial backgrounds, reflecting his widespread respect as a civic leader, successful merchant, and defender of human liberty.

Forten’s legacy lived on directly through his family's continued public service. During the American Civil War, his descendants helped recruit African American soldiers for the Union Army, including troops for the 127th Regiment of United States Colored Troops (USCT), whose silk regimental flag was painted by Philadelphia Black artist David Bustill Bowser. Family heirlooms, including the Forten family Bible and a dining table preserved across seven generations, survived through descendants into the twenty-first century.

In modern historiography, scholars such as Dr. Julie Winch, author of the definitive biography *A Gentleman of Color: The Life of James Forten*, have restored Forten to prominence as a vital figure in the founding generation of the United States. His life demonstrates how free African Americans actively shaped the economic, political, and social fabric of the early American republic.

In 2023, the Museum of the American Revolution in Philadelphia mounted a landmark special exhibition entitled *Black Founders: The Forten Family of Philadelphia*. The exhibition brought together over one hundred historical artifacts, family heirlooms, interactive sailmaking stations, and original theatrical productions—including *Meet Young James Forten* and *Meet Elder James Forten*—to illuminate Forten’s journey from a teenage Revolutionary sailor to a titan of the abolitionist movement. The exhibition was complemented by a permanent 360-degree digital virtual tour and comprehensive educational guides for schools nationwide.

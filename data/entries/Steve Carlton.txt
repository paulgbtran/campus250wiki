## Lead section

**Steven Norman Carlton** (born December 22, 1944), nicknamed **"Lefty,"** is an American former Major League Baseball (MLB) left-handed starting pitcher who played 24 seasons in the major leagues, most notably for the Philadelphia Phillies from 1972 to 1986. Widely regarded as one of the most dominant pitchers in baseball history, Carlton was the cornerstone of the Phillies' rotation during the franchise's golden era in the late 1970s and early 1980s, culminating in Philadelphia's first World Series championship in 1980.

Carlton was the first pitcher in Major League Baseball history to win four Cy Young Awards (1972, 1977, 1980, and 1982), a feat achieved entirely during his tenure with Philadelphia. Armed with an exceptional combination of physical durability, a blistering fastball, and a legendary sharp-breaking slider, he became the second pitcher in MLB history to record 4,000 career strikeouts, following Nolan Ryan. Over his career, Carlton amassed 329 victories and 4,136 strikeouts, ranking near the top of nearly every major statistical category for left-handed pitchers.

Before arriving in Philadelphia, Carlton began his professional career with the St. Louis Cardinals, where he established himself as a premier talent and won a World Series title in 1967. Following a high-profile contract dispute after the 1971 season, the Cardinals traded Carlton to the Philadelphia Phillies on February 25, 1972, in exchange for pitcher Rick Wise. In his inaugural 1972 season in Philadelphia, Carlton produced one of the greatest individual pitching campaigns in baseball history, winning the National League Pitching Triple Crown by recording 27 wins, 310 strikeouts, and a 1.97 ERA for a last-place team that won only 59 total games.

Beyond his on-field achievements, Carlton was famed for his intense, solitary training regimen—incorporating Eastern philosophies, visualization techniques, and martial arts long before such practices were common in professional sports—as well as his decision to enact a total media blackout that lasted over a decade. Inducted into the National Baseball Hall of Fame on his first ballot in 1994 with 95.6% of the vote, Carlton remains an iconic figure in Philadelphia sports history. His uniform number 32 was retired by the Phillies in 1989.

## Early life and education

Steven Norman Carlton was born on December 22, 1944, in Miami, Florida. Raised in North Miami, he attended North Miami High School. Although he did not play high school baseball, he demonstrated an unusually strong left arm and natural physical stamina while pitching in American Legion Baseball. Although he showed immense promise on the diamond, he was not heavily scouted by major league teams during his high school years due to his thin frame.

Following high school, Carlton enrolled at Miami-Dade Community College, where he continued to refine his pitching mechanics and build physical strength. During his time playing college baseball in South Florida, scout George Silvey of the St. Louis Cardinals recognized Carlton's raw potential, particularly the late movement on his fastball and his athletic frame. In 1963, the Cardinals signed Carlton as an amateur free agent for a modest signing bonus.

Carlton began his professional career in the Cardinals' minor league system, spending time with the Rock Island Redbirds, Winnipeg Goldeyes, and Tulsa Oilers. Across three minor league seasons, he demonstrated rapid development, honing a sharp curveball to complement his fast-moving left-handed delivery. By early 1965, the 20-year-old left-hander had earned a call-up to the major leagues, setting the stage for a hall-of-fame career.

## Career

### St. Louis Cardinals (1965–1971)

Carlton made his Major League Baseball debut with the St. Louis Cardinals on April 12, 1965. After spending part of the 1965 and 1966 seasons shuttling between the majors and the Triple-A Tulsa Oilers, Carlton permanently established himself in the Cardinals' starting rotation in 1967. That season, he posted a 14–9 record with a 2.98 ERA, helping guide St. Louis to the National League pennant and a World Series title over the Boston Red Sox.

In 1969, Carlton emerged as an elite starter, compiling a 17–11 record and being named to his second All-Star team. On September 15, 1969, in a game against the New York Mets, Carlton set an MLB single-game record by striking out 19 batters in a nine-inning complete game, though the Cardinals lost 4–3 on two two-run home runs by Ron Swoboda. 

By 1971, Carlton achieved his first 20-win season, posting a 20–9 record with a 3.56 ERA. However, following the 1971 campaign, Carlton entered salary negotiations with Cardinals owner August "Gussie" Busch Jr. Carlton requested $65,000, which was $10,000 more than Busch's offer of $55,000. Unwilling to yield to Carlton's demands, Busch ordered general manager Bing Devine to trade the star left-hander.

### The 1972 Trade and Historic Season with the Phillies

On February 25, 1972, the Cardinals traded Steve Carlton to the Philadelphia Phillies in exchange for right-handed pitcher Rick Wise. The transaction rapidly came to be regarded as one of the most one-sided trade bargains in professional sports history.

Joining a rebuilding Phillies squad that finished dead last in the National League East, Carlton delivered a season for the ages. In 1972, he won the National League Pitching Triple Crown, leading the league in wins (27), earned run average (1.97), and strikeouts (310). He pitched 346.1 innings, threw 30 complete games, and recorded eight shutouts.

What made Carlton's 1972 performance unprecedented was the team context: the 1972 Phillies won only 59 total games, meaning Carlton accounted for 45.8% of his entire team's victories—a modern Major League Baseball record that has never been matched. He was unanimously awarded his first National League Cy Young Award and finished fifth in NL Most Valuable Player voting.

### Dominance, World Championship, and Four Cy Young Awards (1973–1985)

Although Carlton struggled slightly during the 1973 season due to shoulder fatigue, he quickly regained his form as the cornerstone of the franchise. Under general manager Paul Owens and manager Danny Ozark, the Phillies developed a powerhouse nucleus featuring third baseman Mike Schmidt, catcher Bob Boone, and shortstop Larry Bowa. Carlton served as the undisputed ace of the staff.

In 1977, Carlton won his second Cy Young Award, posting a 23–10 record with a 2.64 ERA and 198 strikeouts, leading the Phillies to 101 regular-season wins and their second consecutive NL East division title.

Carlton achieved his absolute peak during the 1980 season. He captured his third Cy Young Award after leading the National League in wins (24), strikeouts (286), innings pitched (304.0), and complete games (13), while establishing a 2.34 ERA. In the postseason, Carlton pitched the Phillies through the NLCS against the Houston Astros and dominated the Kansas City Royals in the 1980 World Series. Carlton won two games in the Fall Classic, including the decisive Game 6 at Veterans Stadium, securing the first World Series championship in the Philadelphia Phillies' 97-year history.

In 1982, Carlton made history by becoming the first pitcher to win four Cy Young Awards. He posted a 23–11 record with a 3.10 ERA, 286 strikeouts, and 19 complete games. On September 23, 1983, Carlton earned his 300th career victory against the St. Louis Cardinals at Busch Stadium. During the early 1980s, Carlton engaged in a high-profile race with contemporaries Nolan Ryan and Gaylord Perry to break Walter Johnson's long-standing major league career strikeout record of 3,508.

### Later Career and Retirement (1986–1988)

By 1985, age and shoulder injuries began to diminish Carlton's effectiveness. After posting a 4–8 record with a 6.18 ERA through the first two months of the 1986 season, the Phillies released Carlton on June 24, 1986. 

Determined to keep pitching, Carlton signed briefly with the San Francisco Giants later that summer, where he recorded his 4,000th career strikeout on August 5, 1986, against Eric Davis of the Cincinnati Reds. He subsequently pitched short stints for the Chicago White Sox (1986), Cleveland Indians (1987), and Minnesota Twins (1987–1988). Carlton earned a second World Series ring as a member of the 1987 Minnesota Twins, though he was not included on the postseason roster. He officially retired following the 1988 season at age 43.

## Personal life

Steve Carlton married his wife, Beverly Hodges, in 1965, and the couple raised two sons, Steven Jr. and Scott. Throughout his playing career and into retirement, Carlton maintained a quiet personal life centered around his passion for physical fitness, outdoor activities, and martial arts.

During his peak years in Philadelphia, Carlton was renowned for his unorthodox physical and mental preparation techniques, designed in collaboration with strength trainer Gus Hoefling. Long before sports science prioritized core strength and mental focus, Carlton engaged in intense physical regimens that included:
* Plunging his arm into big buckets of uncooked rice to strengthen the muscles in his hand, wrist, and forearm.
* Performing isometric exercises and martial arts stances to build lower-body stability.
* Practicing Eastern meditation, breathing control, and visualization techniques to maintain focus on the mound.

Carlton was also famous for his relationship with the sports media. Beginning in 1978, after believing that media coverage misinterpreted his remarks and created unnecessary distractions, Carlton instituted a complete blackout of news reporters. For over twelve years, he refused to grant on-the-record interviews, answering questions only after his induction into the Baseball Hall of Fame in 1994. Despite his silence toward journalists, Carlton was known among teammates as a generous, dedicated, and witty presence in the clubhouse.

## Legacy

Steve Carlton is universally recognized as one of the greatest left-handed pitchers in the history of Major League Baseball, frequently ranked alongside icons such as Sandy Koufax, Warren Spahn, and Lefty Grove. His devastating slider—characterized by a late, sharp downward break that induced countless swings and misses—is considered one of the single most effective pitches in baseball history.

Carlton's impact on Philadelphia sports culture remains profound. His 1972 campaign is remembered as the benchmark for individual pitching excellence, while his leadership in the 1980 World Series cemented his standing as a Philadelphia legend. During his 15 seasons in Philadelphia, he established franchise records for wins (241), strikeouts (3,031), and games started (499).

In 1989, the Philadelphia Phillies retired Carlton's jersey number 32 and inducted him into the Philadelphia Phillies Wall of Fame. A statue of Carlton depicting his iconic pitching delivery stands outside Citizens Bank Park in South Philadelphia.

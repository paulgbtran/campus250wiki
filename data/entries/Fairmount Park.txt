# Fairmount Park

**Fairmount Park** is a major municipal park system located in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, comprising extensive green spaces, riparian waterways, historic architecture, and major cultural institutions along the Schuylkill River. Managed by the Philadelphia Parks & Recreation Department (PPR), the park consists primarily of East Park and West Park on opposing banks of the Schuylkill River, as well as the surface of the river running between them. Historically developed during the nineteenth century to protect Philadelphia's municipal water supply and preserve open country within a rapidly industrializing urban grid, Fairmount Park grew to become one of the largest public urban park networks in the United States.

The park originated on the prominent hill on the Schuylkill's east bank named "Fairmount" by Pennsylvania founder William Penn. The site was purchased by the city in 1812 to house the Fairmount Water Works. The park expanded steadily through the acquisition of surrounding eighteenth- and nineteenth-century river estates, beginning with Lemon Hill in 1844 and Sedgeley in 1857. Following the City Consolidation Act of 1854, Lemon Hill was formally dedicated as "Fairmount Park" in 1855. State legislative acts in 1867 and 1868 created the Fairmount Park Commission (FPC), empowering it to acquire lands along the Schuylkill River and Wissahickon Creek to safeguard the city's drinking water and establish permanent public grounds for recreation.

Fairmount Park served as the site of the 1876 Centennial Exhibition, the first major official World's Fair in the United States, which catalyzed major landscape, infrastructural, and architectural developments, including the construction of Memorial Hall and Horticulture Hall. Over the twentieth century, the park expanded to roughly 4,500 acres across its main Schuylkill and Wissahickon sections, anchoring a citywide park network often referred to as the "Fairmount Park System." Following a city charter amendment and public referendum in 2008, the Fairmount Park Commission was dissolved in 2010, transitioning administrative governance to the newly formed Philadelphia Parks & Recreation Department and consolidating municipal park management citywide.

Today, Fairmount Park is recognized as a vital civic, environmental, and cultural landmark. It contains significant historic mansions, world-renowned public sculpture managed in tandem with the Association for Public Art (formerly the Fairmount Park Art Association), active recreational facilities including Boathouse Row—the national center for competitive rowing—and major cultural venues such as the Philadelphia Museum of Art, the Philadelphia Zoo, the Please Touch Museum, the Horticulture Center, the Shofuso Japanese House and Garden, and the Mann Center for the Performing Arts.

## History

### Origins and Colonial Era
The geographical area that comprises Fairmount Park was originally inhabited by the Lenape people prior to European colonization. Following the chartering of Pennsylvania in 1681, proprietor William Penn claimed the prominent natural elevation on the east bank of the Schuylkill River, just north of his planned rectangular city grid, naming it "Fairmount." Penn incorporated the hill into his expansive Springettsbury Manor estate. Throughout the eighteenth century, the lands bordering the Schuylkill River north and west of Philadelphia became favored locations for country estates and villa residences constructed by wealthy merchants and civic leaders seeking respite from summer heat and disease in the urban core.

Following late-eighteenth-century yellow fever epidemics that devastated Philadelphia's population, civic authorities recognized the urgent need for a clean, centralized public water supply. In 1812, the Philadelphia City Council's watering committee purchased the Fairmount hill to construct a modern waterworks pumping facility and reservoir. Completed under the direction of engineer Frederick Graff, the Fairmount Water Works began operating in the 1810s. During the 1820s, the city laid out ornamental public gardens, gravel walkways, and classical pavilions surrounding the waterworks and reservoir, creating one of Philadelphia's earliest public recreational destinations.

### Consolidation and Park Establishment (1844–1860s)
As industrial factories, textile mills, and chemical plants proliferated along the Schuylkill River upstream in the early nineteenth century, industrial runoff increasingly threatened the quality of the city's drinking supply. To prevent private industrial development along the riverfront, the city began purchasing adjoining waterfront estates. In 1844, Philadelphia purchased the Lemon Hill estate, formerly owned by merchant Robert Morris and later financier Henry Pratt, who had permitted public access to his extensive private gardens.

The Act of Consolidation of 1854 unified the city of Philadelphia with its surrounding districts and townships within Philadelphia County, granting municipal authorities explicit direction to establish public parks. In 1855, City Council officially dedicated the Lemon Hill property as "Fairmount Park." In 1857, the city acquired the adjacent Sedgeley estate, designed originally by architect Benjamin Henry Latrobe. In 1858, landscape designers James C. Sidney and Andrew Adams were commissioned to design a unified landscape plan connecting Lemon Hill and Sedgeley. Although preliminary roads and tree plantations were installed, large-scale improvements were halted in the mid-1860s as civic advocates lobbied state authorities for a vastly expanded municipal park reserve.

### The Fairmount Park Commission and Centennial Exhibition
In response to growing environmental and public health concerns, the Pennsylvania State Legislature passed the Park Acts of 1867 and 1868. These acts established the Fairmount Park Commission (FPC), a semi-autonomous board granted statutory authority to expropriate lands along both banks of the Schuylkill River and the Wissahickon Creek through eminent domain. The FPC was composed of six municipal officials (or their delegates) and ten private citizens appointed by the local courts to five-year terms. Notable civic leaders served as commissioners, including attorney Eli Kirk Price, publisher Morton McMichael, Civil War General George Gordon Meade, attorney John G. Johnson, investment banker Edward T. Stotesbury, rower and business executive John B. Kelly Sr., and civic activist Ernesta Drinker Ballard.

Although the Commission consulted prominent landscape architects Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux regarding structural designs for the expanded parkland, the FPC elected to implement minimal artificial topographical changes. Retaining the natural contours and existing forested groves of the acquired eighteenth-century villas proved financially economical and preserved the historic rural character of the river valley.

The acquisition of hundreds of acres along the west bank of the Schuylkill allowed Philadelphia to serve as host to the 1876 Centennial Exhibition, celebrating the 100th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. The FPC allocated a 400-acre site in West Park for the World's Fair. Federal, state, municipal, and private investments financed vast infrastructure projects, including grading, road construction, drainage systems, and two permanent architectural edifices: Memorial Hall, designed by Hermann J. Schwarzmann as an art gallery, and Horticulture Hall, a glass conservatory.

### 20th Century: Expansion, Infrastructure, and Encroachments
By 1900, the FPC governed over 3,000 acres across the Schuylkill and Wissahickon sections, making it the largest contiguous urban park system in the nation at the time. During the early twentieth century, additional land acquisitions expanded the park system to roughly 4,500 acres along the river and creek corridors, anchoring a wider municipal network encompassing 10,000 acres citywide.

The FPC exercised broad powers, constructing major civic thoroughfares such as the Benjamin Franklin Parkway, Roosevelt Boulevard, and Cobbs Creek Parkway. However, the park's expansive boundaries and pre-existing rights-of-way left it vulnerable to transportation infrastructure projects. Railroad corridors crossed through park areas, and during the 1950s, state and federal authorities constructed Interstate 76 (the Schuylkill Expressway) directly through West Fairmount Park, severing parkland from the riverfront. Despite these encroachments, the Commission successfully defended parklands against other threats, halting a proposed industrial plant in Fernhill Park in the 1960s, preventing the conversion of Kelly Drive into an express highway, and stopping the proposed Pulaski Expressway through the Tacony and Pennypack watersheds in the 1970s.

### Governance Reform and Dissolution
The Fairmount Park Commission operated semi-autonomously for over 140 years, managing its own budget, territories, and, until 1972, a dedicated park police force. However, its lack of direct taxing authority forced it to rely on annual municipal appropriations for routine operations and maintenance, leading to deferred maintenance and fiscal strain in the post-World War II era.

A 1937 legislative investigation highlighted operational inefficiencies, noting a lack of professional park management training among commissioners and poor attendance at meetings. In 2007, public controversy erupted when City Council and the FPC proposed leasing a section of Burholme Park to the Fox Chase Cancer Center for expansion. Local civic groups challenged the lease, and the Pennsylvania Commonwealth Court ruled that commercial leasing of designated public parkland violated the public trust doctrine.

The controversy led to a municipal referendum in November 2008, in which Philadelphia voters approved an amendment to the Home Rule Charter disestablishing the Fairmount Park Commission. On July 1, 2010, administration of the park system was consolidated under a new municipal agency, Philadelphia Parks & Recreation (PPR), supported by an advisory Commission on Parks and Recreation.

## Architecture

Fairmount Park contains an extensive collection of historic, civic, and landscape architecture spanning the late eighteenth through the twentieth centuries, reflecting changing stylistic movements from Georgian and Federal domestic design to Victorian municipal infrastructure and Beaux-Arts exhibition halls.

### Historic Country Houses
The park preserves a distinguished group of historic villas built as rural retreats by prominent Philadelphians:

*   **Lemon Hill**: Erected in 1800 by merchant Henry Pratt on the site of Robert Morris's earlier estate ("The Hills"), Lemon Hill is a classical Federal-style mansion featuring distinctive oval parlors and commanding views of the Schuylkill River.
*   **Mount Pleasant**: Constructed between 1762 and 1765 for privateer John Macpherson, Mount Pleasant is regarded as one of the finest examples of Georgian residential architecture in North America. It features symmetrical brick masonry, elaborate interior woodwork, and classical pediments.
*   **Woodford**: Built around 1756 as a single-story country home by William Coleman and expanded to two stories around 1771 by later owner David Franks, Woodford showcases Georgian architectural detailing and houses a major collection of colonial decorative arts.
*   **Strawberry Mansion**: Originally named *Summerville*, the core Federal-style structure was built in 1789 for Judge William Lewis, a prominent lawyer and abolitionist. It was expanded with Greek Revival wings in the 1820s by Judge Joseph Hemphill.
*   **Sedgeley Mansion**: Designed by Benjamin Henry Latrobe and constructed between 1799 and 1802, Sedgeley was an early example of Gothic Revival architecture in the United States. Following its acquisition by the city in 1857, the structure fell into disrepair and was demolished, though its surrounding grounds were absorbed into East Park.

### Municipal Infrastructure and Water Works
The **Fairmount Water Works**, constructed between 1812 and 1822 on the east bank of the Schuylkill below the Fairmount dam, was designed by Frederick Graff. The facility integrated high-pressure steam engines and later hydraulic water wheels and turbines inside Greek Revival stone buildings. The complex incorporated public promenades, classical pavilions, and decorative statuary, becoming an internationally acclaimed model of aesthetic utility until its closure in 1909.

Directly above the Water Works, atop the hill of Fairmount, the municipal water reservoir stood until it was dismantled in the early twentieth century. The site was selected as the location for the **Philadelphia Museum of Art**, a monumental Neoclassical building designed by Horace Trumbauer, Julian Abele, and Zantzinger, Borie and Medary, constructed between 1919 and 1928 out of Minnesota dolomite.

### Centennial Exhibition Buildings
Two major permanent edifices were constructed for the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in West Park:

*   **Memorial Hall**: Designed by Hermann J. Schwarzmann in the Beaux-Arts style, Memorial Hall was constructed of granite, glass, and iron to serve as the exhibition's art gallery. Surmounted by a central iron-and-glass dome topped by a statue of Columbia, the building later housed the Pennsylvania Museum and School of Industrial Art (predecessor of the Philadelphia Museum of Art). Following extensive restorations in the 2000s, it reopened in 2008 as the home of the Please Touch Museum.
*   **Horticulture Hall**: Also designed by Schwarzmann, Horticulture Hall was a vast glass-and-iron conservatory built in the Moorish style to display exotic botanical collections. Damaged during a major hurricane in 1954, it was demolished and replaced in 1976 by the modern Fairmount Park Horticulture Center.

### Boathouse Row
Located on the east bank of the Schuylkill River just north of the Water Works, **Boathouse Row** consists of fifteen Victorian-era boathouses constructed between the 1860s and 1900s. Built in a range of architectural styles including Gothic Revival, Second Empire, and Shingle Style, the structures house private rowing clubs affiliated with the Schuylkill Navy, as well as high school and university rowing programs. In the late twentieth century, architectural lighting was installed outlining the gables and frames of each boathouse, creating a widely recognized nighttime waterfront silhouette.

### Shofuso Japanese House and Garden
Situated on the grounds of the Horticulture Center in West Park, the **Shofuso Japanese House and Garden** features a traditional 17th-century style *shoin-zukuri* house. Built in Japan in 1953 using traditional materials and techniques, the house was initially exhibited at the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) in New York before being moved to Fairmount Park in 1958. It replaced an earlier Japanese structure, the 1600s Nio-mon temple gate acquired during the 1904 Louisiana Purchase Exposition, which had burned down in 1955. In 2007, Japanese artist Hiroshi Senju created and donated twenty large-scale waterfall murals installed inside the house.

## Cultural Significance

Fairmount Park occupies a central position in the cultural, social, and athletic history of Philadelphia, acting as a site for public recreation, civic gatherings, scientific preservation, and artistic patronage.

### Recreation and Sports
Recreational activity in Fairmount Park built upon traditional public use of the Schuylkill River and Wissahickon Creek dating back to the eighteenth century. Henry Pratt's open-door policy at Lemon Hill in the early 1800s set a precedent for public visitation, and by the late 1850s, civic festivals drew thousands of racially and socially diverse residents to the park grounds.

The park developed into an international center for competitive rowing following the formation of the Schuylkill Navy in 1858. Rowers from clubs such as Undine, University, and Bachelors' Barge Club competed regularly along the river. Olympic champion rower John B. Kelly Sr. achieved international distinction rowing on the Schuylkill, later serving as chairman of the Fairmount Park Commission.

Throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the park accommodated diverse recreational pursuits:
*   **Cycling and Equitation**: Cyclists gained legal access to park roads in the 1880s, joining horse riders who utilized miles of dirt bridgeways.
*   **Organized Athletics**: The Commission established public baseball diamonds, tennis courts, basketball courts, and soccer fields across East and West Parks.
*   **Performing Arts**: Music venues were integrated into the landscape, including the Lemon Hill band shell, the Robin Hood Dell (established in 1930 for outdoor summer concerts), and the Mann Center for the Performing Arts (opened in 1976 as the summer home for the Philadelphia Orchestra).

### Public Art and Sculpture
Fairmount Park features one of the dense collections of public outdoor sculpture in North America, due in large part to the work of the **Fairmount Park Art Association** (FPAA), established in 1872. As the nation's first private nonprofit organization dedicated to integrating public art with urban planning, the Art Association commissioned, conserved, and placed dozens of major sculptures throughout the park and along the Benjamin Franklin Parkway.

Prominent sculptures installed within Fairmount Park and its arterial parkways include:
*   ***Joan of Arc*** (1890) by Emmanuel Frémiet: A gilded bronze equestrian statue installed near the river.
*   ***All Wars Memorial to Colored Soldiers and Sailors*** (1934) by J. Otto Schweizer: A bronze and granite monument erected to honor Pennsylvania's African American military servicemen.
*   ***Three Way Piece Number One: Points*** (1964) by Henry Moore: A monumental abstract bronze installed near the river drive.
*   ***Kopernik*** (1972) by Dudley Talcott: A modern stainless steel sculpture honoring astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus.
*   ***Jesus Breaking Bread*** (1976) by Walter Erlebacher: A bronze sculpture commissioned for the 41st International Eucharistic Congress, located on the Parkway.
*   ***Iroquois*** (1983–1999) by Mark di Suvero: A monumental red-painted steel sculpture installed near the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

In June 2010, the Art Association (renamed the Association for Public Art) launched **Museum Without Walls™: AUDIO**, an interactive multi-platform audio program providing curated narrative histories for 35 outdoor sculptures along Kelly Drive and the Benjamin Franklin Parkway.

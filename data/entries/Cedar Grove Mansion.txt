## Lead section

**Cedar Grove Mansion** is a historic summer seat and country house museum located in West Fairmount Park, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Originally constructed between 1748 and 1750 in the Frankford neighborhood of Philadelphia, Cedar Grove served as the ancestral summer retreat for five generations of the prominent Quaker Paschall-Morris family. In the late 1920s, to protect the residence from suburban industrial expansion, the entire structure was meticulously dismantled stone-by-stone and reconstructed in Fairmount Park under the stewardship of the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

The estate provides an intact visual record of the evolution of American domestic architecture and decorative tastes across more than a century. Beginning as a modest two-room stone retreat built by Elizabeth Coates Paschall, a wealthy Quaker widow, the home underwent two major expansions in 1795 and 1812. These additions transformed the modest colonial house into an expansive three-story country manor blending Georgian symmetry with early Federal architectural features.

Cedar Grove is particularly noted for retaining its original interior furnishings and domestic artifacts, which were preserved continuously by the Paschall-Morris descendants and deeded alongside the house. The interior collections highlight Philadelphia craftsmanship across multiple eras, including notable examples of Queen Anne, Chippendale, and Federal period furniture, porcelain, and kitchenware reflecting Quaker aesthetic traditions of functional simplicity and quality.

Today, Cedar Grove is administered by the Philadelphia Museum of Art in partnership with the City of Philadelphia. It stands as a landmark within the Fairmount Park historic house network, serving as a testament to colonial and Early Republic domestic life, women's property ownership in 18th-century Pennsylvania, and early 20th-century historic preservation efforts in the United States.

## History

### Origins and Early Construction (1748–1750)
The origins of Cedar Grove date to December 1746, when Elizabeth Coates Paschall (1702–1768), a Quaker merchant's widow and practicing herbalist, purchased fifteen acres of farmland in Frankford, approximately five miles north of historic Philadelphia. Seeking a rural retreat away from the summer heat and periodic epidemic outbreaks in the urban core, Paschall initiated construction on a country house in 1748, which was completed by 1750. The initial structure was a compact, story-and-a-half stone dwelling featuring two primary ground-floor rooms and a corner staircase.

Following Elizabeth Paschall's death in 1768, the property passed to her daughter, Sarah Coates Paschall (1731–1812). Sarah maintained the estate through the turbulent era of the American Revolutionary War. During the British occupation of Philadelphia in 1777–1778, British troops occupied parts of the surrounding Frankford area, though the house survived undamaged. In 1795, Sarah Coates Paschall's niece, Sarah Paschall (1772–1842), married Isaac Wistar Morris (1770–1831), a member of a prominent Philadelphia Quaker merchant family, bringing the estate into the Paschall-Morris line.

### Expansions and Early Republic Period (1795–1812)
To accommodate a growing family and host social gatherings, Isaac Wistar Morris and Sarah Paschall Morris undertook substantial additions to Cedar Grove. In 1795, the couple doubled the size of the original house by expanding its footprint and adding a second floor. This expansion incorporated expanded bedchambers and refined parlor spaces designed in the prevailing Late Georgian style.

In 1812, a second major alteration added a third story, a wrapped piazza (veranda), and an expansive gambrel roof featuring a molded cornice. These modifications unified the earlier stone structures into a cohesive, three-story country residence. The interior was outfitted with updated hearths, classical wooden mantelpieces, and built-in cupboards that reflected the lighter, elegant aesthetics of the Federal style while preserving the understated Quaker motif of the original building.

### Industrialization, Relocation, and Museum Integration (1926–1928)
Through the 19th and early 20th centuries, the Frankford region evolved from an agrarian countryside into a densely built industrial hub dominated by textile mills, iron foundries, and rail lines. By the 1920s, Cedar Grove was surrounded by factories and the tracks of the Pennsylvania Railroad, making its original site unviable as a residential property or historic park.

In 1926, Lydia Thompson Morris (1849–1932), the great-granddaughter of Sarah Paschall Morris and Isaac Wistar Morris and the last private owner of Cedar Grove, offered the building and its entire contents to the City of Philadelphia. She stipulated that the house be moved to a parkland setting to ensure its long-term preservation. Under the direction of Fiske Kimball, director of the Philadelphia Museum of Art, and architect Erling H. Pedersen, the house was carefully documented, dismantled, and transported to Lansdowne Drive in West Fairmount Park. Reconstructed between 1927 and 1928, Cedar Grove opened to the public in 1928 as one of the nation's earliest examples of architectural salvage and museum-managed house preservation.

## Architecture

Cedar Grove's architectural structure illustrates a century of structural adaptation. Built primarily from locally quarried native fieldstone covered in light plaster and whitewash, the mansion features classic Pennsylvania colonial proportions:

* **Exterior Structure:** The front facade exhibits a symmetrical five-bay layout with multi-pane double-hung sash windows, dark wooden shutters, and a central entrance. A covered piazza spans the front facade, providing shade and outdoor living space typical of regional 18th-century summer retreats.
* **Roofline and Chimneys:** The house is capped by a prominent gambrel roof with dormer windows on the third floor. Two large internal brick chimneys service corner hearths throughout the primary living quarters.
* **Interior Layout:** The floor plan follows a central-hall organization. The ground floor features a formal parlor, dining room, central stair hall, and an attached service kitchen equipped with an authentic open hearth, crane, and original iron cooking implements.
* **Woodwork and Millwork:** The interior contains intact raised paneled walls, molded chair rails, wrought-iron hardware, and built-in glass corner cupboards executed in native yellow pine and walnut.

## Cultural significance

Cedar Grove holds substantial cultural and historical importance within Philadelphia and the wider context of American decorative arts:

1. **Quaker Heritage and Material Culture:** Unlike many grand country estates of the colonial elite that favored ostentatious Baroque or Rococo ornamentation, Cedar Grove reflects the Quaker principle of "plainness"—focusing on structural quality, craftsmanship, and utility without superfluous gilding.
2. **Female Landownership:** The construction of the original homestead by Elizabeth Coates Paschall highlights the legal and financial agency exercised by wealthy unmarried or widowed women in colonial Pennsylvania. Paschall's surviving medical journals and property ledgers remain valuable primary sources for historians of early American medicine and gender studies.
3. **Preservation History:** The relocation of Cedar Grove in 1926 served as a foundational project for the historic house museum movement in the United States, demonstrating early technical methodologies for deconstructing, cataloging, and reassembling masonry historic structures.

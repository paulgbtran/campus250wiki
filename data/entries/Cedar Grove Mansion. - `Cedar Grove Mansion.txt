## 2. Lead section

Cedar Grove Mansion is a historic country house museum located in West Fairmount Park, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Originally constructed between 1748 and 1750 as a modest summer retreat in the Harrowgate neighborhood (near Frankford, northeast of downtown Philadelphia), the estate evolved over nearly two centuries into a grand family seat reflecting successive generations of Quaker design, material culture, and architectural adaptation. Built by Elizabeth Coates Paschall, a widowed Quaker businesswoman, Cedar Grove is notable for its continuous passage through five generations of female descendants within the Coates, Paschall, and Morris family lines.

Architecturally, Cedar Grove represents a synthesis of mid-18th-century vernacular Colonial building techniques and early 19th-century Georgian and Federal enhancements. The structure features indigenous Pennsylvania gray stone, a broad gambrel roof, wrap-around piazzas (porches), and impeccably preserved interior woodwork. The home expanded organically across three major construction phases—1748–1750, 1795, and 1812—mirroring the growing wealth, domestic needs, and aesthetic evolution of its Quaker inhabitants.

In the 1920s, rapidly expanding industrialization surrounding its original site endangered the estate. In response, Lydia Thompson Morris deeded the mansion and its full complement of original family furnishings to the City of Philadelphia. Between 1926 and 1928, the entire structure was meticulously dismantled, stone by stone, and reerected in West Fairmount Park under the supervision of the Philadelphia Museum of Art. Today, Cedar Grove stands as one of the premier historic site installations maintained by the museum, providing an authentic window into 18th- and early 19th-century domestic life in Philadelphia.

## 3. Origins and construction

The origin of Cedar Grove is tied to the life and ambition of Elizabeth Coates Paschall (1702–1768). Born into a prominent Philadelphia Quaker family, Elizabeth Coates developed a strong aptitude for commerce, land management, and domestic medicine. Following the death of her husband, merchant Joseph Paschall, in 1741, she assumed management of the family’s business affairs and real estate holdings. Unusually self-reliant for a woman of her era, she maintained an extensive remedy book detailing herbal medicine and practical remedies, earning a reputation as a knowledgeable practitioner among her peers.

In 1746, seeking refuge for her children from the seasonal epidemics and summer heat of urban Philadelphia, Paschall purchased fifteen acres of rural property in Harrowgate, near Frankford Creek. Between 1748 and 1750, she oversaw the construction of the initial dwelling at Cedar Grove. The original house was a simple, two-story stone structure consisting of five rooms, designed according to traditional Quaker principles of utility, modesty, and durability. Paschall’s early stewardship set a precedent of independent female property ownership that defined the mansion’s history for over 150 years.

## 4. History and relocation

The functional history of Cedar Grove Mansion spans its operational life as an active working farm, a seasonal country retreat, and its eventual transition into a public museum asset managed by civic institutions. During the mid-to-late 18th century, Cedar Grove served as a center for seasonal agricultural activity, orchards, and family gatherings. Positioned along key transit routes northeast of Philadelphia, the estate offered respite during periods of urban unrest and disease outbreaks, including the yellow fever epidemics of the 1790s.

Following Elizabeth Coates Paschall’s death, ownership passed to her daughter, Sarah Paschall Morris (1733–1795). In 1795, the property transferred to Sarah’s son, Isaac Wistar Morris, and his wife, Sarah Coates Morris. Under Isaac and Sarah Morris, Cedar Grove underwent significant structural transformations to accommodate their growing family. In 1795, the couple added a substantial two-story wing and extended wrap-around wooden porches. In 1812, a major renovation added a third story under a large gambrel roof, creating the unified, expansive residence seen today.

By the early 20th century, the surrounding neighborhood of Frankford had transformed into a dense industrial and rail corridor. Recognizing that the mansion’s historical integrity was compromised by urban encroachment, Lydia Thompson Morris (the last private owner) negotiated with the Philadelphia Museum of Art and city commissioners in 1926 to preserve the structure. The mansion was systematically dismantled, transported across the city, and reassembled in West Fairmount Park between 1926 and 1928. Since its public opening, Cedar Grove has functioned as an educational historic house museum administered by the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

## 5. Interior and decorative arts

Domestic and family life at Cedar Grove reflected the social values, religious customs, and material culture of Philadelphia’s Quaker elite. Five generations of the Paschall-Morris lineage resided in or maintained the property, preserving family heirlooms, diaries, and household records across nearly two centuries. The domestic rhythm of the estate was shaped by Quaker principles of plainness, though paired with refined craftsmanship in household acquisitions.

The interior of Cedar Grove is notable for containing its original accumulation of family furnishings, which remained with the house during its 1926 relocation. The decorative objects span several distinct style periods:
* **William and Mary & Queen Anne:** Early 18th-century walnut tables, high chests, and simple turnings associated with Elizabeth Coates Paschall.
* **Chippendale:** Mid-to-late 18th-century Philadelphia-crafted chairs, carved desks, and dining furniture added during Sarah Coates Paschall's tenure.
* **Federal:** Early 19th-century sideboards, painted furniture, and refined metalwork acquired by Isaac and Sarah Morris during the 1812 expansion.

The kitchen block features an authentic open hearth equipped with original iron cookware, a built-in bake oven, and utilitarian ceramic wares, highlighting the labor and daily routines involved in managing an 18th-century country estate.

---

## 6. Legacy

Cedar Grove Mansion occupies a significant place in the architectural and preservation history of Philadelphia. It stands as an enduring physical record of Quaker architectural evolution, illustrating how early rural farmhouses were enlarged into sophisticated suburban villas without losing their foundational simplicity. The house's succession of female owners—from Elizabeth Coates Paschall to Lydia Thompson Morris—provides a compelling historical case study of female property control and estate stewardship in early America.

Civically, the relocation of Cedar Grove between 1926 and 1928 marked an early landmark achievement in American architectural preservation. Rather than allowing industrial development to demolish the home, civic leaders and the Philadelphia Museum of Art executed one of the earliest successful full-structure relocation projects in the region. Today, Cedar Grove is preserved as part of Fairmount Park's historic house collection, serving as an educational site for scholars of Early American decorative arts, social history, and historic preservation.

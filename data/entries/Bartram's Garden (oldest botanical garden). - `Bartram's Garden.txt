# Bartram's Garden

| **Bartram's Garden** |
| :--- |
| **Location** | 5400 Lindbergh Boulevard (54th Street & Lindbergh Blvd), West bank of the Schuylkill River, Southwest Philadelphia, Pennsylvania |
| **Coordinates / Area** | Kingsessing Township; originally 102 acres (1728), expanded to 12 acres of curated gardens at peak |
| **Established** | 1728 |
| **Founders** | John Bartram (1699–1777) |
| **Designation** | National Historic Landmark (Designated October 1960) |
| **Management** | City of Philadelphia, Fairmount Park Commission / Philadelphia Parks & Recreation, The John Bartram Association |
| **Significance** | Oldest surviving botanic garden in North America |

**Bartram's Garden** is a historic botanical garden located on the west bank of the Schuylkill River in Southwest Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Established in 1728 by the self-taught naturalist John Bartram (1699–1777), it is recognized as the oldest surviving botanical garden in North America. Over nearly three centuries, the property has operated as a family farm, a commercial plant nursery, an international hub of botanical scientific exchange, a 19th-century public pleasure garden, and a municipal park.

During the 18th century, the garden served as the primary nexus for the collection, cultivation, and distribution of North American flora to European naturalists, aristocrats, and scientific institutions. It hosted prominent political figures of the early American republic, including George Washington, Thomas Jefferson, and Benjamin Franklin, as well as foreign and domestic naturalists who sought out its expansive collection of native plant species.

Following John Bartram's death in 1777, the garden was maintained and expanded across two subsequent generations of the Bartram family—most notably under his son William Bartram and granddaughter Ann Bartram Carr—before financial strain prompted its sale in 1850. Preserved through private stewardship and a subsequent civic campaign in the late 19th century, the property came under municipal control in 1891.

In October 1960, Bartram's Garden was designated a National Historic Landmark in recognition of its foundational role in the history of American botany and horticulture. Today, the site is operated as a public park and educational heritage location by the non-profit John Bartram Association in partnership with the City of Philadelphia.

---

## Early life and education

The founder of the garden, John Bartram, was born in 1699 in Darby, Pennsylvania, to a Quaker family. Lacking formal higher education or institutional scientific training, Bartram was a self-taught naturalist whose intellectual development relied heavily on keen observation of local plant life, self-directed reading, and agricultural experimentation. His passion for botany led him to acquire land along the Schuylkill River to establish a dedicated farm and experimental plot.

In 1728, Bartram purchased a 102-acre tract in Kingsessing Township, an area originally settled by Swedish colonists in the mid-17th century. On this property, situated along the riverbank, Bartram laid the foundations for his family farm and botanic garden. In 1731, he personally constructed a two-story stone house on the site, which became the center of the estate and domestic life for his family.

Bartram's early botanical pursuits were cultivated through interactions with prominent intellectuals in colonial Philadelphia. He established close friendships with statesman and polymath Benjamin Franklin and provincial leader James Logan. Logan, a scholar of natural philosophy, shared his botanical research with Bartram, including early experiments on the fertilization of Indian corn (*Zea mays*). Inspired by these exchanges, Bartram conducted his own hybridization experiments on *Lychnis dioica*, corroborating Logan's findings regarding plant sexuality and reproductive mechanisms.

## Career

### 18th-century plant trade and scientific networks
Bartram's career expanded rapidly after 1731, when James Logan introduced him to Peter Collinson, a London cloth merchant, fellow of the Royal Society, and avid gardener. Their correspondence initiated a trade network through which Bartram shipped boxes of North American seeds, saplings, and dried botanical specimens across the Atlantic—a service known informally as "Bartram's Boxes." 

By 1750, Bartram was supplying native flora to prominent European collectors, including Sir Hans Sloane, founder of the British Museum, and Swedish taxonomist Carl Linnaeus, who famously praised Bartram as a premier natural botanist. Bartram advertised his seeds for sale in London publications, expanding his business into a major transatlantic trade operation. To gather new plant varieties, Bartram undertook extensive botanical expeditions throughout the North American colonies, traveling from the Great Lakes down to Florida. Upon returning to Kingsessing, he engineered specialized micro-environments across his property, constructing artificial ponds, marshlands, open meadowlands, seed nurseries, and greenhouses to accommodate aquatic, temperate, and tropical specimens.

### The second generation: William and John Bartram Jr.
Following John Bartram's death in 1777, during the American Revolutionary War, ownership of the estate passed to his sons, John Bartram Jr. (1743–1812) and William Bartram (1739–1823). William Bartram, who had accompanied his father on numerous expeditions, gained international fame as a botanical illustrator, natural historian, and author. Under the brothers' management, the garden maintained its commercial focus while solidifying its status as an international scientific destination.

Distinguished botanists frequented the garden to consult with William Bartram and examine its collections. Visitors included French botanist André Michaux, as well as American naturalists Benjamin Smith Barton, Thomas Say, Thomas Nuttall, and Frederick Pursh. High-profile political leaders visited the grounds to acquire plants for their personal estates; George Washington and Thomas Jefferson repeatedly placed commercial orders with the Bartrams. In the summer of 1787, delegates attending the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia famously adjourned a session early to take a boat trip across the Schuylkill River for an afternoon tour of the gardens. The Bartrams also maintained plant exchanges with local neighbors, notably William Hamilton of the nearby Woodlands estate, who presented William Bartram with a specimen of the *Ginkgo biloba* tree in 1785—one of the first three ginkgoes introduced to North America from England.

### The third generation and pleasure garden era
In the early 19th century, management transitioned to the third generation of the family under Ann Bartram Carr (1779–1858), daughter of John Bartram Jr., and her husband, Colonel Robert Carr (1778–1866). Under their stewardship, the property reached its maximum physical and operational scale as a commercial nursery. The cultivated grounds encompassed twelve acres of specialized gardens and ten greenhouses, containing over 1,400 native North American plant species and upwards of 1,000 exotic varieties.

Faced with increasing commercial competition and economic shifts, the Carrs diversified the garden's use in 1844 and 1845 by opening it to the public as a commercial summer pleasure garden. Visitors were offered refreshments, ice cream, and guided walks, with steamboats running regular excursions three days a week from the Delaware River wharves to the garden’s Schuylkill dock.

## Personal life

Throughout the 18th and early 19th centuries, the Bartram house and gardens functioned as both an active working agricultural enterprise and a private home. The family practiced Quaker traditions of hospitality, regularly hosting traveling naturalists, international scholars, and regional leaders at their dining table.

Financial difficulties mounting by the mid-19th century forced Colonel Robert Carr and Ann Bartram Carr to sell the property. In 1850, the estate was purchased by railroad industrialist Andrew Eastwick (1811–1879). Eastwick recognized the cultural and historical value of the site. Rather than demolishing the original stone house and botanical landscape, he preserved them as part of his private estate. While constructing his own mansion, "Bartram Hall," nearby, Eastwick resided in the historic John Bartram house to ensure its preservation.

Following Eastwick's death in 1879, the property fell into a brief period of neglect. Recognizing the danger of urban industrial encroachment along the Schuylkill River, a preservation campaign was launched by Thomas Meehan (1826–1901), a former gardener for Eastwick who had become a nurseryman and member of Philadelphia's Common Council, alongside Charles S. Sargent (1841–1927), director of Harvard University's Arnold Arboretum.

---

## Legacy

### Municipal acquisition and preservation
Meehan's legislative advocacy culminated in 1888 when the City of Philadelphia officially placed Bartram's Garden on the city map for parkland reservation. The city formally acquired full title to the land in 1891, integrating it into the city's public park system. 

In 1893, descendants of John Bartram established the John Bartram Association to oversee the historical interpretation and preservation of the garden. Partnering with University of Pennsylvania botany professor John Muirhead Macfarlane (1855–1943), the association compiled a comprehensive archive and research library dedicated to the Bartram family’s scientific legacy. Management of the site was transferred to the Fairmount Park Commission in 1923, which undertook structural restorations of the historic stone house and garden beds.

### Historic designation and living collections
On October 9, 1960, the U.S. Department of the Interior designated Bartram's Garden a National Historic Landmark. Today, the 45-acre public site operates as a living museum of American botanical history, urban agriculture center, and public park managed co-operatively by the John Bartram Association and Philadelphia Parks & Recreation.

The garden retains several historically significant living plant specimens, including:
* The historic **1785 Ginkgo tree**, considered the oldest surviving specimen of *Ginkgo biloba* in North America.
* A **Yellowwood tree** (*Cladrastis kentukea*) sent to the garden in 1796 by French botanist André Michaux.
* Specimen plantings of ***Franklinia alatamaha***, a flowering tree discovered by John and William Bartram along the Altamaha River in Georgia in 1765. The tree became extinct in the wild by the early 19th century; all living examples of *Franklinia* today descend from the seeds collected and propagated at Bartram's Garden.
* The **Bartram Oak** (*Quercus × bartramii*), a naturally occurring hybrid oak identified and cataloged on the site.

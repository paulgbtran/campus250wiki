### Lead section

Absalom Jones (November 5, 1746 – February 13, 1818) was an African-American abolitionist, clergyman, and community organizer who became a foundational figure in the religious and civic history of post-Revolutionary Philadelphia. Born into slavery in Delaware, Jones taught himself to read and write before purchasing his own freedom and that of his family. He spent the remainder of his life in Philadelphia, where he emerged as one of the nation's most influential leaders for the free Black community during the early republic.

Jones is historically recognized as the first African American to be ordained as a priest in the Episcopal Church of the United States, receiving his ordination in 1804. Alongside his close friend and fellow reformer Richard Allen, Jones co-founded the Free African Society in 1787, one of the earliest mutual aid societies established by African Americans in the United States. The organization provided crucial financial, social, and spiritual support to formerly enslaved people transitioning to freedom in urban Pennsylvania.

During the devastating Yellow Fever Epidemic of 1793 in Philadelphia, Jones played a vital leadership role by organizing relief efforts, nursing the sick, and burying the dead at the request of civic authorities. In the aftermath of the crisis, Jones co-authored a prominent pamphlet defending the Black community against racist allegations of misconduct, marking one of the earliest published prose defenses of African Americans by Black authors in the country.

In 1794, Jones founded the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas, the first Black Episcopal congregation in the United States. Throughout his career as a rector, he advocated vigorously against slavery, petitioning the U.S. Congress to abolish the institution and petitioning for the repeal of restrictive fugitive slave laws. Today, Jones is commemorated annually on February 13 in the Calendar of Saints of the Episcopal Church.

### Early life and education

Absalom Jones was born into chattel slavery on November 5, 1746, on a plantation in Sussex County, Delaware, owned by Abraham Wynkoop, a wealthy planter. From a young age, Jones demonstrated an intense desire for literacy and education. He saved small gratuities given to him by visitors to purchase a primer and a spelling book, teaching himself the fundamentals of reading. He sought assistance from literate acquaintances on the plantation and in the surrounding town to further his understanding.

In 1762, Benjamin Wynkoop (son of Abraham Wynkoop) sold the Delaware farm and relocated to Philadelphia, bringing 16-year-old Jones with him. In Philadelphia, Jones worked as a clerk and manual laborer in Wynkoop’s retail store. Despite the demanding hours, Jones secured permission to attend an evening school for Black students operated by the Society of Friends (Quakers). There, under the tutelage of abolitionist educators, he expanded his mastery of reading, writing, and mathematics.

In 1770, Jones married Mary Thomas, an enslaved woman owned by Sarah King, a neighbor of the Wynkoop family. The marriage took place at St. Peter's Church in Philadelphia. Driven by a desire to ensure that any future children would be born free—under the legal doctrine of *partus sequitur ventrem*—Jones prioritized purchasing his wife's freedom before his own. He raised funds by taking on additional labor, borrowing money, and securing small loans from supportive Quakers. After securing Mary's manumission, Jones continued working tirelessly to pay off his debts and save enough money to buy his own freedom, which Benjamin Wynkoop finally granted via a deed of manumission on October 1, 1784.

### Career

#### The Free African Society and Walkout from St. George's
Following his manumission in 1784, Jones became an active lay preacher at St. George’s Methodist Episcopal Church in Philadelphia. Due to his compelling preaching and outreach, large numbers of Black Philadelphians joined the congregation. However, as the Black membership grew, white church trustees instituted segregated seating policies, eventually forcing Black worshippers to sit in a newly constructed balcony gallery. 

Earlier in April 1787, Jones and Allen had established the Free African Society (FAS). Designed as a non-denominational mutual aid organization, the FAS provided financial relief for widows and orphans, offered burial assistance, maintained moral oversight, and served as a vital forum for civic organization among Philadelphia’s growing population of free African Americans.

In November 1792, during a Sunday morning service at St. George's, church ushers physically pulled Absalom Jones, Richard Allen, and William Gray from their knees while they were engaged in prayer in the gallery. In protest against this racial discrimination, Jones, Allen, and the majority of the Black congregants staged a historic walkout, severing their ties with St. George's.

#### The 1793 Yellow Fever Epidemic
In the late summer of 1793, Philadelphia—then the capital of the United States—was struck by a catastrophic yellow fever epidemic. Under the mistaken belief held by physician Benjamin Rush that African Americans were immune to the disease, Mayor Matthew Clarkson appealed to Absalom Jones and Richard Allen to mobilize the Black community to assist the city.

Jones and Allen organized teams of free Black citizens to serve as nurses, transport the afflicted, perform medical treatments, and bury the dead when many white residents fled the city. Despite their heroic and essential contributions, publisher Mathew Carey published a pamphlet in late 1793 accusing Black workers of extortion and theft during the crisis. In response, Jones and Allen published *A Narrative of the Proceedings of the Black People, During the Late Awful Calamity in Philadelphia* (1794), providing a detailed accounting of their services, refuting Carey's accusations, and demonstrating the moral integrity and civic devotion of the Black community.

#### Pastoral Ministry and Anti-Slavery Activism
Following the departure from St. George's, the members of the FAS decided to build an independent place of worship. While Richard Allen favored establishing a Methodist congregation, Jones led the majority in aligning with the Protestant Episcopal Church, seeking a traditional liturgical structure and greater local autonomy. 

In 1794, the congregation constructed and dedicated the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas. The church was formally admitted into the Episcopal Diocese of Pennsylvania, with the stipulation that it could manage its own internal affairs, though Black members were initially denied voting rights in diocesan conventions.

Bishop William White ordained Jones as a deacon in 1795 and subsequently ordained him as a priest on January 1, 1804. Under Jones's leadership as rector, St. Thomas grew rapidly, establishing a day school for Black children, a variety of benevolent societies, and an active anti-slavery network. Jones was an outspoken opponent of the transatlantic slave trade and domestic chattel slavery, organizing annual anti-slavery sermons and petitioning state and federal governments for emancipation. On January 2, 1800, he was the primary signatory on a petition submitted to the U.S. Congress calling for the immediate abolition of the slave trade and the repeal of the Fugitive Slave Act of 1793.

### Personal life

Absalom Jones’s personal life was characterized by deep religious devotion, strong family bonds, and an unwavering commitment to community welfare. After marrying Mary Thomas in 1770, the couple lived in Philadelphia, where they established a stable home despite the precarious social and economic position of free and enslaved African Americans during the late colonial and early national periods. Jones’s willingness to delay his own legal freedom for over a decade in order to ensure his wife's liberty highlighted his character and devotion.

Throughout his adult life, Jones maintained a modest lifestyle, residing near his church in the center of Philadelphia. Though he and Richard Allen took different denominational paths—with Allen establishing the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church in 1816—the two men maintained a close personal friendship and joint political alliance until Jones's death. Jones was known among his contemporaries for his gentle demeanor, persuasive pulpit eloquence, and deep pastoral care for the impoverished and marginalized residents of the city.

---

### Legacy

Absalom Jones died on February 13, 1818, at his home in Philadelphia at the age of 71. His funeral was attended by thousands of residents, including prominent civic leaders, clergy, and members of the African-American community, reflecting his standing as a revered elder statesman of the city. He was buried in the churchyard of the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas. His remains were later reinterred within the sanctuary of the modern St. Thomas congregation in West Philadelphia, where a shrine honors his memory.

Jones's legacy endures through the institutions he helped build and his pioneering role in African-American civil rights and religious history. By establishing independent Black religious and civic organizations, Jones demonstrated that African Americans could govern their own affairs, construct lasting institutions, and articulate a powerful moral vision for national reform.

In 1973, the Episcopal Church officially designated February 13 as the Feast Day of Saint Absalom Jones in its liturgical calendar. Churches across the United States celebrate his feast day annually with special services, educational programs, and fundraising events for Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) affiliated with the Episcopal Church.

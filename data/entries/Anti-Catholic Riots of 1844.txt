## Lead section

The **Anti-Catholic Riots of 1844** (also known as the **Nativist Riots of 1844**) were a series of violent, bloody sectarian clashes that took place in May and July 1844 in Philadelphia County, Pennsylvania. Spanning the industrial suburbs of Kensington and Southwark, the riots pitted native-born Protestant nativists against newly arrived Irish-American Roman Catholic immigrants. The unrest resulted in the destruction of dozens of homes, a Catholic convent, and two prominent Catholic churches, as well as the deaths of at least twenty people and injuries to scores more. 

The riots arose from decades of growing ethnic and religious antagonism fueled by rapid Irish immigration to Philadelphia's textile manufacturing hubs. Tensions escalated sharply in the early 1840s over a dispute regarding public education, specifically whether Catholic school children should be required to read from the Protestant King James Bible and sing Protestant hymns. Political nativists, organized under the American Republican Association and championed by newspaper editor Lewis Levin, weaponized the educational dispute to stoke anti-Catholic fears and mobilize Protestant voters in an election year.

The conflict erupted into two distinct waves of violence. In May 1844, street fighting in Kensington triggered widespread arson and violence, requiring the intervention of state militia and federal troops to restore order. After a brief period of martial calm, violent unrest flared again in July in Southwark, where nativist crowds clashed directly with state militia forces in pitched street battles that involved the use of cannon fire by both sides.

The extraordinary duration and severity of the 1844 riots shocked the nation and highlighted the severe inadequacies of Philadelphia’s fragmented municipal watch system. In the immediate aftermath, nativists achieved major political victories at the ballot box. However, the chaos also sparked urgent debates over urban governance and peacekeeping, directly catalyzing legislative reforms that established unified police forces and ultimately led to the Consolidation Act of 1854, which unified the City of Philadelphia and its outlying county districts under a single municipal government.

## Background

Sectarian and ethnic friction had a long history in Philadelphia, dating back to the late eighteenth century when Irish textile workers began migrating to the city after being displaced by industrial mechanization in the British Isles. As early as 1828, cultural and religious animosity turned fatal when an off-duty watchman was killed following a dispute in which he disparaged Irish immigrants. In 1831, street fighting broke out on Fifth Street between Irish Catholics and Protestant Orangemen celebrating the anniversary of the Battle of the Boyne.

By the early 1840s, anti-Catholic agitation intensified as Protestant clergymen and political figures organized against what they perceived as a Papal threat to American republican institutions. In 1842, dozens of Protestant ministers established the American Protestant Association to defend the nation against Roman Catholicism. Political nativism gained a powerful press organ in early 1843 when editor Lewis Levin (1808–1860) turned the Philadelphia *Daily Sun* into a platform for xenophobic attacks against Catholic immigration. In December 1843, Levin helped found the American Republican Association, a political party explicitly dedicated to reducing the political influence of foreign-born citizens.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                       ROOTS OF THE CONFLICT                           |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  1780s–1830s: Irish textile migration; early clashes (1828, 1831)     |
|  1842: Formation of the American Protestant Association               |
|  1843: Lewis Levin uses Daily Sun to promote American Republican Party|
|  1844 (Feb): Hugh Clark proposes suspending school Bible reading      |
|  1844 (May): Nativists march on Kensington; violence erupts           |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

The primary flashpoint for the 1844 riots was a dispute over religious instruction in Philadelphia's public schools. Roman Catholic Bishop Francis Patrick Kenrick (1796–1863), an Irish immigrant, objected to Protestant teachers leading Catholic students in singing Protestant hymns and forcing them to read from the Protestant King James Bible. Kenrick requested that Catholic pupils be permitted to read the Catholic Douay-Rheims version instead. Nativist leaders seized upon Kenrick's requests, framing them as a "popish plot" to ban the Bible from public schools altogether.

In February 1844, the controversy reached a boiling point in the northeastern suburb of Kensington, home to a large concentration of Irish immigrant weavers. Hugh Clark (1796–1862), a Catholic school director in Kensington, suggested suspending all Bible reading in local classrooms until the school board could formulate an accommodation acceptable to both Protestants and Catholics. Nativist politicians and activists interpreted Clark's proposal as a direct assault on American liberty and Protestant heritage. Thousands of nativists rallied in protest at Independence Square before taking their movement directly into the immigrant stronghold of Kensington.

## Course of events

### May Riots in Kensington

On May 3, 1844, nativists held a political rally in Kensington but were aggressively chased out of the neighborhood by local Irish residents. Three days later, on May 6, nativists reassembled in greater numbers at a Kensington market to reassert their presence. A violent melee broke out, during which 18-year-old nativist George Shiffler (1826–1844) was shot and fatally wounded. Shiffler's death was immediately mythologized by the nativist press as a patriotic martyrdom. By the end of the day, a second man—a bystander—was dead, and several other nativists were wounded, two mortally.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                       CHRONOLOGY OF VIOLENCE                          |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  MAY 3: Nativist rally in Kensington repelled by Irish residents.     |
|  MAY 6: Second Kensington rally; George Shiffler shot dead.           |
|  MAY 7-8: Arson in Kensington; St. Michael's & St. Augustine's burned.|
|  MAY 10: Order restored by militia, posses, Army & Navy forces.       |
|  JULY 5-6: Crowd surrounds St. Philip de Neri in Southwark for arms.  |
|  JULY 7-8: Cannon combat between militia and mob; military withdraws. |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

On May 7, the Sheriff of Philadelphia County called upon the First Brigade of the Pennsylvania Militia, commanded by Brigadier General George Cadwalader (1806–1879), to enforce order. Although the militia deployed to the scene, troops struggled to contain dispersed groups of rioters who roamed the district setting fires. On May 8, nativist mobs systematically gutted Irish residences (including the home of Hugh Clark), a Sisters of Charity convent, and two Catholic churches: St. Michael's Church at Second and Master Streets, and St. Augustine's Church at Fourth and Vine Streets. Order was fully restored only by May 10, following a massive force deployment comprising military companies from neighboring cities, local police, citizen posses, and federal U.S. Army and Navy troops.

### July Battle in Southwark

For eight weeks following the May riots, Philadelphia maintained an uneasy calm. However, both sides quietly prepared for renewed conflict. In the southern district of Southwark—a nativist stronghold—concerns over potential anti-Catholic attacks led the brother of a local priest to stockpile firearms in the basement of the Church of St. Philip de Neri on Queen Street. On Friday, July 5, word of the armaments spread, and a mob of thousands surrounded the church demanding the surrender of the weapons.

The Sheriff requested military assistance, and on July 6, General Cadwalader led approximately two hundred militia troops into Southwark to secure the building and protect a small group of militia guards and nativist prisoners inside. On Sunday, July 7, the surrounding crowd armed itself with improvised cannon mounted on wheels. Urged on by nativist speakers, the mob forced the militia guards to relinquish control of the church and release their prisoners.

At sunset on July 7, Cadwalader returned at the head of a larger militia column and attempted to disperse the crowd. When rioters attacked the soldiers with bricks, paving stones, and bottles, Cadwalader ordered his men to fire into the mob, killing at least two people and wounding several others. The crowd responded by launching a coordinated counterattack around 9:00 PM. For the next four hours, rioters and militia engaged in pitched urban combat along the streets of Southwark, exchanging musket and heavy cannon fire. By the morning of July 8, four militiamen and approximately twelve rioters were dead, with dozens more severely wounded. To stop the bloodshed, Southwark's local aldermen negotiated the withdrawal of Cadwalader's militia, replacing them with state militia reinforcements brought in from across Pennsylvania to patrol the rest of the city.

## Aftermath

The Anti-Catholic Riots of 1844 were among the longest and most severe episodes of urban unrest in nineteenth-century American history. Unlike brief, spontaneous riots typical of the antebellum era, the Philadelphia violence persisted over months due to sustained political organization and press coverage.

```
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        LONG-TERM REFORMS                              |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  1844 (Oct): Nativists win Congressional & local electoral seats.     |
|  1845 (Apr): Mandated police force ratio (1 officer per 150 taxpayers)|
|  1850: Establishment of unified Philadelphia Police District          |
|  1854: Consolidation Act unites Philadelphia City and County          |
+-----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

Public response to the riots was sharply divided. While broad swathes of Philadelphia's populace expressed horror at the arson and chaos, nativists successfully converted anti-immigrant fervor into electoral power. In the October 1844 elections, which saw the highest voter turnout in Philadelphia's history to that point, American Republican candidates swept local offices, and Lewis Levin was elected to the United States House of Representatives alongside a fellow nativist colleague. Concurrently, over two thousand prominent citizens signed a formal petition endorsing General Cadwalader and praising the militia’s use of legal force to restore peace.

The obvious inability of local township watchmen and volunteer fire companies to quell large-scale rioting exposed major structural defects in the administration of Philadelphia County, which was then divided into several independent districts and townships. In April 1845, the Pennsylvania State Legislature enacted a law requiring every major municipality and district in Philadelphia County to maintain a professional police force consisting of at least one officer per 150 taxable residents. 

In 1850, the legislature established a unified Philadelphia Police District to cover the entire metropolitan area, bringing suburbs like Kensington and Southwark under centralized law enforcement authority. These law enforcement reforms served as an essential stepping stone toward the municipal Consolidation Act of 1854, which dissolved the independent county districts and merged all of Philadelphia County into a single, unified city government.

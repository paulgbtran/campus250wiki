## 2. Lead section

**Sadie Tanner Mossell Alexander** (January 2, 1898 – November 1, 1989) was a trailblazing American economist, attorney, civil rights pioneer, and public servant based in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. She achieved landmark historical firsts across multiple professions, earning national distinction as the first African-American woman in the United States to receive a Ph.D. in economics (1921), the first African-American woman to graduate from the University of Pennsylvania Law School (1927), and the first African-American woman admitted to practice law in the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania.

Born into one of Philadelphia's most prominent intellectual African-American families, Alexander dedicated her life to expanding economic opportunity and constitutional rights for Black Americans. Her early academic scholarship focused on the living conditions, wages, and expenditure patterns of Black migrant families during the Great Migration. Facing systemic racial and gender barriers in academic economics, she transitioned to the field of law, establishing a legal practice in Philadelphia alongside her husband, attorney and future judge Raymond Pace Alexander. Together, they mounted successful legal challenges against racial discrimination in public accommodations, employment, housing, and government institutions.

Throughout her career, Alexander served in significant local, state, and national policy roles. In 1946, President Harry S. Truman appointed her to the President's Committee on Civil Rights, where she helped draft the landmark report *To Secure These Rights* (1947), which laid the foundation for federal desegregation policy, anti-lynching legislation, and civil rights reform. She later served as the Assistant City Solicitor for Philadelphia and was appointed to leadership roles under the administrations of Presidents John F. Kennedy and Jimmy Carter.

Alexander was also a devoted civic leader, serving as the first National President of Delta Sigma Theta Sorority from 1919 to 1923 and leading the Philadelphia Commission on Human Relations from 1952 to 1968. Her pioneering work at the intersection of economics, law, and civil rights established her as a foundational figure in 20th-century African-American history and Philadelphia history.

## 3. Early life and education

Sadie Tanner Mossell was born in Philadelphia on January 2, 1898, into a distinguished African-American family with a long legacy of academic excellence, public service, and religious leadership. Her father, Aaron Albert Mossell II, was the first African American to graduate from the University of Pennsylvania Law School in 1888. Her mother, Mary Tanner Mossell, belonged to the prominent Tanner family; Sadie's maternal grandfather was Benjamin Tucker Tanner, a bishop in the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church and editor of the *Christian Recorder*, while her uncle was the internationally renowned painter Henry Ossawa Tanner. Her paternal uncle, Dr. Nathan Francis Mossell, was the first African-American graduate of the University of Pennsylvania School of Medicine and co-founded the Frederick Douglass Memorial Hospital and Training School in Philadelphia.

Following her father's departure from the family home during her early childhood, Mossell spent her youth moving between Philadelphia and Washington, D.C., where she attended the prestigious M Street High School (later renamed Dunbar High School). Returning to Philadelphia for higher education, she enrolled at the University of Pennsylvania in 1915. Despite experiencing severe racial isolation and social segregation on campus—where white students and faculty frequently ignored or excluded her—she excelled academically. She earned a Bachelor of Science in Education in 1918 and a Master of Arts in Economics in 1919.

Mossell continued her graduate studies at the University of Pennsylvania under a Francis Sergeant Pepper Fellowship. In June 1921, she made history by becoming the first African-American woman in the United States to earn a Ph.D. in economics. Her doctoral dissertation, titled *The Standard of Living Among One Hundred Negro Migrant Families in Philadelphia*, examined the economic reality of African-American working-class families who had migrated from the rural South to Philadelphia during World War I. Her groundbreaking research analyzed how real wages, inflation, housing discrimination, and urban industrial shifts impacted household budgets, establishing early standards for empirical economic research on Black working-class urban communities.

Despite her doctoral credential, pervasive racial and gender discrimination prevented academic institutions and private enterprises from hiring an African-American woman economist. Determined to pursue public advocacy and economic justice through legal avenues, Mossell entered the University of Pennsylvania Law School in 1924. In 1927, she became the school's first African-American female graduate and subsequently became the first Black woman admitted to the Pennsylvania state bar.

## 4. Career

### Early Economic Work and Transition to Law
Immediately following her doctoral graduation in 1921, Alexander sought employment as an economist. However, university economics departments and corporate research organizations refused to hire her because of her race and gender. From 1921 to 1923, she worked as an actuary for the North Carolina Mutual Life Insurance Company in Durham, North Carolina, one of the nation's premier Black-owned financial enterprises. 

Upon returning to Philadelphia and earning her law degree in 1927, Alexander joined her husband, Raymond Pace Alexander, in establishing the law firm of Alexander & Alexander. The firm became a cornerstone of civil rights litigation in Pennsylvania. Alexander specialized in estate planning, family law, probate, and civil rights cases, providing high-quality legal representation to African-American residents who were routinely denied service by white legal practices.

### Civil Rights Litigation and Municipal Service
During the late 1920s and 1930s, Sadie Tanner Mossell Alexander and her husband engaged in active litigation to enforce equal protection under the law. They successfully challenged illegal racial segregation in Philadelphia restaurants, hotels, movie theaters, and public recreational facilities. In 1936, Alexander was appointed Assistant City Solicitor for the City of Philadelphia, making her one of the first Black women in the nation to hold a municipal legal office.

Alexander was a firm advocate for full employment and federal intervention in the labor market. Combining her background in economics with her legal knowledge, she argued that true civil rights could not be achieved without economic security, fair employment practices, and equal access to capital and housing.

### Presidential Appointments and National Policy
In December 1946, President Harry S. Truman appointed Alexander to the Executive Order 9808 President's Committee on Civil Rights. She was one of two African Americans named to the 15-member national commission, alongside Dr. Channing H. Tobias. 

The committee's seminal 1947 report, *To Secure These Rights*, served as the foundational blueprint for the mid-century Civil Rights Movement. Alexander was instrumental in drafting recommendations that called for federal legislation to end lynching, the abolition of poll taxes, the protection of voting rights, the desegregation of the armed forces, and the establishment of the Fair Employment Practice Committee (FEPC).

In later decades, Alexander continued her national public service. President John F. Kennedy appointed her to the Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights Under Law in 1963. In 1979, President Jimmy Carter appointed her to chair the White House Conference on Aging, where she advocated for economic security, healthcare access, and social safety nets for elderly Americans.

### Organizational Leadership and Human Relations
Alexander held extensive leadership roles in major civic and professional organizations. From 1919 to 1923, she served as the first National President of Delta Sigma Theta Sorority, establishing its national administrative structure and chartering dozens of collegiate and alumnae chapters nationwide. 

From 1952 to 1968, she served on the Philadelphia Commission on Human Relations, leading the agency as its chair for several years. In this capacity, she oversaw citywide efforts to enforce non-discrimination ordinances in public housing, municipal employment, and commercial development.

## 5. Personal life

On November 29, 1923, Sadie Tanner Mossell married Raymond Pace Alexander (1897–1974), a fellow University of Pennsylvania Law School graduate who went on to become a distinguished civil rights attorney, Philadelphia City Council member, and the first African American appointed to the Pennsylvania Court of Common Pleas. The couple formed a lifelong professional and personal partnership, working jointly on landmark legal cases and civic campaigns throughout Greater Philadelphia.

The Alexanders had two daughters: Mary Elizabeth Alexander Eddleman (1934–2017) and Dr. Rae Alexander-Minter. The family resided in West Philadelphia and later in Mount Airy, remaining active members of the African Methodist Episcopal Church and key figures in Philadelphia's political and cultural life.

Following the death of her husband in 1974, Alexander remained active in legal and civic affairs until her health declined in the 1980s due to Alzheimer's disease. She died on November 1, 1989, at a retirement facility in Philadelphia at the age of 91. She was buried alongside her husband at West Laurel Hill Cemetery in Bala Cynwyd, Pennsylvania.

## 6. Legacy

Sadie Tanner Mossell Alexander left a profound legacy as a pioneer in economics, law, and civil rights advocacy. Her career broken open doors historically closed to African Americans and women, establishing a precedent for intersectional advocacy that linked economic policy directly to constitutional protections.

Her academic contributions to economics, long overlooked by mainstream 20th-century economic associations, have been widely reassessed by modern scholars. Organizations such as the National Economic Association (NEA) and **The Sadie Collective**—a non-profit organization founded in 2018 to increase the representation of Black women in economics, finance, and policy—formally recognize Alexander as a founding matriarch of Black political economy.

In Philadelphia, her memory is preserved through public institutions and academic honors:
* **Sadie Tanner Mossell Alexander University Partnership School**: Opened in 2001 in West Philadelphia, this public university-assisted school (commonly known as the Penn Alexander School) was established through a partnership between the School District of Philadelphia and the University of Pennsylvania.
* **University of Pennsylvania Endowments**: Penn Law established the Sadie T.M. Alexander Chair in Law, along with civil rights scholarships and student organizations named in her honor.
* **Historical Markers**: Pennsylvania Historical and Museum Commission markers commemorate her former residence and contributions to state legal history.

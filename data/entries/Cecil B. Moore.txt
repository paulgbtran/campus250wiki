### Lead section

**Cecil B. Moore** was an influential American attorney, civil rights leader, and political activist whose bold, militant approach to direct action transformed the civil rights movement in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Serving as the president of the Philadelphia Branch of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) during the 1960s, Moore broke away from traditional, cautious civil rights strategies in favor of aggressive litigation, mass marches, and daily protest pickets that directly confronted institutional racism, employment discrimination, and urban segregation.

Moore is best remembered for leading the prolonged and historic campaign to desegregate Girard College, a boarding school for "poor white male orphans" located behind an imposing ten-foot-high, fourteen-inch-thick wall in North Philadelphia. Established in 1848 through the bequest of wealthy 19th-century philanthropist Stephen Girard, the institution maintained strict racial segregation for over a century, despite being situated in the heart of an expanding, predominantly Black neighborhood. Recognizing the school as a glaring symbol of racial exclusion and public discrimination, Moore orchestrated a combined strategy of relentless grassroots demonstrations outside its perimeter walls and decisive legal challenges in federal court.

His leadership in the Girard College fight culminated in a watershed civil rights victory in 1968, when federal court rulings holding that the school's discriminatory policy violated the Fourteenth Amendment were finalized on May 20, 1968, after the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal. This forced the institution to open its doors to four Black male students on September 11, 1968, breaking down one of the region's most prominent institutional bastions of racial exclusion.

Through his legal practice in North Philadelphia and his unyielding advocacy on behalf of working-class African Americans, Moore permanently redefined municipal politics and civil rights strategy in Greater Philadelphia. Often described as the voice of working-class African Americans who felt ignored by established municipal elites, Moore's fierce independence and uncompromising stance left an indelible mark on the urban history and legal landscape of Pennsylvania.

### Early life and education

Cecil B. Moore was born in West Virginia in 1915 and served as a sergeant in the United States Marine Corps during World War II. After earning his undergraduate degree from Bluefield State College and completing his legal education at Temple University School of Law in 1953, Moore established a private practice as a defense attorney in North Philadelphia, quickly building a reputation as a ferocious, skillful advocate in the courtroom. Unlike many established attorneys of his era who aligned closely with municipal elites, Moore focused his practice on serving working-class African American residents, criminal defendants, and victims of police harassment or systemic neglect.

Practicing in an era marked by deep-seated institutional barriers, Moore utilized his legal knowledge to challenge discrimination across civic, judicial, and municipal systems. His experience representing residents in North Philadelphia provided him with a firsthand understanding of the economic disenfranchisement, housing inequality, and educational exclusion affecting Black Philadelphians in the post-World War II decade. This grounded legal work served as the foundation for his broader political activism and prepared him to lead major civil rights initiatives across the city.

### Career

#### Leadership of the Philadelphia NAACP

In the early 1960s, Cecil B. Moore assumed the presidency of the Philadelphia Branch of the NAACP. Under his leadership, the organization underwent a radical transformation. Moore rejected the quiet, diplomatic negotiations favored by older establishment civil rights figures, believing that tangible progress required direct confrontation, public exposure of racial hypocrisy, and unyielding pressure on local institutions. 

Moore mobilized working-class Black residents, youth, and neighborhood activists, building a massive, politically active base in North Philadelphia. Under his tenure, the local branch targeted discrimination in trade unions, public construction projects, housing, and commercial businesses across the city. His combative style frequently brought him into friction with both city leaders and traditional civil rights organizations, but it galvanized popular support among community members who sought immediate structural change.

#### The Girard College Desegregation Campaign

The defining effort of Moore’s career was the campaign to desegregate Girard College. The boarding school had been created under the 1831 will of French-born merchant and banker Stephen Girard, who left $2 million and specific instructions to establish a free boarding school restricted exclusively to "poor white male orphans." Opening in 1848, the campus was enclosed by a ten-foot-high stone wall in North Philadelphia. By the mid-20th century, the neighborhood surrounding the school had transitioned into a predominantly Black community, making the walled, racially restricted campus a stark and painful symbol of exclusion.

Earlier attempts to integrate the school—including legal efforts by Black physician Dr. Nathan Mossell in 1891, free legal aid offered by *Philadelphia Tribune* editor E. Washington Rhodes in 1953, and city council resolutions introduced by attorney Raymond Pace Alexander—had been met with firm resistance. Following the 1954 *Brown v. Board of Education* decision, Alexander and civic leaders argued that because the school was managed by the Board of Directors of City Trusts (a municipal body), its segregation violated the Fourteenth Amendment. After the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in 1957 (*Pennsylvania v. Board of Directors of City Trusts*) that the municipal board could not enforce racial segregation, the Philadelphia Orphans' Court removed the public board and appointed private trustees to evade public non-discrimination mandates.

#### Mass Protests and Legal Victory

Unwilling to let the private board structure stall integration, Moore launched a campaign combining legal action with persistent direct-action protests. Beginning in May 1965, Moore led months of daily picket lines outside the high stone walls of Girard College. Mobilizing thousands of demonstrators, activists, and local residents, Moore kept national media attention focused on the school. The protests attracted national civil rights figures, including Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., who visited Philadelphia in August 1965 to speak to thousands of demonstrators rallied outside the school’s gates.

Simultaneously, legal actions were filed against the trust administrators to challenge the legality of the exclusion under federal law. The dual strategy of public agitation and relentless court filings pressed the federal judiciary to address the broader implications of racially discriminatory private trusts managed with public ties.

On March 7, 1968, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit ruled that excluding Black students from Girard College violated the Fourteenth Amendment. On May 20, 1968, the U.S. Supreme Court denied certiorari, declining to hear an appeal and letting the lower court's ruling stand. The legal victory forced the institution to integrate. On September 11, 1968, four Black male students enrolled at Girard College, effectively ending more than a century of racial exclusion at the historic institution.

### Personal life

Cecil B. Moore was known for his commanding personal presence, sharp intellect, and unapologetic rhetorical style. A formidable presence in North Philadelphia, he was deeply connected to the everyday lives of the working-class residents he represented. Moore eschewed the polish of conventional politicians, preferring direct, forceful language that resonated with ordinary citizens. His legal office in North Philadelphia served not only as a firm for criminal and civil cases but also as an informal headquarters for grassroots civil rights strategies, political organizing, and community support.

---

### Legacy

Cecil B. Moore’s impact on Philadelphia’s civil rights history and political development remains visible across the city. By demonstrating that grassroots direct action coupled with aggressive legal strategy could dismantle entrenched segregation, Moore provided a blueprint for northern civil rights activism during the 1960s and beyond.

His successful campaign against Girard College changed the trajectory of the school, which expanded its admissions policies further in 1982 by admitting female students and later adjusting its single-parent household qualifications to serve a diverse population of needy students. The history of his movement and the broader Black community in Philadelphia has been commemorated in historical literature, such as Arthur C. Willis's *Cecil's City: A History of Blacks in Philadelphia 1636–1979*, and highlighted in documentary productions like *Cecil's People*. Today, Moore is remembered as a central figure in transforming North Philadelphia into a focal point of civil rights leadership, legal strategy, and community empowerment.

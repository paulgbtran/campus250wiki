### Lead section

**Cliveden**, also known as the **Chew House**, is an eighteenth-century Georgian country house and estate located in the Germantown neighborhood of Northwest Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Constructed between 1763 and 1767 as a summer retreat for Benjamin Chew—a prominent lawyer, legal scholar, and Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania—Cliveden stands as one of the most architecturally significant examples of colonial American domestic architecture. Situated on a sprawling suburban property along Germantown Avenue, the residence was built using native Wissahickon schist, reflecting both European classical proportions and regional Pennsylvania masonry traditions.

The house occupies a central place in the military history of the American Revolutionary War. On October 4, 1777, Cliveden served as a formidable defensive fortress during the Battle of Germantown. A British garrison comprising members of the 40th Regiment of Foot, commanded by Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Musgrave, barricaded themselves inside the thick stone walls of the mansion to withstand a surprise offensive launched by General George Washington's Continental Army. The unsuccessful American assault on the heavily fortified structure resulted in heavy casualties and delayed the Continental advance, ultimately contributing to the retreat of Washington's forces and securing a tactical victory for the British army under Sir William Howe.

Beyond its military significance, Cliveden was the ancestral home of the influential Chew family for over two centuries. Across seven generations, the estate operated as a social and political hub for Philadelphia’s elite, while simultaneously serving as a complex site of labor that encompassed enslaved African Americans, indentured servants, and free hired workers. The estate’s surviving architectural features, grounds, and extensive archival record provide critical insights into colonial legal culture, transatlantic trade, landed wealth, and the institution of slavery in the Mid-Atlantic region.

Today, Cliveden is designated as a National Historic Landmark and is preserved as a historic site open to the public. Managed by Cliveden, Inc., in partnership with the National Trust for Historic Preservation, the property functions as a museum and community space. Its interpretive programming explores the Revolutionary War battle, the architectural history of the estate, and the nuanced stories of the diverse individuals—both free and unfree—who lived and worked on the grounds across two hundred years.

### Origins and construction

The origin and design of Cliveden are deeply intertwined with the early life, education, and professional advancement of its founder, Benjamin Chew (1722–1810). Born in Anne Arundel County, Maryland, into a wealthy Quaker family with substantial landholdings, Chew received a rigorous classical education before pursuing formal legal training. In the early 1740s, he traveled to London to study law at the Honorable Society of the Middle Temple, one of the prestigious Inns of Court. There, he immersed himself in English common law, classical literature, and European architectural treatises, gaining an appreciation for British high culture and Palladian architectural principles that would later dictate the design of his Philadelphia estate.

Upon returning to North America, Chew settled in New Castle, Delaware, and later moved his primary legal practice to Philadelphia in 1754. His elite legal training, combined with mentorship from figures such as Andrew Hamilton, accelerated his rise within Pennsylvania's proprietary government under the Penn family. As Chew accumulated legal fees, landholdings, and political appointments—serving as Attorney General and later Chief Justice—he sought to build a country seat that would mirror the rural estates of the British gentry and provide an escape from Philadelphia’s seasonal yellow fever and summer heat.

In 1763, Chew acquired eleven acres of land along the main thoroughfare in Germantown, approximately six miles northwest of Philadelphia's urban center. Construction began that same year under the direction of master builder and carpenter Jacob Knor, alongside local stonemasons and craftsmen. Drawing inspiration from pattern books popular among wealthy British colonials, such as James Gibbs's *A Book of Architecture*, Chew personally oversaw much of the design process. The resulting house featured symmetrical five-bay facades, a pedimented central pavilion, urn-topped roof dormers, and finely cut ashlar masonry made from local quarried stone. The interior featured high ceilings, elaborate ornamental woodwork, classical cornices, and a grand central hallway suitable for high-society entertainments.

### History and Battle of Germantown

Throughout its long history, Cliveden functioned as a major nexus for colonial politics, legal administration, military conflict, and domestic agriculture. Completed in 1767, the estate served initially as the primary summer retreat for the Chew family and a showcase for Benjamin Chew’s social standing. During the decade preceding the American Revolution, Cliveden hosted Philadelphia's top political elites, including members of the Continental Congress, provincial governors, and legal scholars, serving as an informal forum for high-level political discourse during a period of escalating tensions between the American colonies and the British Crown.

The most dramatic chapter in Cliveden’s career occurred on the morning of October 4, 1777, during the Battle of Germantown. Following the British occupation of Philadelphia, George Washington launched a complex, four-column offensive to surprise Sir William Howe’s forces stationed in Germantown. As the Continental troops pushed down the Germantown Turnpike under heavy fog, Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Musgrave and approximately 120 soldiers of the British 40th Regiment of Foot barricaded themselves inside the thick stone walls of Cliveden.

When the main American force encountered this improvised stronghold, Washington convened a quick council of war. Brigadier General Henry Knox advocated for reducing the fortress before continuing the advance, adhering to traditional military doctrine that an army should not leave a fortified enemy post in its rear. Continental artillery opened fire on the mansion, but the heavy Wissahickon schist walls withstood the bombardment with minor structural damage. Repeated infantry assaults led by American forces were repulsed with heavy losses by British musket fire from the upper-story windows. The prolonged engagement at Cliveden absorbed critical time, created tactical confusion amid the dense fog, and allowed Howe to organize a counterattack that ultimately forced the Continental Army to retreat.

Following the battle, the house suffered extensive damage, including shattered windows, charred woodwork, pitted stonework, and structural breaches. Benjamin Chew, who had been placed under house arrest in New Jersey by revolutionary authorities due to his neutral political stance, temporarily sold the damaged property to Blair McClenachan in 1779. However, Chew repurchased Cliveden in 1797 and oversaw extensive repairs, preserving the damaged physical fabric as an explicit testament to the historical events that had unfolded on the property.

### Domestic life and labor

Domestic life at Cliveden reflected the social dynamics, domestic customs, and labor systems of elite eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Philadelphia families. Benjamin Chew married twice—first to Mary Macfunn, and following her death, to Elizabeth Osborne—fathering a total of fourteen children. The extended Chew family inhabited Cliveden alongside a varied domestic staff. The mansion was designed to accommodate lavish entertainment, hosting prominent social figures such as George and Martha Washington, John Adams, and John André, the British officer who frequented Germantown during the 1777–1778 British occupation.

The domestic operations of Cliveden relied heavily on a labor force composed of enslaved African Americans, indentured servants, and hired day laborers. Historically, Benjamin Chew was one of the largest enslavers in the region, holding dozens of men, women, and children across his urban residence in Philadelphia, his rural properties at Cliveden, and his agricultural plantations in Delaware and Maryland. Enslaved individuals at Cliveden performed demanding tasks including fine carpentry, stone maintenance, gardening, cooking, laundering, and personal service for the Chew family.

Archival records preserved in the Chew Family Papers illuminate the lives of unfree workers who lived on the property. Enslaved individuals such as Richard, along with generations of domestic workers, navigated the complexities of servitude within a region gradually adopting post-revolutionary gradual abolition laws. Over time, as Pennsylvania enacted the Gradual Abolition Act of 1780, the labor force at Cliveden transitioned from chattel slavery to indentured contracts and paid wage labor, though economic dependence and social stratification persisted.

Throughout the nineteenth century, successive generations of the Chew family maintained Cliveden as a multi-generational family home. Family members preserved original furnishings, family portraits, decorative arts, and personal correspondence, cultivating a domestic museum within their own living space that commemorated both their ancestry and the home's role in the birth of the republic.

### Legacy

Cliveden’s historical, architectural, and military legacy remains significant in Philadelphia and American history. The site was formally recognized for its historical importance in the twentieth century, receiving designation as a National Historic Landmark in 1961 and being listed on the National Register of Historic Places. In 1972, the Chew family transferred ownership of the historic residence and its remaining six-acre grounds to the National Trust for Historic Preservation, ensuring its permanent conservation and public accessibility.

```
1961: Designated National Historic Landmark
  │
1972: Transferred by Chew Family to National Trust for Historic Preservation
  │
2000s: Excavations & Archival Projects (Chew Family Papers cataloged at HSP)
  │
Present: Public Museum & Venue for Annual Battle of Germantown Reenactment
```

In the decades following its public opening, Cliveden’s management shifted from traditional military narratives toward a holistic educational approach. While continuing to host the annual Battle of Germantown reenactment—a major community event that draws thousands of visitors each autumn—the museum has integrated historical research regarding slavery, freedom, and domestic labor. Initiatives such as the *Cliveden Diversity & Slavery Initiative* utilize the vast Chew Family Papers (housed at the Historical Society of Pennsylvania) to highlight the lived experiences of enslaved people, indentured laborers, and free workers alongside Revolutionary War histories.

The physical fabric of Cliveden remains remarkably well preserved. Visitors to the site can examine original furniture, decorative arts, and architectural details, including lingering physical scars from musket balls and artillery round shot sustained during the 1777 battle. As an architectural masterpiece, a battlefield monument, and a site of African American historical memory, Cliveden serves as a lens for examining the social, political, and military forces that shaped early American history.

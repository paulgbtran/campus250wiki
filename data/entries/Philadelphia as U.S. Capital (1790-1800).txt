## 2. Lead section

From 1790 to 1800, Philadelphia served as the temporary capital of the United States. Following the ratification of the United States Constitution, which had been drafted in Philadelphia in 1787, federal leaders debated the permanent location of the national seat of government. Under the provisions of the Residence Act of 1790, Congress designated Philadelphia as the temporary capital for a ten-year transitional period while a permanent federal city was constructed along the Potomac River in what became the District of Columbia. During this decade, Philadelphia emerged as the political, commercial, and financial epicenter of the young republic.

The executive and legislative branches operated out of existing and adapted structures in Center City Philadelphia. President George Washington and later President John Adams occupied the President's House, a large mansion at Sixth and Market Streets leased from financier Robert Morris. The United States Congress convened in Congress Hall, a newly constructed building located at Chestnut and Sixth Streets that was originally designed as the Philadelphia County Courthouse. The U.S. Supreme Court and lower federal agencies also established their headquarters nearby, making the city the nerve center of American governance.

During Philadelphia's tenure as the capital, the federal government enacted foundational legislation and established core democratic traditions. The Bill of Rights was ratified by the states in 1791, Congress admitted Vermont, Kentucky, and Tennessee to the Union, and passed major statutes including the Naturalization Acts, the Fugitive Slave Act of 1793, and the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798. The period was marked by the nation's first peaceful transition of presidential power, when John Adams was inaugurated at Congress Hall in 1797, following Washington's second inauguration at the same location in 1793. 

The decade was also defined by social complexity and public health crises. Philadelphia was the most populous urban center in the nation and a magnet for portrait artists, publishers, and financial enterprise. It was simultaneously a center of early African American community building—led by figures such as Absalom Jones, Richard Allen, and James Forten—and a site of deep constitutional tension surrounding legal slavery. Because Pennsylvania had enacted a gradual abolition law, federal slaveholders, including George Washington, actively maneuvered to prevent their enslaved domestic workers from securing legal freedom. The yellow fever epidemic of 1793 further disrupted governance and altered the city's trajectory. In late 1800, the federal government officially relocated to Washington, D.C., concluding a transformative era in Philadelphia's history.

## 3. Background

The selection of Philadelphia as the nation's temporary capital emerged from a contentious political compromise over national finance and state debt. Following the adoption of the U.S. Constitution in 1787, the First United States Congress initially convened in New York City in 1789. However, Southern congressmen expressed misgivings about placing the permanent seat of power in a Northern commercial hub, while Northern representatives resisted placing the capital entirely in the agrarian South. 

The impasse was resolved through the Compromise of 1790, engineered by Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton, Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson, and Representative James Madison. In exchange for Southern support for Hamilton's financial plan—which assumed state revolutionary debts into a unified federal debt—Northern leaders agreed to locate the permanent capital along the Potomac River. To accommodate the transition, Congress passed the Residence Act in July 1790, designating Philadelphia as the seat of the federal government for ten years, from December 1790 until the first Monday in December 1800, when the government would permanently move to the newly designated federal territory.

Philadelphians welcomed the decision and harbored strong hopes that the federal government could be persuaded to remain permanently. To demonstrate the city’s suitability, municipal and state authorities prepared premier accommodations for federal officials. The city offered the newly completed Philadelphia County Courthouse at Sixth and Chestnut Streets—constructed between 1787 and 1789—to serve as Congress Hall. 

For the executive branch, wealthy merchant and federal financier Robert Morris offered his own grand residence at Sixth and Market Streets to President Washington. Originally built in the late 1760s by Mary Lawrence Masters and later occupied by British General Sir William Howe and Continental Major-General Benedict Arnold, the mansion had been purchased and extensively rebuilt by Morris following a major fire in 1780. Additionally, local boosters began constructing an opulent presidential mansion on Ninth Street in an effort to entice the presidency to stay in Philadelphia. However, Washington disavowed the grand Ninth Street building—arranging to be absent on the day of its groundbreaking—and quietly worked behind the scenes to ensure the ultimate fulfillment of the Potomac capital plan.

## 4. Course of events

### The Executive Branch at the President's House
George Washington occupied the President's House on Market Street from November 1790 until the end of his second term in March 1797. Upon leasing the property, Washington directed several structural additions to adapt the home to federal executive functions. He commissioned a two-story bow window addition on the south side of the main house to enlarge the primary reception rooms to thirty-four feet. This space served as the backdrop for official presidential receptions and levees. He also expanded the stable facilities, constructed a servants' hall, and converted a second-floor bathing room into his private office, which functioned as the nation's early Cabinet room.

The presidential household comprised up to thirty individuals, including George and Martha Washington, family members, executive secretaries, free white indentured servants, and nine enslaved Africans brought from Washington's Mount Vernon estate: Oney Judge, Austin, Moll, Giles, Paris, Christopher Sheels, Hercules, Richmond, and Joe. 

The presence of enslaved individuals in the capital highlighted acute legal and moral contradictions. Pennsylvania had enacted the Gradual Abolition Law in 1780, which freed children born to enslaved mothers and allowed non-resident slaveholders to keep enslaved workers in the state for no more than six continuous months before they automatically attained legal freedom. To circumvent this legal restriction without freeing his enslaved workers, Washington regularly rotated them out of Pennsylvania to Virginia or New Jersey before they reached six continuous months of residence, thereby resetting their residency clock. Despite these precautions, Oney Judge, Martha Washington's enslaved personal attendant, escaped to freedom directly from the Market Street house on May 21, 1796, with the assistance of Philadelphia's free Black network.

John Adams assumed the presidency in March 1797 and occupied the President's House until June 1800. Unlike Washington, Adams was not a slaveholder. His administration conducted executive business and hosted public functions from the Market Street residence throughout his term, until the executive offices prepared for their final move south.

### Legislative Activities in Congress Hall
Congress Hall served as the meeting place for the United States Congress throughout the decade. The House of Representatives occupied the broad first-floor chamber, decorated with mahogany desks, studded leather chairs, dark green window valances, and small sand-filled spitting boxes near the fireplaces. The U.S. Senate met on the upper floor in a more formal chamber featuring an oval ceiling medallion with a sunburst design of thirteen stars, an eagle fresco holding an olive branch, and a custom carpet woven by William Sprague of Philadelphia depicting patriotic emblems and state shields.

During its ten years in Congress Hall, the federal legislature completed foundational national business:
* **Constitutional Rights and Statehood:** In 1791, the states officially ratified the Bill of Rights while Congress convened in the building. Congress also oversaw the expansion of the Union, formally admitting Vermont (1791), Kentucky (1792), and Tennessee (1796).
* **Major Legislation:** Congress debated and enacted laws including the Naturalization Acts, the Fugitive Slave Act of 1793, and the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798 during periods of heightened foreign tension such as the Quasi-War with France.
* **Presidential Transitions:** George Washington took his presidential oath of office for his second term in the Senate chamber on March 4, 1793. On March 4, 1797, Congress Hall hosted the inauguration of John Adams in the House of Representatives chamber, establishing the democratic tradition of a peaceful transfer of power between administrations.

### Social, Economic, and Cultural Dynamics
Philadelphia in the 1790s was the most populous urban center in North America, encompassing the city proper and built-up contiguous suburbs such as Southwark and Northern Liberties. The presence of the national government catalyzed economic growth and infrastructure development, including the construction of the Lancaster Turnpike between 1793 and 1795 to link the capital to interior commerce.

The city became a vibrant cultural center. Artists, notably Charles Willson Peale and members of the Peale family, established museums and portrait galleries to document political figures. Philadelphia’s printing and publishing industry expanded rapidly, fueling national political debate through newspapers and pamphlets. Simultaneously, Philadelphia became a vital center for free African American leadership. Community leaders such as Absalom Jones, Richard Allen, and James Forten founded independent churches, mutual aid societies, and civic organizations that advocated for abolition and equal rights.

The capital was severely disrupted in the late summer and autumn of 1793 by a devastating yellow fever epidemic. The outbreak claimed more than 5,000 lives—roughly 10 percent of the city's population—and caused President Washington, his Cabinet, and thousands of residents to flee to surrounding rural areas such as Germantown and Grays Ferry. Recurring yellow fever outbreaks throughout the remainder of the decade damaged Philadelphia's reputation as a safe urban site and eliminated any lingering political support for keeping the permanent capital in Pennsylvania.

## 5. Aftermath

In accordance with the Residence Act, the federal government vacated Philadelphia in 1800. President John Adams left the city in May 1800, spent the summer months at his home in Massachusetts, and moved into the newly constructed, unfinished White House in Washington, D.C., on November 1, 1800. Executive departments packed official archives and records onto vessels and wagons for shipment south, and Congress officially convened its first session in the new capital city in December 1800.

Following the departure of the federal government, the buildings that housed the capital transitioned back to municipal and commercial uses:
* **Congress Hall:** The building reverted to its original intended purpose as the Philadelphia County Courthouse, serving in that capacity throughout much of the nineteenth century before being incorporated into Independence National Historical Park for historical restoration.
* **The President's House:** Robert Morris’s Market Street residence was converted into Francis's Union Hotel in late 1800. After the hotel failed, the building was subdivided for retail shops and boardinghouses. In 1832, the main structure was gutted to erect three narrow commercial storefronts within its footprint. The remaining outer side walls were pulled down during urban clearance in 1951 to establish Independence Mall.

In the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, public interest in the site of the President's House resurfaced. Archaeological investigations in 2000 and 2007 uncovered the structural foundations of the mansion, including the icehouse pit, kitchen ell, underground passages, and the footings of Washington’s two-story bow window. 

Public activism highlighted the history of the nine enslaved individuals who lived and labored on the site. In response, a major public commemoration project was commissioned by the city and the National Park Service. Designed by the architectural firm Kelly-Maiello, *The President's House: Freedom and Slavery in the Making of a New Nation* opened to the public in 2010 directly adjacent to the Liberty Bell Center, preserving the exposed archaeological footings and structural layout of the executive mansion while interpreting the dual history of early American democracy and chattel slavery.

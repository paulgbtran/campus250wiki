## Lead section

The **founding of Philadelphia** in 1682 by English Quaker proprietor William Penn (1644–1718) established the capital of the Province of Pennsylvania. Designed as a planned urban settlement situated between the Delaware and Schuylkill Rivers, Philadelphia was conceived by Penn as a "Holy Experiment"—a colony anchored in principles of religious liberty, freedom of conscience, pacifism, and representative governance. Central to the initial founding period was Penn's diplomatic engagement with the region's indigenous inhabitants, the Lenni Lenape, which established decades of peaceful coexistence unusual among early European colonies in North America.

According to Pennsylvania's most longstanding historical tradition, shortly after Penn arrived in the colony in late October 1682, he convened a grand diplomatic meeting with Lenape leaders in the riverfront town of Shackamaxon (located in present-day Fishtown). Beneath the canopy of a prominent elm tree ("the Great Elm"), Penn and the Lenape exchanged pledges of perpetual peace, friendship, and fair trade. Known as the Treaty of Shackamaxon or "William Penn's Treaty with the Indians," this event became the foundational narrative of colonial Philadelphia, representing a commitment to peaceful coexistence without the requirement of formal military alliances or religious oaths.

Although original paper records from the autumn of 1682 detailing a formal treaty document have not survived—leading historians to evaluate whether the legend consolidates multiple early diplomatic councils, such as a documented June 1683 land treaty with the Lenape chieftain Tamanend—the diplomatic policy enacted during Philadelphia's founding established unprecedented peace along the Pennsylvania frontier. In subsequent decades, Native American speakers and colonial authorities frequently invoked the original spirit of Penn's alliance during treaty negotiations.

The visual and cultural reputation of Philadelphia's founding spread worldwide during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The French philosopher Voltaire famously praised Penn's agreement as the only treaty between European Christians and Native Americans that was never sworn to by oath and never broken. Immortalized in Benjamin West's 1771 canvas *Penn's Treaty with the Indians* and adapted in Edward Hicks's *Peaceable Kingdom* paintings, the founding of Philadelphia remains a central pillar of Pennsylvania's historical identity, commemorated today at Penn Treaty Park.

## Background

In March 1681, King Charles II of England granted William Penn a massive proprietary land charter in North America to settle a debt owed to Penn's deceased father, Admiral Sir William Penn. As a devout member of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers), the younger Penn sought to establish a colonial haven where persecuted religious dissenters could live and worship freely. Penn envisioned his province of Pennsylvania as a "Holy Experiment," governed by constitutional charters that guaranteed freedom of conscience, civil liberties, and democratic participation.

Unlike many contemporary colonial proprietors who claimed territory solely through royal prerogative or military conquest, Penn recognized the prior land rights of the indigenous Lenni Lenape people. Before setting sail for North America, Penn sent advance instructions to his deputy governor, William Markham, and appointed commissioners to inspect potential settlement sites, survey boundary lines, and establish friendly communications with local Native leaders. Penn sent personal letters to the Lenape, expressing his desire to maintain an honest, peaceable, and mutually beneficial relationship.

The site chosen for the provincial capital was a neck of land situated between the Delaware and Schuylkill Rivers. Penn named the city Philadelphia, deriving from the Greek words for "brotherly love" (*phileo* and *adelphos*), underscoring his vision for a harmonious civic community. Long before Penn's survey, the Delaware Valley was occupied by the Lenape, who maintained seasonal encampments and agricultural plots along the riverways. European interaction in the area had been limited to small Swedish and Dutch settlements under the former New Sweden and New Netherland jurisdictions.

Penn departed England aboard the ship *Welcome* in late summer 1682 and arrived in North America in late October 1682. Upon landing at New Castle (in present-day Delaware) and subsequently traveling up the Delaware River, Penn began organizing the civil administration of Pennsylvania and personally overseeing the layout of Philadelphia, while seeking direct contact with the Lenape leaders whose lands surrounded the new town.

## Course of events

Upon his arrival in late October 1682, William Penn initiated direct negotiations with local Lenni Lenape leaders. Tradition holds that Penn traveled north along the Delaware River to Shackamaxon, an established indigenous council ground in what is now the Fishtown neighborhood of Philadelphia. There, beneath a towering elm tree along the waterfront, Penn met with Lenape chiefs to formally establish relations between the European settlers and the Native population.

During the gathering at Shackamaxon, Penn and the Lenape leaders pledged to dwell together in peace and friendship "as long as the creeks and rivers run, and while the sun, moon, and stars endure." In accordance with Quaker testimony against taking formal, legalistic oaths, Penn did not formalize the pact with religious oaths. Instead, the parties ratified their friendship through spoken promises, ceremonial gifts, and the exchange of traditional wampum belts. The Lenape presented Penn with a broad wampum belt woven with white whelk and dark quahog shell beads depicting two figures holding hands, symbolizing unity and harmony between the native inhabitants and the Quaker colonists.

While the tradition of the 1682 Treaty of Shackamaxon became deeply entrenched in popular memory, 19th- and 20th-century historians noted that no original written treaty manuscript dated to late October or November 1682 exists in official provincial records. Given Penn's documented, densely packed administrative schedule during his first months in the colony, some scholars suggest that the popular tradition of the "Great Treaty" synthesizes several distinct diplomatic meetings and land purchases that occurred between late 1682 and mid-1683.

One key historical event underlying the treaty tradition occurred in June 1683, when Penn concluded a series of formal land purchase deeds with the Lenape leader Tamanend (often called Tammany) and other native leaders. This documented council, which may have taken place under the Great Elm at Shackamaxon, included solemn verbal promises of lasting friendship alongside explicit land transfers, providing the concrete historical basis for the legendary Treaty of Shackamaxon.

## Aftermath

The diplomatic policy established during the founding of Philadelphia secured an extended period of peace between Pennsylvania's colonial government and the Lenape nation, distinguishing Pennsylvania from other English colonies that suffered frequent Indian wars. Throughout the eighteenth century, Native American negotiators repeatedly referenced their foundational agreements with William Penn—whom they called "Brother Miquon" (meaning "quill" or "pen")—as the standard for fair dealing and honorable diplomacy.

The reputation of Philadelphia's founding gained international fame through Enlightenment literature and fine art. In his 1733 *Letters Concerning the English Nation*, French philosopher Voltaire wrote his celebrated praise of the agreement, stating it was "the only treaty between those people [the Indians] and the Christians that was not ratified by an oath, and was never infring'ed." Voltaire's commentary cemented Philadelphia's global reputation as a beacon of tolerance and pacifism.

In 1771–1772, artist Benjamin West (1738–1820) was commissioned by Penn’s son, Thomas Penn (1702–1775), to paint *William Penn's Treaty with the Indians When He Founded the Province of Pennsylvania in North America*. West’s grand visual composition depicted Penn, dressed in Quaker attire, surrounded by Lenape leaders and trade goods beneath the Great Elm at Shackamaxon. The painting became an iconic symbol of colonial American history and was widely reproduced in engravings. Quaker folk painter Edward Hicks (1780–1845) later adapted West's imagery in his famous *Penn's Treaty* and *Peaceable Kingdom* canvas series, further embedding the scene into American popular culture.

Physical artifacts and memorials connected to the event remain historically prominent. In 1857, Granville John Penn (a great-grandson of William Penn) donated the traditional hand-joining wampum belt to the Historical Society of Pennsylvania; it is preserved today in the collection of the Historical Society of Pennsylvania. Although the original Great Elm fell during a storm in 1810, the Penn Society erected a marble obelisk at the site in 1827—recognized as Philadelphia's first public monument. The monument bears the inscription: "TREATY GROUND of WILLIAM PENN and the INDIAN NATIVES 1682 UNBROKEN FAITH." The grounds are preserved today as Penn Treaty Park, located at 1341 North Delaware Avenue in Fishtown, commemorating the origin of Philadelphia.

### Lead section

Marian Anderson was an internationally acclaimed African American contralto and a landmark figure in the twentieth-century civil rights movement. Emerging during an era when severe racial discrimination restricted opportunities for Black musicians in the United States, Anderson built a groundbreaking career in classical music that spanned from the mid-1920s through the late 1950s and into the 1960s. Although she was not the first African American concert singer in the country, her extraordinary vocal talent and worldwide popularity superseded those of any predecessors, establishing her as one of the premier concert artists of her generation.

Throughout her rise to prominence, Anderson maintained deep roots in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. In 1924, Anderson purchased a rowhouse at 762 S. Martin Street using earnings from her fledgling musical career to house her family. This home served as Anderson’s primary residence, headquarters, and rehearsal space during the critical years when her national and international reputation solidified. Supported by her family, local African American churches, and community institutions, she overcame systemic racial barriers that barred Black performers from mainstream American music conservatories, symphony orchestras, and opera houses.

Anderson's career achieved historical significance not only through her musical achievements but also through her direct association with key moments in the struggle for civil rights. In 1939, after the Daughters of the American Revolution (DAR) barred her from performing at Constitution Hall in Washington, D.C., First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt resigned from the organization in protest. With the assistance of Mrs. Roosevelt and the U.S. Department of the Interior, Anderson delivered a historic open-air concert on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial on Easter Sunday, April 9, 1939, before an audience of over 75,000 spectators and millions of radio listeners nationwide.

In the decades following her Lincoln Memorial performance, Anderson continued to break racial precedents across the cultural and political landscape. In 1955, she became the first African American artist to perform as a soloist with the Metropolitan Opera in New York City. She served as a U.S. Department of State goodwill ambassador, was appointed by President Dwight D. Eisenhower as an alternate delegate to the General Assembly of the United Nations, and sang at the presidential inaugurations of both Eisenhower (1957) and John F. Kennedy (1961).

### Early life and education

At the turn of the twentieth century, aspiring African American concert musicians faced widespread institutional segregation and racial prejudice across the United States. Mainstream classical music institutions—including symphony orchestras, opera companies, and the vast majority of music conservatories—actively excluded Black students and performers. In her youth, Anderson experienced this systemic exclusion directly when she was denied admission to a music school in Philadelphia based solely on the color of her skin. 

Despite these structural impediments, Anderson received vital support from the Black community in Philadelphia. African American churches and colleges played a central role in sustaining young Black musical talent during this period, offering both emotional encouragement and financial sponsorship. Local church networks and community benefactors rallied around Anderson, recognizing her exceptional vocal gifts as a contralto and helping fund her early voice instruction and recital endeavors.

In 1924, as Anderson’s professional prospects began to take shape, she purchased a brick rowhouse at 762 S. Martin Street in South Philadelphia for her mother, Anna, and family. The purchase was made possible by funds generated from Anderson's early performance engagements. This residence became a vital anchor for the family and served as Anderson’s primary residence, office, and creative base as her career expanded nationally and internationally.

Recognizing that European concert venues offered greater artistic opportunities and far less racial discrimination than were available in the United States, Anderson followed the precedent set by earlier African American artists and traveled abroad to complete her vocal studies. From late October 1927 through 1935, she spent extended periods living, studying, and performing in Europe, where her concerts attracted widespread critical acclaim and established her reputation as a world-class classical vocalist.

### Career

#### European success and manager Sol Hurok

Anderson's extended period in Europe between 1927 and 1935 transformed her career. Free from the rigid racial restrictions of the American musical establishment, she refined her repertoire, performed across major European cultural capitals, and gained critical praise. When she returned to the United States in December 1935, her European credentials significantly bolstered her standing in her home country. 

During her 1935 European concerts, her talent caught the attention of Sol Hurok, an acclaimed impresario and a dominant figure in international concert management, who heard her perform in Paris. Impressed by her vocal mastery, Hurok signed Anderson to a concert-management contract before her return to the United States, expanding her booking opportunities across the globe. Under Hurok’s management, Anderson embarked on another European tour in the winter of 1936 and conducted extensive concert tours of South America in 1937 and 1938. Throughout these intensive years of domestic and international touring, Anderson continued to use her family’s home on Martin Street in Philadelphia as her operational base.

#### White House performances and friendship with Eleanor Roosevelt

In February 1936, Anderson gave a recital at the White House for President Franklin D. Roosevelt and First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt. This performance marked the beginning of a close personal friendship between Anderson and Eleanor Roosevelt that spanned many years. The First Lady became a vocal champion of Anderson's art, writing glowing reviews and bringing her national attention. 

In June 1939, Anderson was invited back to the White House to sing before King George VI and Queen Elizabeth of the United Kingdom during their state visit. One month later, in July 1939, Eleanor Roosevelt personally presented Anderson with the Spingarn Medal, the highest honor awarded annually by the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) for outstanding achievement by an African American.

#### The 1939 Lincoln Memorial Concert

The defining moment of Anderson's career—and a seminal event in the American civil rights movement—occurred early in 1939. Sol Hurok, working alongside representatives from Howard University, sought to book Washington, D.C.'s Constitution Hall for an Anderson recital. The hall was owned and operated by the Daughters of the American Revolution (DAR). The DAR refused to grant access to the venue for any of the dates requested by Hurok, maintaining a strict policy that reserved the stage exclusively for white performers, even while the facility remained open for white artists on those exact dates.

The DAR's public enforcement of racial segregation prompted immediate national outrage. In protest of the organization's overt discrimination, First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt publicly resigned her membership in the DAR, triggering a major nationwide media controversy. 

Unable to secure Constitution Hall, Mrs. Roosevelt and officials within the U.S. Department of the Interior organized a free, open-air concert on federal land. On Easter Sunday, April 9, 1939, Anderson performed on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial. An integrated crowd of more than 75,000 people gathered on the National Mall to hear her sing, while a nationwide radio broadcast carried the performance to millions of listeners. The event transformed Anderson into an enduring national icon of dignity, artistic excellence, and civil rights resistance.

#### Later career and international ambassador

Following the 1939 Lincoln Memorial concert, Anderson's commercial and artistic success surged. By 1941, she was ranked among the top ten highest-paid concert artists in the United States. She maintained a demanding national and international performance schedule throughout the 1940s and 1950s, returning to Europe for a major concert tour in 1949.

On April 24, 1950, Anderson reached an even broader public audience by making her television debut on NBC's *The Voice of Firestone*. Five years later, on January 7, 1955, she achieved another historic breakthrough when she debuted at the Metropolitan Opera in New York City as Ulrica in Giuseppe Verdi's *Un ballo in maschera*, becoming the first African American soloist ever engaged by the Met.

During the Eisenhower administration, Anderson stepped into prominent diplomatic roles. In 1957, she performed the national anthem at the presidential inauguration of Dwight D. Eisenhower. Later that year, she toured Asia as an official goodwill ambassador for the United States Department of State. Following the tour, President Eisenhower appointed Anderson as an alternate delegate to the General Assembly of the United Nations in 1958. In January 1961, she was again selected to perform at a presidential inauguration, singing for President John F. Kennedy.

### Personal life

In 1943, Marian Anderson married Orpheus H. Fisher and established a new permanent residence in Connecticut. Despite her relocation, she retained full ownership of the family home at 762 S. Martin Street in South Philadelphia. Her mother, Anna, and her sister continued to reside at the Martin Street property for the remainder of their lives. Anderson maintained ownership of the historic Philadelphia rowhouse until 1990, when the property was finally sold.

---

### Legacy

Marian Anderson's impact on American culture is recognized both for her musical achievements and her pioneering role in breaking down racial barriers in the performing arts. By asserting her place on international concert stages, at the White House, and within institutions like the Metropolitan Opera, she altered the trajectory for future generations of African American classical musicians. 

Her home in South Philadelphia stands as a physical monument to her life and career. Located at 762 S. Martin Street, the Marian Anderson House was officially listed on the National Register of Historic Places under Reference Number 11000198. The property is managed by the Marian Anderson Residence Museum, which preserves the building as a site of historical and interpretive significance dedicated to civil rights history, African American history, and the performing arts.

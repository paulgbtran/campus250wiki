### Lead Section

The **MOVE Bombing** occurred on May 13, 1985, in the Cobbs Creek neighborhood of West Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. The conflict arose from a long-standing standoff between the City of Philadelphia and MOVE, a radical communal organization combining Black nationalism, anarcho-primitivism, and strict environmentalism. Following years of escalating tensions and neighbor complaints regarding hygiene, noise, and illegal fortifications at the group’s row house headquarters on Osage Avenue, city authorities executed arrest warrants on group members.

The law enforcement operation rapidly escalated into an armed standoff involving heavy weaponry and tear gas. When municipal forces failed to dislodge the barricaded members, Police Commissioner Gregore Sambor, with the authorization of Mayor W. Wilson Goode, ordered a Pennsylvania State Police helicopter to drop an explosive device containing C4 and Tovex onto a fortified bunker constructed on the roof of the home. The resulting explosion ignited a fire, fueled by gasoline stored inside the residence, which quickly engulfed the building and spread to surrounding row houses.

Despite the rapid spread of the flames, city officials made the controversial decision to let the fire burn to dislodge the occupants and prevent police and firefighters from coming under potential crossfire. The conflagration ultimately destroyed over 60 residential homes, killed 11 occupants of the MOVE compound—including group founder John Africa and five children—and left more than 250 local residents homeless. Only two individuals inside the house, Ramona Africa and 9-year-old Birdie Africa (Michael Ward), survived.

The incident drew international condemnation and earned Philadelphia the moniker "the city that bombed itself." An independent investigatory panel appointed by Mayor Goode concluded in 1986 that the actions taken by city officials and law enforcement were "grossly negligent" and "unconscionable." The event remains a significant milestone in modern American political history, civil rights discourse, and the history of municipal policing tactics.

### Background

#### Founding and Philosophy of MOVE
MOVE was founded in 1972 in West Philadelphia by Vincent Leaphart, who adopted the name John Africa. Originally known as the American Christian Movement for Life or the Christian Life Movement, the group centered around an ideology outlined in a 300-page manifesto drafted by Leaphart with the assistance of Donald Glassey, a social worker affiliated with the University of Pennsylvania. Because Leaphart was functionally illiterate, Glassey transcribed and edited the text, which became known as *The Teaching of John Africa* (or *The Guidelines*).

The group advocated a radical return to a hunter-gatherer lifestyle, rejection of modern technology, industrialism, and synthetic medicine, alongside a commitment to animal rights, raw food diets, composting, and communal living. Members adopted the surname "Africa" to honor the continent where they believed human life originated. Group members lived in communal households, primarily in West Philadelphia, and maintained an uncompromising stance against local government authority and corporate institutions.

#### 1978 Powelton Village Siege and the "MOVE Nine"
Tensions between MOVE and the surrounding community grew throughout the 1970s over sanitation issues, the keeping of numerous domestic and wild animals on their properties, weapon code violations, and public broadcasts of John Africa's lectures via high-powered bullhorns. On August 8, 1978, a protracted standoff at the group's compound located at 307–309 North 33rd Street in Powelton Village culminated in a violent confrontation. 

When police moved in to enforce a court-ordered eviction following unpaid utility bills and health complaints, gunfire erupted. Philadelphia Police Officer James J. Ramp was killed by a gunshot wound to the back of his neck, and 18 other people—including police officers, firefighters, MOVE members, and bystanders—were injured. Nine MOVE members (later designated by supporters as the "MOVE Nine") were convicted of third-degree murder in Ramp's death and sentenced to 30 to 100 years in prison, despite MOVE’s contention that Ramp was facing the compound when shot, suggesting the fatal bullet originated elsewhere.

#### Relocation to Cobbs Creek
Following the loss of their Powelton Village compound, surviving group members relocated to 6221 Osage Avenue in Cobbs Creek, a predominantly African American, middle-class neighborhood in West Philadelphia. Intent on securing the release of the imprisoned MOVE Nine, members fortified the row house, building rooftop bunkers and internal barricades. They broadcast politically charged messages and lectures via bullhorns around the clock, accumulated refuse, kept numerous animals on-site, and reportedly threatened neighboring residents who voiced concerns over health risks and un-enrolled children.

Prompted by mounting neighbor petitions and public complaints, District Attorney Edward Rendell issued formal arrest warrants for several MOVE occupants on charges including contempt of court, illegal possession of firearms, and terroristic threats. Mayor W. Wilson Goode, the first African American mayor of Philadelphia, sanctioned a tactical police action to serve the warrants and clear the residence.

### Course of Events

#### The May 13 Operation
On the morning of Monday, May 13, 1985, nearly 500 law enforcement personnel, led by Police Commissioner Gregore Sambor and supported by tactical units, surrounded 6221 Osage Avenue. Nearby residents had been evacuated the previous evening in anticipation of a confrontation. At dawn, Commissioner Sambor announced over a loudspeaker: *"Attention MOVE: This is Gregore Sambor, Commissioner of Police. You have 15 minutes to vacate the premises."*

When the occupants refused to leave, police attempted to flush them out by spraying the structure with high-pressure fire hoses and firing tear gas canisters through holes breached in the exterior walls. MOVE members responded with gunfire, initiating an intense shootout in which law enforcement expended over 10,000 rounds of ammunition into the building over several hours.

#### The Aerial Bombing
By late afternoon, the tactical assault had reached a stalemate. MOVE members remained barricaded inside the fortified residence, protected in part by a roof-level bunker constructed from heavy timber and steel plates. Seeking to dislodge the rooftop bunker, Commissioner Sambor approved the use of an explosive device.

At approximately 5:28 p.m., a Pennsylvania State Police helicopter flew over the row house and dropped a satchel containing Tovex TR-2 and C4 explosives (weighing approximately 3.5 pounds) onto the roof. The blast detonated fuel canisters stored near the bunker, initiating a rapidly spreading fire.

#### The Fire and Escape
The explosion ignited a blaze on the roof that quickly penetrated the attic and upper floor. Rather than immediately directing firefighters to extinguish the flames, municipal leaders made a deliberate decision to let the fire burn, hoping it would force the occupants to exit the house. Officials later cited fears that firefighters would be targeted by MOVE gunfire if they approached the building.

As the fire consumed the structure and began spreading to adjacent homes, several occupants attempted to flee through the rear alleyway. Eyewitness accounts differed on whether police opened fire on escaping individuals; MOVE survivors maintained that police shooting pinned them inside the burning structure. 

By the time firefighters were deployed to contain the blaze, the inferno was out of control. It destroyed 61 row houses along Osage Avenue and Pine Street.

---

### Casualties and Immediate Impact

Eleven individuals inside 6221 Osage Avenue died in the fire:

*   **Adults**: John Africa (Vincent Leaphart), Rhonda Africa, Teresa Africa, Frank Africa, Conrad Africa, and CP Africa.
*   **Children**: Katricia Dotson (Tree Africa), Netta Africa, Delitia Africa, Phil Africa, and Tomasa Africa.

Only two occupants survived: Ramona Africa, an adult member who suffered severe burns, and 9-year-old Michael Ward (known within the group as Birdie Africa). Both escaped the rear of the building during the height of the fire.

Over 250 neighborhood residents were left homeless, and an entire city block was reduced to rubble.

### Aftermath

```
"The dropping of a bomb on an occupied row house was unconscionable."
— Philadelphia MOVE Commission Report (March 6, 1986)
```

#### Investigations and Legal Proceedings
Following widespread local and national outcry, Mayor Goode established the Special Investigation Commission (commonly known as the MOVE Commission) to evaluate the event. Published on March 6, 1986, the commission's report denounced the city's operational planning and execution, labeling the decision to drop a bomb on an occupied residential row house as "grossly negligent" and "unconscionable." The report noted that municipality officials exhibited a failure of leadership and questioned whether similar military tactics would ever have been deployed in a predominantly white neighborhood.

Despite the commission's scathing findings, no municipal or police official faced criminal prosecution for the deaths or destruction. Mayor Goode publicly accepted responsibility for the tragedy, issued a public apology, and subsequently won reelection in 1987.

Ramona Africa was prosecuted and convicted of riot charges related to the May 13 confrontation, serving seven years in prison. Following her release, she acted as MOVE’s primary public spokesperson. In 1996, a federal civil jury awarded Ramona Africa and relatives of two deceased members $1.5 million in damages after finding that the city used excessive force and violated their constitutional rights against unreasonable search and seizure.

Michael Ward was placed in the custody of his biological father after the bombing and underwent years of medical treatment and psychological therapy. He drowned in an accidental incident in 2013 at age 37.

#### Neighborhood Reconstruction and Legacy
Reconstruction efforts in the Cobbs Creek area were marred by corruption and poor construction standards. The replacement row houses built by city-contracted developers suffered from severe structural defects, including leaking roofs, failing plumbing, and improper electrical wiring. The primary contractors were later imprisoned for financial misappropriation. 

In the early 2000s, the City of Philadelphia instituted a buyout program, offering remaining property owners $150,000 each to surrender their homes, leading to a long-term decline in the neighborhood's population and stability. In November 2016, city authorities issued requests for proposals to redevelop dozens of vacant lots on Osage Avenue that had sat abandoned for over a decade.

The 1985 incident is widely cited by historians, legal scholars, and civil rights advocates as an extreme instance of domestic state force and municipal police brutality in the United States. In subsequent decades, activists and scholars have drawn parallels between the MOVE bombing and modern discussions surrounding militarized policing, urban civil rights, and systemic racism in American law enforcement.

## 2. Lead Section

The **Rodin Museum** is an art museum and formal sculpture garden located along the Benjamin Franklin Parkway in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Opened in 1929, the museum houses one of the most comprehensive collections of works by the renowned French sculptor Auguste Rodin (1840–1917) outside Paris. Administered by the Philadelphia Museum of Art, the site encompasses a purpose-built Beaux-Arts pavilion, an enclosed courtyard, and surrounding landscaped grounds situated between 21st and 22nd Streets.

The establishment of the museum was the result of a major civic gift by Jules Mastbaum (1872–1926), a Philadelphia movie-theater magnate and philanthropist. Beginning in 1923, Mastbaum assembled a vast collection of Rodin's bronzes, plasters, and drawings with the explicit intention of donating them to the citizens of Philadelphia. To showcase the collection, Mastbaum commissioned French-born architect Paul Philippe Cret and landscape architect Jacques Gréber to design a dedicated building and garden ensemble integrated into the urban plan of the Benjamin Franklin Parkway.

Among the museum's most prominent outdoor holdings are monumental bronze sculptures that frame its entrance approach. These include a cast of *The Thinker*, positioned before a stone portico, and the very first bronze cast ever produced of Rodin’s masterwork, *The Gates of Hell*, which serves as the central doorway facade for the museum building.

Today, the Rodin Museum stands as a key cultural anchor within Philadelphia's Parkway Museum District. It continues to serve as an international destination for the study and appreciation of late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century sculpture, preserving both Rodin's artistic legacy and the early twentieth-century architectural vision of Philadelphia's civic leaders.

## 3. History

The origins of the Rodin Museum are deeply tied to the career of Auguste Rodin and the patronage of Jules Mastbaum. Rodin, born the son of a Parisian civil servant, pursued art from an early age at a government craft school. Despite failing three times to gain admission to the prestigious École des Beaux-Arts, Rodin developed a revolutionary sculptural style characterized by intense realism, anatomical vitality, and expressive surface modeling. Over a career marked by public debate and critical controversy, Rodin broke from traditional neoclassical conventions, establishing himself as a foundational figure in modern sculpture.

In 1880, Rodin received a crucial commission from the French government to design a decorative portal for a proposed Museum of Decorative Arts in Paris. Drawing inspiration from Dante Alighieri’s *Inferno*, as well as classical and Renaissance precedents, Rodin embarked on *The Gates of Hell*, a massive project that occupied him for decades. Although the planned decorative arts museum was never constructed, *The Gates of Hell* became the fertile origin for over 180 individual figures, many of which—including *The Thinker*—were enlarged and cast as standalone works. However, the complete *Gates of Hell* was never cast in bronze during Rodin's lifetime.

The creation of the Philadelphia museum began decades later, when Jules Mastbaum began collecting Rodin’s works in the early 1920s. Determined to establish a museum for the public benefit, Mastbaum secured permission from the Musée Rodin in Paris to cast sculptures from Rodin's original plaster molds. Notably, Mastbaum commissioned two original bronze castings of *The Gates of Hell*—the first bronze cast went to Philadelphia, while the second was designated for the Musée Rodin in Paris. He also commissioned a monumental cast of *The Thinker* based on Rodin's 1902–1904 enlarged plaster version.

Although Mastbaum died in 1926 before construction was completed, his estate and family carried out his vision. The completed Rodin Museum opened to the public in 1929, with ownership of the land and collection vested in the city and management entrusted to the Philadelphia Museum of Art. The institution remains a lasting tribute to Mastbaum’s philanthropic vision and his effort to bring European art to the American public landscape.

## 4. Architecture

The architecture of the Rodin Museum represents a collaboration between architect Paul Philippe Cret and landscape architect Jacques Gréber, both of whom played central roles in shaping the French Neoclassical aesthetic of the Benjamin Franklin Parkway. Designed in the Beaux-Arts tradition, the complex harmonizes indoor gallery space with open-air formal gardens, creating a unified axial progression from the street to the interior galleries.

Visitors approach the museum from the Parkway through an outer entry gate into a formally arranged courtyard bounded by limestone walls and clipped hedges. The centerpiece of the garden approach is a monumental bronze cast of *The Thinker*, elevated on a substantial concrete and limestone base. The statue is framed directly behind by a stone portico facade. This architectural layout intentionally replicates the arrangement at Rodin’s estate in Meudon, France, where a cast of *The Thinker* sits before the reassembled stone facade of the Chateau d'Issy, marking the grave site of Rodin and his wife, Rose Beuret.

Beyond the inner garden path sits the main museum pavilion. The central entrance of the building is anchored by the massive bronze portal of *The Gates of Hell*. Standing over 20 feet high and 13 feet wide, the portal was incorporated directly into the limestone doorway structure by Cret and Gréber. By embedding the bronze relief directly into the museum façade, the architects made the monumental sculpture an essential structural and visual element of the building's design. Inside, the museum's natural-lit galleries house plaster studies, smaller bronzes, and archival materials.

## 5. Cultural Significance

The Rodin Museum holds significant cultural and artistic importance as both an architectural monument and a museum dedicated to a single artist. The presence of *The Gates of Hell* and *The Thinker* links Philadelphia to major developments in late nineteenth-century European art history.

*The Gates of Hell* is widely recognized as Rodin’s magnum opus. Containing more than 180 writhing figures struggling against swirling backgrounds, the portal captures themes of human anguish, desire, and mortality. Stylistically influenced by Michelangelo's sculptures and Lorenzo Ghiberti’s *Gates of Paradise* in Florence, the relief incorporates key narrative and symbolic figures:
* **The Three Shades**: Positioned atop the central pediment, these three identical figures point downward toward the damned below.
* **The Thinker**: Situated on the tympanum directly beneath the Three Shades, presiding over the scene.
* **Count Ugolino and his Sons**: Located on the lower portion of the right door, depicting the tragic figure from Dante’s *Inferno*.
* **Paolo and Francesca**: Positioned on the left door, representing the legendary doomed lovers.

*The Thinker* itself carries complex cultural meanings. Originally modeled in 1880–81 as a small figure representing Dante pondering his poem, it was enlarged between 1902 and 1904. When a large bronze cast was exhibited in Paris in 1904, it was embraced as a universal symbol of human intellect and labor. Rodin himself described the work during a period of French social tension as a "social symbol" magnifying "the fertile thought of those humble people of the soil who are nevertheless producers of powerful energies." The Philadelphia cast, measuring 6 feet 7 inches in height, remains one of the city's most recognizable icons of public art.

Through the stewardship of the Philadelphia Museum of Art and the Association for Public Art, the Rodin Museum continues to maintain its collection, outdoor sculptures, and historic gardens, preserving a key chapter of Philadelphia's civic design and art collecting history.

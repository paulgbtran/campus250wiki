### Lead section

Alexander Stirling Calder (1870–1945) was a distinguished American sculptor whose civic monuments and public fountains played a central role in defining the architectural and visual landscape of twentieth-century Philadelphia. Born into one of America's most celebrated artistic dynasties, Calder represented the middle generation of a century-long sculpturing tradition in Philadelphia, standing between his father, Alexander Milne Calder (1846–1923), and his son, Alexander "Sandy" Calder (1898–1976). His work skillfully merged classical allegorical composition with regional themes and dynamic civic design.

Calder's most renowned contribution to Philadelphia's public realm is the *Swann Memorial Fountain* (officially titled *The Fountain of Three Rivers*), completed in 1924 in Logan Square along the Benjamin Franklin Parkway. Designed in close collaboration with Philadelphia architect Wilson Eyre, Jr., the monumental bronze and granite fountain serves as a civic monument to Dr. Wilson Cary Swann, founder of the Philadelphia Fountain Society. Calder's sculptural grouping allegorized the three primary waterways of the Philadelphia region—the Delaware River, the Schuylkill River, and Wissahickon Creek—through expressive human nudes and spirited animal forms.

Beyond its artistic merit, Calder's work stood as an integral landmark in the urban transformation of Philadelphia during the early twentieth century. As the city developed the Benjamin Franklin Parkway to connect City Hall with Fairmount Park, Calder's fountain provided a focal point for Logan Square, balancing grand European-inspired urban planning with distinctively local geographical and historical iconography.

The legacy of Alexander Stirling Calder remains permanently enshrined across Philadelphia's physical environment. Alongside the colossal statue of *William Penn* atop City Hall created by his father and the modern kinetic mobile sculptures pioneered by his son, Calder's work forms an essential link in an artistic lineage that defines Philadelphia's public space.

### Early life and education

Alexander Stirling Calder was born in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, on January 11, 1870. He grew up embedded in the city's vibrant art community, profoundly influenced by the work of his father, Scottish-born sculptor Alexander Milne Calder. During Stirling Calder's youth, his father was engaged in designing the elaborate sculptural program for Philadelphia City Hall, a massive project culminating in the 37-foot bronze statue of William Penn placed atop the building's tower. Surrounded by plaster casts, stonemasons, and grand civic commissions from an early age, Calder naturally gravitated toward the sculptural arts.

Calder received his formal artistic instruction in Philadelphia, immersing himself in classical drawing and anatomical modeling. His artistic education was grounded in the traditions of late nineteenth-century Beaux-Arts sculpture, emphasizing monumental harmony, allegorical symbolism, and integration with architectural contexts. Calder was also mindful of Philadelphia's rich tradition of public statuary, including historical works such as John Cheere’s 1774 lead statue of William Penn. Originally imported from England and installed in the Pine Street Garden of Pennsylvania Hospital in 1804, Cheere's figure—designed with a downward gaze intended for viewing from below—had previously served as a direct conceptual influence on his father's planning for the City Hall monument.

Refining his craft through rigorous training and early regional commissions, Stirling Calder developed an artistic style that balanced traditional figurative modeling with expressive, lively naturalism. His early exposure to Philadelphia's civic institutions and patron networks laid the groundwork for his eventual selection for major municipal projects.

### Career

Calder established himself as a master of public sculpture through numerous prestigious civic commissions throughout the United States, but his defining artistic accomplishment took place in his hometown of Philadelphia. In the early 1920s, Calder was selected to create the sculptural centerpiece for Logan Square, a major urban node along the newly constructed Benjamin Franklin Parkway.

The commission was funded through a bequest from Maria E. Swann to honor her late husband, Dr. Wilson Cary Swann, the founder and president of the Philadelphia Fountain Society. Established to build public drinking fountains throughout the urban grid, the society sought a monumental water feature that would combine public beauty with symbolic resonance. Partnering with architect Wilson Eyre, Jr., Calder designed a masterwork titled *The Fountain of Three Rivers*, known popularly as the *Swann Memorial Fountain*.

```
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                        SWANN MEMORIAL FOUNTAIN                               |
|                  (The Fountain of Three Rivers, 1924)                         |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Delaware River    |  Schuylkill River   |  Wissahickon Creek                 |
|  (Powerful Mature  |  (Mature Woman      |  (Young Female Figure              |
|   Male Figure)     |   Holding Swan)     |   Leaning Modestly Against Swan)   |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
|  Basin Elements    |  Bronze Frogs and Turtles Shooting Water Jets            |
|  Center Dynamics   |  50-Foot Central Geyser & Interlaced Sprays (Eyre)       |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

Calder’s sculptural composition featured three central bronze figures reclining on granite bases within a 124-foot-diameter fountain pool:

* **The Delaware River**: Personified as a powerful, mature male figure, capturing the force and scale of the region's largest river.
* **The Schuylkill River**: Personified as a mature woman holding the neck of a water-spouting swan, symbolizing a somewhat smaller yet crucial regional water route.
* **Wissahickon Creek**: Personified as a young female figure leaning modestly against an agitated, water-spouting swan. This arrangement combined classical references to Leda and the Swan with a playful visual pun on Dr. Swann’s surname.

To complete the fountain’s aquatic composition, Calder modeled bronze frogs and turtles along the perimeter of the basin, positioned to shoot water jets inward toward the central figures. Calder deliberately contrasted the calm, composed human figures with the energetic, ruffled fauna. Architect Wilson Eyre, Jr. engineered a central geyser shooting 50 feet into the air, surrounded by staggered and interlaced jets that created a dramatic water display.

In 1924, the *Swann Memorial Fountain* was completed and dedicated, quickly becoming a popular gathering point and civic landmark in Logan Square.

### Personal life

Alexander Stirling Calder maintained deep ties to the artistic, cultural, and civic leadership of Philadelphia throughout his life. Raised in a household dedicated to public art, he passed these creative traditions down to his own family. Calder married painter Nanette Lederer, and together they cultivated a home environment centered on visual art and creative experimentation.

Their son, Alexander "Sandy" Calder (1898–1976), would achieve international acclaim as one of the twentieth century's most innovative artists, famous for inventing the kinetic mobile sculpture and producing monumental stabiles. The close artistic connection between the generations remained a defining aspect of the family's public identity, with Stirling Calder encouraging his son's early spatial and mechanical interests. Stirling Calder passed away in New York City on January 7, 1945, at the age of 74, leaving behind a legacy as one of the preeminent American sculptors of his era.

---

### Legacy

Alexander Stirling Calder's work remains a cornerstone of Philadelphia's public art heritage. The *Swann Memorial Fountain* continues to serve as an architectural centerpiece along the Benjamin Franklin Parkway, drawing residents and visitors to Logan Square every summer.

Art historians and urban planners frequently highlight the unique spatial narrative formed by the three generations of Calder sculptors along Philadelphia's central civic axis:

1. **City Hall**: Anchoring Center City, crowned by Alexander Milne Calder's monumental *William Penn*.
2. **Logan Square**: Midway down the Parkway, centered on Alexander Stirling Calder's *Swann Memorial Fountain*.
3. **Philadelphia Museum of Art**: Terminating the Parkway, housing major modern works by Alexander "Sandy" Calder, including his celebrated mobile *Ghost*.

The preservation and interpretation of Calder’s public works have been sustained by civic institutions such as the City of Philadelphia and the Association for Public Art (formerly the Fairmount Park Art Association). Historians such as Penny Balkin Bach, long-time Executive Director of the Association for Public Art and author of *Public Art in Philadelphia* (Temple University Press, 1992), have documented Calder’s contributions, emphasizing his ability to harmonize classical sculpture with local history, ecology, and municipal geography.

### 2. Lead Section

The **Betsy Ross House** is a historic dwelling located at 239 Arch Street in the Old City neighborhood of Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. The structure is traditionally commemorated as the residence where the seamstress and upholsterer Elizabeth Griscom Ross (1752–1836), known historically as Betsy Ross, lived and worked during the American Revolutionary War. According to popular American tradition, it was in this house in May or June of 1776 that Ross received a secret committee of the Continental Congress—consisting of George Washington, Robert Morris, and George Ross—and subsequently created the first official flag of the United States, featuring thirteen red and white stripes and thirteen five-pointed stars arranged in a circle.

Constructed around 1740, the building is a prominent surviving example of 18th-century middle-class vernacular architecture in Philadelphia. Designed as a modest urban dwelling and commercial shopfront, the structure reflects the dense architectural layout characteristic of Philadelphia’s early artisan districts, where small-scale tradespeople maintained dual residential and workshop spaces under a single roof.

During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, as Center City Philadelphia underwent rapid industrialization and commercial redevelopment, the Betsy Ross House faced physical deterioration and the threat of demolition. In 1898, artist Charles Weisgerber and a coalition of civic leaders established the American Flag House and Betsy Ross Memorial Association, launching a national grassroots fundraising campaign to purchase and preserve the site. The property was comprehensively restored in 1937 under the direction of historic preservation architect Richardson Brognard Okie, funded through a gift by industrialist A. Atwater Kent, and subsequently gifted to the City of Philadelphia in 1941.

Today, the Betsy Ross House functions as a historic house museum managed by Historic Philadelphia, Inc. Situated within walking distance of Independence National Historical Park, Christ Church, and Elfreth's Alley, the site serves as a national focal point for Revolutionary War history, civic tourism, and the interpretation of 18th-century women's trades and domestic life.

### 3. History

#### Colonial Origins and 18th-Century Tenancy
The land on which the Betsy Ross House sits was part of the original grid plan surveyed for William Penn's capital city in the late 17th century. By around 1740, a two-and-a-half-story brick dwelling was constructed on Mulberry Street (later renamed Arch Street). The property was situated in a bustling maritime and trade district near the Delaware River waterfront, populated predominantly by artisans, mariners, shopkeepers, and laborers. Like many small urban structures of the era, the building was leased to various tradespeople who utilized the front parlor on the ground floor as a commercial shop while occupying the upper levels as living quarters.

Elizabeth "Betsy" Griscom Ross rented the property from 1773 to 1785, initially moving in with her first husband, John Ross, and continuing her independent upholstery business there after his death in early 1776 while serving in the local militia. As a skilled tradeswoman operating an upholstery business during the War of Independence, Ross produced items such as bed coverings, curtains, chair cushions, and ship colors for merchant and military vessels. Historical tradition holds that in late spring 1776, a delegation representing the Continental Congress visited Ross in her front parlor to ask if she could manufacture a national ensign based on a draft sketch. According to the account passed down through her descendants, Ross suggested changing the six-pointed stars in the draft to five-pointed stars, demonstrating how to fold and cut a five-pointed star with a single snip of her shears.

#### Post-Revolutionary Transitions and 19th-Century Commercial Use
Following Betsy Ross's departure from the Arch Street residence in 1785, the property continued to serve as a mixed-use commercial and residential site. Throughout the 19th century, the building accommodated a succession of small businesses, including a shoe shop, a tailor’s establishment, an apothecary, and a cigar store. By the mid-to-late 19th century, the lower floor was converted into a saloon and tavern, while the surrounding neighborhood evolved from a quiet residential-craft precinct into a commercial shipping and industrial corridor.

In 1870, Betsy Ross’s grandson, William J. Canby, presented a paper to the Historical Society of Pennsylvania outlining his grandmother’s role in creating the first American flag, citing affidavits gathered from family members. Canby’s accounts published nationwide sparked renewed public interest in Betsy Ross and brought national attention to the Arch Street building as a sacred national landmark.

#### Preservation, Restoration, and Municipal Ownership
By the late 1890s, the aging structure was at risk of decay and redevelopment. In 1898, artist Charles Weisgerber—who had painted a large historical portrait titled *Birth of Our Nation's Flag*—co-founded the American Flag House and Betsy Ross Memorial Association. The association launched a national campaign encouraging citizens, school children, and patriotic organizations to contribute small donations ("dime subscriptions") in exchange for membership certificates. This campaign succeeded in raising the funds needed to acquire the property in 1905.

In 1937, radio manufacturer and philanthropist A. Atwater Kent donated substantial capital to undertake a thorough restoration of the house. Renowned preservation architect Richardson Brognard Okie was retained to direct the restoration. Okie removed 19th-century storefront additions, stabilized the brick walls, replaced damaged timber, and restored the interior floor plan to its 18th-century appearance using period-appropriate materials and salvage components from demolished colonial-era Philadelphia buildings. In 1941, the association formally transferred ownership of the restored house and adjacent courtyard to the City of Philadelphia.

### 4. Architecture

#### Style and Exterior Features
The Betsy Ross House is a traditional mid-18th-century Philadelphia brick town house built in the vernacular Georgian style. It rises two and a half stories above a stone cellar and features a steeply pitched gabled roof punctuated by dormer windows. The front facade is laid in Flemish bond brickwork—a hallmark of mid-18th-century Philadelphia construction—with alternating glazed headers and red stretchers that create a subtle decorative pattern.

The main facade on Arch Street features multi-pane wood sash windows with stone sills and simple wooden architraves. A simple wooden shed canopy shelters the main entrance door on the ground level, restoring the appearance of typical 18th-century Philadelphia artisan dwellings. A narrow brick alleyway along the side of the property provides historical access to the rear yard and service spaces.

#### Interior Layout and Vernacular Design
The interior configuration follows a compact spatial organization commonly referred to in regional architectural history as a "bandbox" or "Trinity-style" plan influence, characterized by a single main room on each floor stacked vertically around a narrow, winding wooden staircase. 

*   **Ground Floor:** The ground level consists of two primary rooms: a front shop parlor where Betsy Ross conducted her upholstery business and received customers, and a rear kitchen equipped with a large brick fireplace, beehive oven, and cooking hearth.
*   **Second Floor:** The second story contains the principal bedchamber and living spaces, restored with 18th-century furniture, textiles, and domestic artifacts reflective of an artisan household during the Revolutionary era.
*   **Attic and Cellar:** The half-story attic served historically as additional sleeping quarters or storage for raw materials such as feathers, horsehair, and heavy fabrics used in upholstery. The cellar provided cool storage for food and household goods.

#### Modern Complex and Courtyard
Over the course of the 20th century, the site was expanded to include adjacent real estate to enhance visitor access and fire safety. A brick-paved public courtyard situated adjacent to the original dwelling features gardens, an outdoor assembly space, a museum gift shop, and civic memorials. The courtyard also contains the reinterred grave of Betsy Ross, whose remains were relocated to the site in 1975 ahead of the United States Bicentennial celebrations.

### 5. Cultural Significance

#### Symbolism in Civic Nationalism and Public Memory
The Betsy Ross House holds a permanent position in the iconography of the American Revolution and early national identity. Although 20th-century professional historians have noted a lack of contemporary 1776 written documentation proving that Betsy Ross created the first Stars and Stripes, the site remains a vital symbol of national origins and civic mythology. The narrative of Ross and her Arch Street parlor plays a foundational role in how the story of the American flag is transmitted across generations.

#### Representation of Colonial Women's Labor
Beyond the flag legend, the site serves as an invaluable educational platform for interpreting the economic and social history of working-class women in 18th-century urban America. Upholstery was a complex, physically demanding trade requiring knowledge of textiles, leatherwork, mattress making, and furniture construction. The Betsy Ross House provides visitors with insight into how widowed women navigated financial independence, operated small businesses, and contributed directly to military logistics and municipal life during the Revolutionary War.

#### Modern Tourism and Educational Operations
As one of Philadelphia's most visited historic sites, the Betsy Ross House attracts hundreds of thousands of visitors annually. Managed by Historic Philadelphia, Inc., the museum offers living history interpretations, interactive educational programs, and annual events such as Flag Day celebrations on June 14. Integrated into the historic tour circuits of Old City, the house serves as an essential link between popular national tradition, architectural preservation, and urban history in Philadelphia.

## Lead section

**Glen Foerd on the Delaware** is an 18-acre historic estate, museum, and public park located in the Torresdale neighborhood of Northeast Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Situated along the Delaware River near Poquessing Creek, it is recognized as the sole remaining Gilded Age country estate along the Philadelphia bank of the Delaware River that remains intact and fully open to the public. Originally established in the mid-19th century as a summer retreat for prominent civic leaders, the estate reflects more than a century of architectural evolution, landscape design, and upper-class domestic life in Philadelphia.

The site was initially developed around 1850 by Charles Macalester, a wealthy Philadelphia financier and presidential advisor, who constructed a summer residence in the Italianate style named "Glengarry." In 1893, the property was purchased by Robert H. Foerderer, an influential industrialist and U.S. Congressman who made his fortune through the revolutionary "Vici Kid" chrome-tanning process for leather. Foerderer and his family renamed the estate "Glen Foerd," combining the original Scottish name with their family moniker, and commissioned extensive Beaux-Arts and Colonial Revival additions in the early 1900s under architect William Davenport Hewitt.

Throughout the 20th century, Glen Foerd served as the residence of Foerderer’s daughter, Florence Foerderer Tonner, and her husband Philip E. Tonner. Florence Tonner was a patron of the arts and avid collector who amassed a substantial collection of fine art, rare prints, antiques, and sculpture. Upon her death in 1971, she bequeathed the estate, its structures, and its artistic holdings to a public trust, ensuring its permanent protection and conversion into a municipal historic park managed in partnership with the City of Philadelphia.

Today, Glen Foerd on the Delaware is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and functions as a non-profit cultural venue, educational center, and passive recreation park. Featuring intact historic outbuildings—including a stone water tower, carriage house, and riverfront boat house—alongside historic tree specimens and formal gardens, Glen Foerd stands as a crucial artifact of Philadelphia's industrial wealth, landscape preservation, and architectural history.

## Establishment and early history

The early development and foundational history of Glen Foerd began in 1850 when Charles Macalester (1798–1873), a prominent Scottish-American businessman and financier, acquired the rural riverfront parcel in Torresdale, then located within Lower Dublin Township prior to the 1854 Consolidation Act of Philadelphia. Macalester was a major figure in Philadelphia’s economic and political circles, having served as a director of the Second Bank of the United States and an intimate financial advisor to several U.S. Presidents, most notably James Buchanan. Seeking an escape from the densely populated urban core of Center City Philadelphia, Macalester chose the scenic high ground overlooking the Delaware River to build a grand summer villa.

Macalester named his original residence "Glengarry" after his ancestral family seat in the Scottish Highlands. Designed in the prevailing Italianate architectural style popular during the American mid-19th century, the original villa featured asymmetrical massing, decorative brackets, tall narrow windows, and broad verandas designed to catch cooling river breezes. The surrounding acreage was landscaped to complement the natural topography of the river bank, featuring native hardwood trees and sweeping lawns that showcased unimpeded vistas across the Delaware River toward New Jersey.

During Macalester's tenure, Glengarry served as a prominent locus of political and social activity. Presidential visits and gatherings of Philadelphia elite were common at the estate. Macalester spent his later years expanding the acreage and refining the grounds, laying the spatial footprint for what would eventually become one of the premier country seats of the region. Following Macalester's death in 1873, the property passed through family heirs and temporary stewards, remaining largely preserved in its original mid-Victorian configuration until the late 19th century.

---

## Foerderer ownership and expansion

The estate entered its most transformative phase of industrial ownership and architectural expansion in 1893, when it was purchased by Robert H. Foerderer (1860–1903). Foerderer was one of Philadelphia's preeminent Gilded Age manufacturing magnates. He revolutionized the global textile and leather trade by perfecting and patenting the "Vici Kid" process, a specialized chrome-tanning method that rendered goat hides soft, durable, and water-resistant. Foerderer's industrial complexes in Frankford became among the largest leather manufacturing plants in the world, generating massive personal wealth and elevating Foerderer to political prominence as a U.S. Representative from Pennsylvania.

Upon acquiring Glengarry, Foerderer renamed the estate "Glen Foerd," fusing its original Scottish title with his surname. In 1902, Foerderer initiated a massive expansion project to transform the mid-Victorian villa into a grand Gilded Age mansion suited for grand entertaining and modern living. He hired prominent Philadelphia architect William Davenport Hewitt (of the architecture firm Hewitt Brothers) to orchestrate the renovation. Hewitt encased and expanded the original Italianate structure within an elaborate Beaux-Arts and Colonial Revival envelope. 

The enlargement added a third story, a majestic classical portico supported by soaring Ionic columns along the river facade, an expansive ballroom, and a dedicated art gallery fitted with a glass dome. Interior spaces were fitted with intricate woodwork, elaborate plaster moldings, and an ornate 1902 Haskell pipe organ. Outbuildings were simultaneously constructed or updated, including a stone water tower, a brick carriage house, a boat house on the river's edge, and formal rose gardens designed by the renowned horticultural firm Thomas Meehan & Sons. Although Robert Foerderer died suddenly in 1903 shortly before the renovations were completed, his vision established Glen Foerd as a landmark of industrial wealth and architectural sophistication.

## Tonner era and art collection

Following the death of Robert H. Foerderer and his wife Caroline, the domestic life of Glen Foerd centered around their daughter, Florence Foerderer Tonner (1882–1971), and her husband, Philip E. Tonner. Florence Tonner was a central figure in Philadelphia's cultural and philanthropic circles for over six decades. Under her stewardship, Glen Foerd evolved from a private family residence into a vibrant cultural retreat and home to extensive artistic collections.

Florence Tonner maintained an intense personal passion for fine art, printmaking, classical sculpture, and decorative antiques. Over her decades residing at Glen Foerd, she filled the mansion’s gallery and living rooms with thousands of pieces, including works attributed to European masters such as John Constable and Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot, alongside 19th- and 20th-century American painters, rare European porcelain, vintage tapestries, and an extensive library of historic books. The mansion served as a meeting place for local art societies, musical performances utilizing the home’s pipe organ, and philanthropic fundraisers for civic and charitable organizations across Greater Philadelphia.

Despite the rapid industrialization and urban expansion of Northeast Philadelphia throughout the mid-20th century, the Tonners preserved the estate's rural residential character, keeping the 18 acres of riverfront grounds, mature arboretum plantings, and formal garden spaces intact. Florence Tonner lived at Glen Foerd until her death in 1971 at the age of 89. Having no direct heirs, she structured her estate planning to ensure that Glen Foerd would avoid commercial subdivision and remain preserved in perpetuity for the benefit of the surrounding community and general public.

---

## Legacy and public preservation

Glen Foerd on the Delaware occupies a distinct place in the architectural, environmental, and civic heritage of Philadelphia. In accordance with Florence Foerderer Tonner’s multi-tiered bequest, ownership of the estate was transferred to the Fairmount Park Commission (now Philadelphia Parks & Recreation), while a dedicated non-profit organization—the Glen Foerd Conservation Corporation—was established to manage, preserve, and operate the property. In 1979, Glen Foerd was officially listed on the National Register of Historic Places, recognizing its architectural integrity and historical association with Philadelphia’s industrial expansion.

In contemporary Philadelphia, Glen Foerd functions as an active community museum, public park, and environmental center along the Delaware River. The estate's grounds feature rare specimens of trees, an intact riverbank ecosystem, and accessible trails connected to regional greenway networks. The preserved mansion houses the original furnishings and art collection of Florence Foerderer Tonner, offering visitors an authentic window into the lifestyle, aesthetic taste, and domestic environment of early 20th-century Philadelphia high society.

Through ongoing restoration efforts, educational workshops, environmental programming, and cultural events, Glen Foerd remains a vital educational and recreational asset. It stands as a rare surviving link to the grand era of river estates that once lined the Delaware River, exemplifying the preservation of Philadelphia’s rich architectural, industrial, and artistic legacy.

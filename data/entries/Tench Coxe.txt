## Lead Section

**Tench Coxe** (May 22, 1755 – July 17, 1824) was an American political economist, merchant, public official, and prolific writer from Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Operating at the intersection of commerce, government policy, and constitutional debate during the Founding Era, Coxe was a central architect of early American economic nationalism. His federal administrative career spanned both Federalist and Democratic-Republican administrations, serving as a Pennsylvania delegate to the Continental Congress, Assistant Secretary of the Treasury, Commissioner of the Revenue, and Purveyor of Public Supplies.

Coxe played a pivotal role in shaping the economic foundations of the early United States. Working alongside Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton, Coxe gathered extensive statistical data and drafted key sections of the seminal *Report on Manufactures* (1791). He was among the earliest American economic theorists to advocate for large-scale industrialization, mechanized manufacturing, protective tariffs, and the domestic cultivation of cotton to supply northern textile mills, laying the intellectual groundwork for Philadelphia’s emergence as a major American manufacturing center.

In addition to his contributions to economic policy, Coxe was a prominent political pamphleteer during the ratification debates surrounding the United States Constitution. Writing under various pseudonyms, most notably "A Pennsylvanian," he authored widely circulated essays defending the proposed Constitution. His writings provided detailed commentaries on the rights of citizens, militia organization, and executive power, and are frequently cited in modern constitutional jurisprudence for their explicit formulation of the individual right to keep and bear arms under the Second Amendment.

Despite early alignment with Federalist leaders, Coxe later broke with Alexander Hamilton over personal and policy disputes, transitioning to Thomas Jefferson’s Democratic-Republican Party in the late 1790s. Jefferson appointed him Purveyor of Public Supplies in 1803, a post Coxe held through the War of 1812 era. Throughout his life, Coxe remained deeply invested in land development and industrial innovation, leaving a complex legacy as a political pragmatist and economic visionary in early Pennsylvania history.

## Early Life and Education

Tench Coxe was born in Philadelphia on May 22, 1755, into a prominent and wealthy merchant family with deep roots in colonial Pennsylvania and New Jersey. His father, William Coxe, was a successful Philadelphia merchant, civic leader, and landholder who served as mayor of Philadelphia in 1760. His mother, Mary Tench Coxe, descended from a well-connected Anglo-Irish family. Growing up in the commercial heart of Philadelphia, young Coxe was exposed early to maritime trade, mercantile affairs, and colonial politics.

Coxe received his formal education at the College of Philadelphia (now the University of Pennsylvania), entering in 1769. Though he was intended for a legal career, his family directed him toward commercial enterprise, placing him in his father's merchant firm, Coxe & Furman. By his early twenties, Coxe had acquired a detailed command of international trade networks, currency exchange, accounting, and shipping economics, skills that would define his later public career.

The outbreak of the American Revolutionary War posed political and personal dilemmas for the Coxe family. In 1777, when British forces occupied Philadelphia under General Sir William Howe, the young Coxe remained in the city and briefly joined the Royal Pennsylvania Loyalists, a provincial Loyalist regiment. However, when British troops evacuated Philadelphia in June 1778, Coxe remained behind, surrendered to Patriot authorities, and took an oath of allegiance to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania. Although political opponents later used his brief Loyalist association against him throughout his public life, Coxe quickly rehabilitated his civic standing in Philadelphia, resuming his mercantile operations and joining local patriotic civil societies.

## Career

### Economic Nationalism and Industrial Advocacy
During the 1780s, Coxe became a vocal advocate for economic self-sufficiency and the expansion of domestic industry in Pennsylvania. Observing the economic instability under the Articles of Confederation—characterized by trade imbalances, reliance on imported British goods, and scarce specie—he joined other Philadelphia merchants in advocating for a stronger national government capable of regulating trade.

In August 1787, Coxe co-founded the Pennsylvania Society for the Encouragement of Manufactures and the Useful Arts. Delivering the inaugural address to the society at the University of Pennsylvania, Coxe articulated a bold vision for American economic development. He called for the introduction of modern European textile machinery (such as spinning jennies and water frames), financial premiums for inventors, state incentives for factory establishment, and domestic cotton cultivation in the Southern states to feed Mid-Atlantic mills. His economic writings, published in tracts and periodicals such as *The American Museum*, promoted a balanced national economy integrating agriculture, commerce, and manufacturing.

### Federal Government Service and Treasury Department
Coxe was selected as a delegate representing Pennsylvania to the Continental Congress in 1789. Following the establishment of the new federal government under the U.S. Constitution, Alexander Hamilton appointed Coxe as Assistant Secretary of the Treasury in May 1790. In this position, Coxe functioned as Hamilton’s primary administrative assistant, managing complex statistical compilations, trade figures, and revenue logistics.

Coxe made substantial contributions to Hamilton’s landmark *Report on Manufactures* presented to Congress in December 1791. Coxe authored the initial draft of the report, compiling empirical data on existing domestic industries and advocating for tariffs, bounties, and infrastructure development. While Hamilton refined the philosophical arguments of the final text, Coxe’s empirical research provided the underlying structure. In May 1792, President George Washington appointed Coxe as Commissioner of the Revenue, where he supervised national tax collections, lighthouses, and revenue officers until 1797.

### Constitutional Defense and Second Amendment Writings
During the constitutional ratification debates of 1787 and 1788, Coxe was one of the most prolific Federalist essayists. Writing under the pseudonym "A Pennsylvanian" in the *Pennsylvania Gazette* and other major newspapers, he published series of essays explaining and defending the newly drafted Constitution.

Coxe’s essays specifically addressed concerns raised by Anti-Federalists regarding federal standing armies and civilian rights. In his widely read piece *An Examination of the Constitution for the United States* (1787), Coxe emphasized that the civilian populace possessed the inherent right to own and bear private arms, asserting that an armed citizenry served as an ultimate safeguard against government tyranny. His explicit framing of private arms ownership alongside militia service has made his writings a key primary source for historians and legal scholars studying the original intent of the Second Amendment to the U.S. Constitution.

### Partisan Realignment and Jeffersonian Service
Despite his early collaboration with Hamilton, personal friction and policy disagreements over tax administration and political patronage severely strained their relationship. By the mid-1790s, Coxe increasingly aligned himself with Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, criticizing Federalist foreign policy and standing army proposals. President John Adams removed Coxe from his post as Commissioner of the Revenue in December 1797.

Following his dismissal, Coxe openly joined the Democratic-Republican Party. He authored political pamphlets supporting Thomas McKean’s gubernatorial campaign in Pennsylvania and Thomas Jefferson’s presidential campaign in 1800. In recognition of his support and administrative talents, President Jefferson appointed Coxe as Purveyor of Public Supplies in 1803. Operating from Philadelphia, Coxe oversaw federal procurement of military uniforms, ordnance, naval stores, and equipment for the U.S. military until 1812, managing complex logistics during the buildup to the War of 1812.

## Personal Life

Tench Coxe was married twice. In 1777, he married Catherine McCall, the daughter of a prominent Philadelphia family; she died in childbirth the following year in 1778. In 1782, Coxe married his cousin, Rebecca Coxe. The couple maintained a residence in Philadelphia and had ten children together, establishing a prominent family lineage that remained active in Pennsylvania legal, financial, and political circles for generations.

In addition to his government service, Coxe was a speculative land investor. Believing that North American land values would appreciate rapidly, he acquired vast holdings across Pennsylvania, North Carolina, and New York. Most notably, Coxe acquired tens of thousands of acres of timberland and anthracite coal lands in northeastern Pennsylvania, specifically within Luzerne, Schuylkill, and Carbon counties. Although land speculation burdened Coxe with recurring financial liquidity troubles during his lifetime, his holdings in the Pennsylvania anthracite region later proved enormously valuable to his heirs during the nineteenth-century American industrial revolution.

Coxe was active in Philadelphia civic and intellectual societies, maintaining membership in the American Philosophical Society and participating in local Episcopal church governance at St. Peter's Church. He died in Philadelphia on July 17, 1824, at the age of 69, and was buried in the churchyard at St. Peter's Church in Center City Philadelphia.

---

## Legacy

Tench Coxe’s contributions to American history lie in his pioneering economic theories and his role in early constitutional commentary. As an early proponent of economic nationalism, Coxe articulated a vision of an economically self-sufficient republic that combined Southern agricultural production with Northern manufacturing capabilities—a model that anticipated Henry Clay’s "American System" and shaped early nineteenth-century American economic policy.

Within Philadelphia history, Coxe is recognized as a vital advocate who helped transform the region into the "Workshop of the World." His institutional support for technological acquisition, industrial societies, and textile production created the framework that established Philadelphia and eastern Pennsylvania as premier manufacturing hubs throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries.

In legal history and constitutional law, Coxe’s writings remain influential. Modern legal historians and American courts, including the Supreme Court of the United States in landmark Second Amendment cases such as *District of Columbia v. Heller* (2008), have relied on Coxe’s 1787 and 1788 pseudonymous essays to illuminate the eighteenth-century public understanding of the right to keep and bear arms.

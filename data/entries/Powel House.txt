## Lead section

The **Powel House** is a historic residence located at 244 South 3rd Street, between Spruce and Walnut Streets, in the Society Hill neighborhood of Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Built between 1765 and 1766, the elegant Georgian townhouse is widely recognized as one of the finest surviving examples of colonial domestic architecture in the United States. Originally commissioned by merchant and ironmaster Charles Stedman, the property became famous as the grand residence of Samuel Powel, a prominent political figure, wealthy landowner, and key civic leader during the American Revolutionary era.

Samuel Powel served as the final mayor of Philadelphia under British colonial rule and subsequently became the city's first mayor following the re-chartering of the city after the establishment of American independence, earning him the popular epithet "the Patriotic Mayor." Along with his wife, Elizabeth Willing Powel, he turned the residence into a premier center of political and social life in colonial and early republican Philadelphia. The house frequently hosted elite figures of the Revolutionary period, most notably George and Martha Washington, who developed a close personal friendship with the Powel family.

Architecturally, the building represents the pinnacle of 18th-century Philadelphia craftsmanship, designed by a member of the prestigious Carpenters' Company of Philadelphia with interior modifications executed by the noted master builder Robert Smith. Its refined exterior proportions and interior wood carving reflected the immense wealth and cosmopolitan tastes of the city's ruling merchant class. In 1918 and 1925, historic architectural elements from the building's interior were sold to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York City and the Philadelphia Museum of Art, drawing national attention to the house's artistic merit.

In 1931, the Powel House faced imminent destruction when plans were announced to demolish the structure to make way for an open-air parking garage. The subsequent public effort to rescue and preserve the townhouse, led by Frances Wister and the newly formed Philadelphia Society for the Preservation of Landmarks, marked a watershed moment in the history of historic preservation in Philadelphia. Today, the Powel House stands as a protected historic site and museum, preserved to showcase the social life, civic governance, and architectural grandeur of late 18th-century America.

## History

### Lineage and Acquisition

The history of the Powel House is tied to the rise of one of Philadelphia's most affluent families. The family's fortune began with Samuel Powell (the elder), an orphan who emigrated from England to the Pennsylvania colony in 1685 accompanied by his aunt. Settling in William Penn's fledgling capital, the elder Powell accumulated vast wealth through an advantageous marriage and his skills in bridge-building and carpentry. Over his lifetime, he acquired dozens of residential properties and real estate holdings across Philadelphia. His son, Samuel Powell Jr., worked as a merchant and adopted the simplified spelling of the family surname, "Powel." 

When Samuel Powell Jr. died in 1747, his father outlived him by nine years. Upon the elder Powell's death in 1756, the vast real estate empire skipped a generation and passed directly to his eighteen-year-old grandson, Samuel Powel (born 1738). As the sole heir to a fortune that included approximately ninety properties, young Samuel Powel possessed the financial freedom to undertake an extended Grand Tour of Europe. While most young elites spent one or two years abroad, Powel remained in Europe for seven years. During his travels, he gained audience with prominent European figures, including Pope Clement XIII, the Duke of York, and the Enlightenment philosopher Voltaire, who reportedly advised Powel to "hate priests." Powel disregarded Voltaire's admonition, converting while in England from his family's traditional Quakerism to the Anglican Church.

Upon returning to Philadelphia, Powel married Elizabeth Willing, the daughter of Charles Willing and Ann Shippen, uniting two of the colony's most influential merchant dynasties. Rather than choosing to live in one of the nearly ninety properties he had inherited, Powel elected to purchase a newly constructed, luxurious townhouse on South 3rd Street. The home had been built between 1765 and 1766 for Charles Stedman, a merchant and forge co-owner who never actually resided in it. Facing severe financial difficulties, Stedman sold the home to Powel in August 1769; Stedman's financial collapse later culminated in his commitment to a debtors' prison in 1774.

### Revolutionary Era and Civic Leadership

Samuel Powel quickly established himself as a central figure in municipal governance. In 1775, he was elected Mayor of Philadelphia, making him the last mayor to serve under the Pennsylvania colonial charter administered by the British Crown. When the American Revolutionary War broke out, Powel aligned himself with the patriot cause. His commitment to the newly formed republic earned him widespread trust, and following the war, he was chosen as the first Mayor of Philadelphia under the new 1789 city charter of independent Pennsylvania. 

Throughout the Revolutionary era and the subsequent period when Philadelphia served as the temporary capital of the United States, the Powel House operated as an informal salon for national leaders. Samuel and Elizabeth Powel were renowned for their opulent hospitality. George and Martha Washington were frequent guests at the 3rd Street residence, regularly attending dinners and receptions hosted by the Powels. The personal bond between the families was deep and enduring; the Washingtons hosted the Powels at Mount Vernon at least six times over the years. 

Powel's social standing was further demonstrated by his civic duties; in April 1790, he served as a pallbearer at the funeral of Benjamin Franklin. Powel’s life and public career came to an abrupt end in 1793, when he contracted yellow fever during the catastrophic epidemic that swept Philadelphia, claiming thousands of lives.

### Twentieth-Century Alterations and Preservation

Following the death of Samuel Powel and the eventual turnover of the property, the house entered a period of decline and commercial use. By the early 20th century, the property had come into the ownership of Wolf Klebansky. Recognizing the commercial value of the building's intact 18th-century decorative woodwork, Klebansky removed key architectural features from the interior—including ornate woodwork and room paneling—and sold them: the second-floor ballroom paneling went to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York City (acquired 1918), and the first-floor rear parlor paneling went to the Philadelphia Museum of Art (acquired 1925).

By 1931, the structure was severely neglected and slated for complete demolition to make way for a commercial open-air parking garage. The threat to the building mobilized local civic leaders and early preservationists, led by Frances Wister. The successful effort to halt the demolition and purchase the building led to the founding of the Philadelphia Society for the Preservation of Landmarks and served as a catalyst for historic preservation campaigns in Center City Philadelphia, ultimately ensuring the survival of the house and laying the groundwork for the broader historic preservation movement in Society Hill.

## Architecture

The Powel House is a classic example of Georgian domestic architecture, a style characterized by symmetrical elevation, balanced proportions, and decorative detailing derived from classical precedents. Built of red brick laid in Flemish bond with contrasting stone dressings, the three-story structure features a grand entrance with a pedimented doorway and an embellished transom.

The construction was originally managed by a master builder belonging to the Carpenters' Company of Philadelphia, a prominent guild of craftsmen responsible for many of the city's landmark colonial structures. Following his purchase of the residence, Samuel Powel commissioned further interior alterations overseen by master builder and architect Robert Smith. Smith enhanced the home's interior spaces with fine Rococo-influenced plasterwork, elaborate wood carving, molded cornices, and carved mantelpieces.

The interior layout follows a traditional side-hall plan, maximizing living space and light:
* **First Floor**: Designed for formal reception, featuring a wide entry passageway, a dining room, and a rear parlor.
* **Second Floor**: Dominated by an expansive second-floor ballroom, widely regarded as one of the most sophisticated entertaining spaces in late-18th-century America. The ballroom featured plaster ceiling moldings, large light-yielding windows, and high-end woodwork designed to host banquets and dances.
* **Third Floor**: Contained private family bedchambers, outfitted with handcrafted fireplaces and detailed molding.

Though portions of the original wood ornament were displaced in 1918 and 1925 when acquired by the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Philadelphia Museum of Art, subsequent conservation projects have restored the interior space, incorporating period-appropriate woodwork and period furnishings to recreate its 18th-century aesthetic.

## Cultural significance

The Powel House holds a unique position in American political and cultural history, representing both the pinnacle of high society during the founding era and a success story of American architectural preservation. As the residence of the "Patriotic Mayor," it symbolizes the ideological transition of Philadelphia's merchant aristocracy from royal subjects under the British Crown to leading citizens of the new American Republic.

The frequent presence of key Revolutionary figures within its walls highlights its role as an informal center of diplomacy and political negotiation. The documented friendship between the Powels and the Washington family offers insight into the personal networks that sustained early American leadership.

Additionally, the Powel House is historically significant in the evolution of historic preservation in the United States:
1. **Museum Representation**: The 1918 and 1925 sales of its interior elements to the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Philadelphia Museum of Art established the Powel House's decorative arts as benchmark standards for mid-Atlantic Georgian design in American art history.
2. **Preservation Landmark**: The campaign to rescue the house from demolition in 1931, led by Frances Wister and the Philadelphia Society for the Preservation of Landmarks, demonstrated the feasibility of community-driven historic preservation, helping establish modern protocols for saving historic urban structures from commercial redevelopment.

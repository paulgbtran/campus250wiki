## Lead section

The founding of the African Methodist Episcopal Church (AME Church) in 1794 marked a landmark event in American religious, social, and political history. Originating in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, the movement began as a collective assertion of religious independence by free Black congregants resisting racial discrimination and forced segregation within white-dominated churches. Led by minister and former enslaved person Richard Allen, local Black Methodists established Mother Bethel A.M.E. Church, creating an autonomous institutional space dedicated to worship, community empowerment, and social justice.

The catalyst for the church's establishment was a dramatic walkout from St. George’s Methodist Episcopal Church in Philadelphia in November 1792. When white leaders attempted to enforce racial segregation during prayer by pulling Black worshipers off their knees in the newly constructed gallery, Allen, alongside religious leader Absalom Jones, led the Black members out of the church. Prior to the walkout, Allen and Jones had organized the Free African Society in 1787, which laid the groundwork for independent Black community leadership and places of worship across the city.

In 1793, Richard Allen purchased a parcel of land at Sixth and Lombard Streets in Philadelphia, moving a former blacksmith shop onto the site to serve as a meeting place. On July 29, 1794, Bishop Francis Asbury officially dedicated Mother Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church. Over the following two decades, Allen and his congregation fought prolonged legal battles against the white Methodist hierarchy, which attempted to control Bethel's pulpit, property, and finances. In 1816, following a decisive ruling by the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania affirming the congregation's legal autonomy, Allen convened Black Methodist leaders from neighboring states to unite into a single national body, forming the African Methodist Episcopal Church and consecrating Allen as its first bishop.

As the first fully independent African American denomination chartered in the United States, the AME Church expanded rapidly beyond Philadelphia, serving as a vital nexus for abolitionism, community aid, educational advancement, and the Underground Railroad. Today, Mother Bethel A.M.E. Church stands on the oldest parcel of real estate continuously owned by African Americans in the nation, symbolizing a legacy of Black self-determination and spiritual freedom.

## Background

In the aftermath of the American Revolutionary War, Philadelphia became home to one of the largest communities of free Black citizens in North America. The passage of Pennsylvania's Gradual Abolition Act of 1780, combined with Quaker abolitionist activism and maritime employment opportunities, attracted hundreds of formerly enslaved people seeking freedom and economic self-sufficiency. As the Black population expanded, religion played a foundational role in community life. Many free African Americans joined Methodist congregations, drawn by the movement’s early anti-slavery stances, emotional preaching, and democratic structure.

Richard Allen, born into slavery in Delaware in 1760, purchased his freedom in 1780 and became a licensed Methodist preacher. Arriving in Philadelphia in 1786, Allen began holding early morning services for Black residents at St. George’s Methodist Episcopal Church, located on Fourth Street. The popularity of Allen’s ministry led to a rapid increase in Black attendance. However, as the Black membership grew, white church leaders expressed discomfort with integrated services and sought to curtail Black leadership and access within the sanctuary.

Tensions culminated in November 1792 during a Sunday service at St. George's. Unbeknownst to the Black congregants, church trustees had instituted a policy forcing non-white members to sit in a newly constructed balcony. When Absalom Jones, William White, and Richard Allen knelt in prayer near the front of the gallery, white trustees physically interrupted them, attempting to yank Jones to his feet and demand that they move to the rear. Rather than submit to segregated worship, the Black congregants waited until prayer concluded and withdrew en masse from St. George’s Church.

Prior to the walkout, Allen and Jones had organized the Free African Society (FAS) in April 1787. Formed as a non-denominational mutual-aid organization, the FAS provided financial relief, burial assistance, and moral guidance to free Black Philadelphians. The society also demonstrated the community's capacity for civic leadership, notably when its members provided vital medical care and burial services during the 1793 Yellow Fever Epidemic. However, theological differences soon emerged within the society, as members debated whether to affiliate with the Protestant Episcopal Church or remain faithful to Methodism.

## Course of events

While Absalom Jones led a majority of the Free African Society to form the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas in 1794, Richard Allen remained deeply committed to Methodist doctrine and discipline, believing Methodism was best suited to the spiritual needs of Black Americans. In 1793, using his personal savings from his work as a chimney sweep and shoemaker, Allen purchased a plot of land at Sixth and Lombard Streets in South Philadelphia. He acquired an old wooden blacksmith shop, hauled it to the site by a team of horses, and renovated the structure into a house of worship.

On July 29, 1794, Bishop Francis Asbury of the Methodist Episcopal Church dedicated the building as Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church. Although Asbury ordained Allen as a deacon in 1799—making him the first African American ordained minister in the Methodist Church—the white-dominated Philadelphia Conference maintained that white elders held ultimate ecclesiastical authority over Bethel, including the rights to preach in its pulpit and oversee its financial assets.

Throughout the early 1800s, white Methodist elders routinely attempted to force their governance upon Bethel Church. Elders demanded keys to the property, sought to inspect church financial books, and insisted on assigning preachers without congregational consent. In several instances, white ministers threatened to lock out congregants or sell the property. Allen and the Bethel trustees resisted these incursions through legal filings, civil suits, and congregational votes.

The struggle culminated in late 1815 when a white presiding elder, the Reverend Robert Burch, attempted to force his way into Bethel's pulpit. Congregants physically blocked him from taking the altar, forcing him to leave. The legal controversy reached the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, which issued a landmark judgment in January 1816 (*Commonwealth ex rel. Bell v. Church*). The court ruled in favor of Bethel, establishing that the Black congregation held exclusive legal ownership of its property and was fully independent of the general Methodist Episcopal Church hierarchy.

Securing their independence in Philadelphia, Allen recognized the necessity of establishing a broader unified denomination to protect Black Methodist churches facing similar coercion in other regions. In April 1816, Allen convened a general convention at Bethel Church, inviting Black delegates from congregations in Pennsylvania, Maryland, Delaware, and New Jersey. On April 9, 1816, sixteen delegates gathered to form a single, unified body named the African Methodist Episcopal Church. On April 11, 1816, Richard Allen was consecrated as the church's first bishop, establishing a national administrative structure.

## Aftermath

The establishment of the African Methodist Episcopal Church created a vital institutional framework for Black social, political, and spiritual advancement in nineteenth-century America. Following its incorporation in 1816, the AME Church expanded rapidly across Northern free states, founding conferences in New York, New England, Ohio, and upper Midwestern territories. Prior to the Civil War, the church operated under severe legal restrictions in the antebellum South, though clandestinely maintained congregations in cities like Charleston and Baltimore.

Beyond its religious function, the AME Church served as a cornerstone of the abolitionist movement and Black political organizing. Mother Bethel and subsequent AME churches functioned as major hubs along the Underground Railroad, providing shelter, food, and forged freedom papers to escaping enslaved people. Bishop Allen and his wife, Sarah Allen, turned their personal home and church basements into safe houses. The church also hosted early Colored Conventions, where Black leaders assembled to protest slavery, challenge black codes, and advocate for citizenship rights.

The denomination placed strong emphasis on literacy, press freedom, and higher education. In 1848, the AME Church began publishing *The Christian Recorder*, which remains the oldest continuously published African American newspaper in the United States. In 1856, the church co-founded Wilberforce University in Ohio, the nation's first private Historically Black College and University (HBCU) owned and operated by African Americans.

Following the Civil War and the passage of the Reconstruction Amendments, AME missionaries traveled into the former Confederacy, organizing millions of newly emancipated people. By the late nineteenth century, the AME Church grew into one of the largest independent Black institutions in the world. Today, Mother Bethel A.M.E. Church in Philadelphia remains an active congregation and a designated National Historic Landmark, hosting a museum dedicated to preserving the legacy of Richard Allen and the founding of the African Methodist Episcopal Church.

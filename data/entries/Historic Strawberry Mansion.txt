## Lead section

**Historic Strawberry Mansion**, originally named *Summerville*, is a landmark historic house museum located in East Fairmount Park in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Standing on a bluff overlooking the Schuylkill River, it is the largest of the historic park mansions maintained within Fairmount Park. The house serves as a prime architectural specimen of late 18th-century Federal design combined with early 19th-century Greek Revival additions, reflecting the changing aesthetic and social ambitions of Philadelphia's socio-economic elite during the early Republic.

The property was established around 1789–1790 by Judge William Lewis, a prominent Philadelphia lawyer, state legislator, and federal judge best remembered as the primary author of Pennsylvania’s Act for the Gradual Abolition of Slavery in 1780. Lewis designed the estate as a country retreat from the urban center of Philadelphia, which was then serving as the temporary capital of the United States. Following Lewis’s death, the estate was purchased in 1821 by Joseph Hemphill, a judge and United States Congressman, who expanded the structure between 1825 and 1828 by constructing two balanced Greek Revival wings.

In the mid-19th century, following the decline of private country seats along the river, the house transitioned into public hands when the City of Philadelphia acquired the property in 1867 to form Fairmount Park. During the post-Civil War era, the mansion was leased to local concessionaires who operated a restaurant and dairy counter on the premises. The popularity of fresh strawberries and cream served to park visitors gave rise to the nickname "Strawberry Mansion," a title that eventually replaced its original name, *Summerville*, and was later adopted by the adjacent residential neighborhood in North Philadelphia.

In 1926, during the Sesquicentennial International Exposition, a coalition of prominent women formed the Committee of 1926 to restore and preserve historic structures throughout Philadelphia. The organization undertook a comprehensive restoration of Historic Strawberry Mansion, officially opening it to the public as a historic house museum in 1931. Today, the mansion remains open for public tours, educational programs, and community events, housing an extensive collection of 18th- and 19th-century American furniture, decorative arts, rare porcelain, and historic doll collections.

## History

### Construction and Early Ownership (1789–1821)
The history of Historic Strawberry Mansion began shortly after the American Revolutionary War, when Philadelphia was the intellectual, political, and financial center of the newly formed United States. In 1789, Judge William Lewis (1751–1819) acquired land along the eastern banks of the Schuylkill River. Lewis was an eminent legal scholar who had served in the Pennsylvania General Assembly and was appointed United States Attorney for the District of Pennsylvania by President George Washington in 1789, later becoming a federal judge for the United States District Court for the District of Pennsylvania.

Lewis built a two-story Federal-style country residence on the site, calling it *Summerville*. Like many wealthy Philadelphians of his era, Lewis sought refuge from the summer heat and periodic yellow fever epidemics that plagued the densely populated city core. *Summerville* provided a tranquil environment for entertaining prominent figures of the Revolutionary and Constitutional eras, including George Washington, Alexander Hamilton, and John Adams. Raised in a Quaker family, Lewis was a legal pioneer who authored Pennsylvania’s landmark 1780 legislation, the Act for the Gradual Abolition of Slavery—the first legal mechanism for gradual emancipation enacted by a democratic legislature in the Western Hemisphere. Lewis occupied *Summerville* until his death in 1819.

### Hemphill Expansion and Greek Revival Era (1821–1867)
In 1821, the estate was purchased by Judge Joseph Hemphill (1770–1842), a prominent jurist, member of the United States House of Representatives, and investor in local manufacturing enterprises. Hemphill transformed the modest Federal estate into an impressive mansion suitable for grand social entertainments. Between 1825 and 1828, he commissioned the construction of two substantial two-story wings designed in the Greek Revival style, framing the original Federal central block.

Hemphill was deeply involved in the arts and trade. He partnered with William Ellis Tucker in the development of Tucker Porcelain, the first commercially successful porcelain manufactory in the United States. Hemphill used *Summerville* to showcase American-made porcelain and fine crafts to distinguished international visitors, including the Marquis de Lafayette during his triumphal tour of the United States in 1824–1825. Following Hemphill's death and subsequent economic shifts, the estate passed through several private owners before urban expansion and environmental protection efforts reshaped the Schuylkill River corridor.

### Fairmount Park Acquisition and Naming (1867–1925)
By the mid-19th century, industrial development along the Schuylkill River posed a direct threat to Philadelphia's municipal water supply. To protect the river watershed and create public recreational space, the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania and the City of Philadelphia established Fairmount Park. In 1867, the city formally acquired *Summerville* along with surrounding country estates through eminent domain and purchase.

Under the management of the Fairmount Park Commission, the house was converted into a public amenity. During the late 1860s and 1870s, the mansion was leased to private operators who established a popular restaurant and pleasure pavilion catering to city residents, riverboat passengers, and parkgoers. The lessee, Mrs. Grimes, became famous for serving fresh strawberries harvested from nearby gardens accompanied by rich cream. The popular destination became known colloquially as the "Strawberry Mansion." Over time, this vernacular name eclipsed the historic title *Summerville*, and when the surrounding urban neighborhood developed to the east during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, it adopted the name Strawberry Mansion as well.

### Preservation and Museum Era (1926–Present)
By the early 20th century, the mansion had fallen into neglect due to decades of heavy commercial use and limited municipal maintenance funds. In 1926, in conjunction with the Sesquicentennial Exposition held in Philadelphia, a group of civic-minded women organized as the Committee of 1926 (originally the Women's Committee 1926) to preserve Pennsylvania's rich colonial and early federal heritage.

Led by Mrs. J. Willis Martin, the Committee secured a lease from the Fairmount Park Commission to restore Historic Strawberry Mansion. Over five years, the committee raised private funds, conducted historical research, repaired structural damage, and furnished the interior with authentic period antiques, decorative arts, and historical artifacts. The restored mansion officially opened to the public as a museum in 1931. The Committee of 1926 has maintained continuous stewardship of the house for nearly a century, operating it in partnership with Philadelphia Parks & Recreation.

## Architecture

```
+--------+--------------+             +----------+------------+
| Greek Revival Wing    |             | Greek Revival Wing    |

+-----------------------+             +-----------------------+
```

Historic Strawberry Mansion stands as a compelling architectural study in the transition from late 18th-century Federal design to early 19th-century Greek Revival styles:

* **Federal Core Structure (c. 1789–1790):** The central core of the house exhibits classic Federal features. Built of brick coated in smooth white stucco with subtle marble accents, the two-story main hall features a symmetrical five-bay front facade, a centered entrance framed by delicate fanlights and sidelights, and multi-pane double-hung sash windows. The hipped roof is punctuated by dormer windows and detailed classical cornices, emphasizing proportion, balance, and light.

* **Greek Revival Wings (c. 1825–1828):** The flanking two-story wings added by Joseph Hemphill introduce monumentality to the building's outline. These wings feature Greek Revival detailing, including low-pitched roofs, prominent entablatures, Doric interior molding, and floor-to-ceiling triple-hung windows designed to access the surrounding verandas. The additions doubled the floor space of the residence, providing a dedicated banquet hall and music room for hosting large gatherings.

* **Interior Layout and Collections:** The interior follows a traditional central-passage plan anchored by a grand hallway and open stairwell. The principal rooms feature restored carved mantelpieces, decorative plaster ceiling medallions, and original heart-pine flooring. The museum houses a curated collection of 18th- and 19th-century American decorative arts, including Chippendale, Hepplewhite, and Sheraton furniture, rare Tucker porcelain pieces, antique silver, and the notable Sarah Logan Wister Starr Indian Doll Collection, which features handcrafted native dolls from across the Americas.

## Cultural significance

Historic Strawberry Mansion holds a multi-faceted place in Philadelphia’s cultural and social history:

* **Legal and Abolitionist Heritage:** As the home of Judge William Lewis, the estate is directly linked to the legal foundation of American anti-slavery efforts. Lewis's authorship of the 1780 Act for the Gradual Abolition of Slavery represents a monumental milestone in human rights law, establishing Philadelphia as an early sanctuary for emancipation efforts.

* **Early American Craftsmanship and Enterprise:** Through its association with Joseph Hemphill, the mansion stands as an artifact of American industrial independence. Hemphill's promotion of Tucker Porcelain inside the house marked an early attempt to break European dominance over luxury manufactured goods in favor of American artisanal enterprise.

* **Pioneering Women-Led Historic Preservation:** The restoration of the mansion in the 1920s by the Committee of 1926 stands alongside Mount Vernon and the historic houses of Germantown as a landmark achievement in early historic preservation. The project showcased the leadership of women in safeguarding American material culture and civic heritage during the early 20th century.

* **Civic Anchor and Park Identity:** The mansion bridges the natural expanse of Fairmount Park with the residential communities of North Philadelphia. By bestowing its name upon the surrounding neighborhood, Historic Strawberry Mansion remains an enduring geographic and cultural landmark for the region.

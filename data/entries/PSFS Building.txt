### Lead Section

The **PSFS Building**, officially known today as the **Loews Philadelphia Hotel**, is a 36-story skyscraper located at 1200 Market Street in Center City, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Commissioned in 1930 by the Philadelphia Saving Fund Society (PSFS) and designed by architects George Howe and William Lescaze, the structure is widely recognized by architectural historians as the first International Style skyscraper constructed in the United States. Completed in 1932, the tower represented a radical departure from the historicist Beaux-Arts and decorative Art Deco styles that dominated early 20th-century commercial design, establishing a new paradigm for American high-rise architecture.

Rising 491 feet (150 meters) above the corner of 12th and Market Streets, the building served as the corporate headquarters for the Philadelphia Saving Fund Society—the nation's first savings bank—for six decades. Constructed during the depths of the Great Depression, the skyscraper stood as a bold statement of financial confidence and modernity. Howe and Lescaze synthesized European modernist principles with American structural engineering, utilizing a functional layout that separated office space, retail services, and vertical utility circulation into clearly articulated volume components.

Beyond its stylistic novelty, the PSFS Building was an engineering marvel for its era. It was the second fully air-conditioned skyscraper in the United States (following the 1928 Milam Building in San Antonio) and featured cutting-edge technological integrations, including custom escalators, advanced elevator banks, and an expansive second-story banking hall suspended above ground-level commercial spaces. Its exterior is characterized by a polished black granite base, vertical limestone piers, cantilevered horizontal office bands, and a 27-foot-tall red neon "PSFS" sign crowning the penthouse, which became an indelible fixture of the Philadelphia skyline.

Following the insolvency of the Philadelphia Saving Fund Society in 1992, the building underwent a comprehensive adaptive reuse project. In 2000, it reopened as the Loews Philadelphia Hotel after a sensitive multi-million-dollar restoration that preserved its historic banking hall, executive boardrooms, and landmark exterior features. Designated a National Historic Landmark in 1976, the PSFS Building remains one of Philadelphia's most celebrated architectural monuments and a fundamental landmark in the global evolution of modern architecture.

### History

#### Origins and Commissioning (1929–1930)
The Philadelphia Saving Fund Society, founded in 1816 as the first mutual savings bank in the United States, experienced substantial growth during the early 20th century. By the late 1920s, bank president James M. Willcox sought a prominent location for a new main headquarters to replace their existing facility at 700 Walnut Street. PSFS acquired the property at the southwest corner of 12th and Market Streets, situated within Philadelphia’s rapidly evolving retail and business core.

Willcox made the non-traditional decision to bypass conservative architectural firms of the era, commissioning American architect George Howe and Swiss-born architect William Lescaze, who formed the partnership Howe & Lescaze in 1929. Howe had recently left the traditional Beaux-Arts firm Mellor, Meigs & Howe to pursue modernist forms, while Lescaze brought direct experience with European avant-garde design principles. Willcox tasked the architects with designing an efficient, profitable building that maximized light and floor space while visually expressing the progressive nature of the institution.

#### Construction and Completion (1931–1932)
The design process coincided with the onset of the Great Depression following the Wall Street Crash of October 1929. Despite economic turbulence, PSFS proceeded with the ambitious project, viewing the construction as a demonstration of financial stability. Howe and Lescaze produced multiple iterations between 1929 and 1930, refining the structural framework to optimize rental revenues from both ground-floor retail units and upper-level office tenants.

Construction commenced in 1931 with the George A. Fuller Company serving as general contractor and Purdy and Henderson overseeing structural engineering. Fabrication of the steel framework proceeded at a rapid pace, utilizing advanced cantilever techniques to eliminate corner structural columns on upper office floors. The building officially opened in 1932, drawing international attention from architectural critics, engineers, and urban planners for its functional clarity and total lack of applied historical ornament.

#### Corporate Operations and Insolvency (1932–1992)
For six decades, the PSFS Building operated as the operational hub of the bank, with its massive second-floor banking room serving thousands of daily depositors. The upper floors served as office spaces rented to legal, corporate, and government tenants, providing a steady stream of income. The building’s mechanical systems, including its groundbreaking central air-conditioning, were continuously modernized to maintain high commercial occupancy.

During the Savings and Loan Crisis of the late 1980s and early 1990s, PSFS expanded aggressively into commercial lending and corporate real estate ventures under its parent corporation, Meritor Financial. The institution suffered catastrophic financial losses, leading to its seizure by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) in 1992. The bank's assets were liquidated, and the landmark building was put up for sale, leading to a period of uncertainty during which portions of the interior sat vacant.

#### Adaptive Reuse: The Loews Hotel (1997–Present)
In the late 1990s, the developer Rubin Company acquired the building with plans to convert the commercial structure into a premier convention hotel to support the nearby Pennsylvania Convention Center. Designed by the architectural firms Bower Lewis Thrower Architects and Arthur Cotton Moore Associates, a $115 million adaptive reuse project began in 1997.

The rehabilitation was structured under strict federal guidelines to maintain the building's historic integrity. The double-height banking hall on the second floor was converted into a grand ballroom, preserving its original marble cladding and custom clock fixtures. Executive boardrooms on the 33rd floor were restored for high-end meetings and events, while upper office floors were converted into 581 hotel rooms. The building officially reopened in April 2000 as the Loews Philadelphia Hotel.

#### Overall Form and Structural Layout
The PSFS Building is designed around an asymmetrical, functional grouping of volumes that reflects the internal use of space—a foundational tenet of the International Style. Howe and Lescaze discarded the traditional tripartite skyscraper model (base, shaft, capital decorated with cornices and column orders) in favor of a structural T-plan tower rising above a wide base.

The building is organized into three primary visual components:
1. **The Base**: A polished black granite foundation enclosing ground-floor retail shops and the main entrance lobby.
2. **The Mezzanine and Banking Hall**: A cantilevered podium featuring floor-to-ceiling glass ribbon walls that illuminate the elevated banking hall.
3. **The Office Tower**: A vertical shaft clad in gray Indiana limestone piers that project outward, emphasizing verticality, contrasted by recessed dark brick and aluminum spandrels that form continuous horizontal bands.

On the building's southern elevation, a distinct vertical service tower clad in dark matte brick houses the elevator shafts, stairwells, and utility risers, visually separating the building's mechanical core from its open office spaces.

#### Exterior Materials and Fenestration
The exterior materials were selected for their textural contrast, durability, and modern aesthetic. The lower three stories are faced with polished Swedish black granite, providing a solid grounding effect. Above the base, the double-height second floor opens to the street via broad expanses of plate glass framed in matte-finish stainless steel and aluminum.

The upper 30 stories are wrapped in alternating bands of gray Indiana limestone and continuous horizontal ribbon windows. The structural steel frame allowed the office floors to be cantilevered beyond the primary columns, eliminating thick corner masonry piers and providing unobstructed interior natural light.

#### Interior Space and Furnishings
The interior of the PSFS Building represented a total work of art (*Gesamtkunstwerk*), in which every functional detail was custom designed by Howe and Lescaze. 

* **Banking Hall**: Located on the second floor to maximize valuable ground-floor retail rentals, the banking room featured a soaring 30-foot ceiling, columns wrapped in polished black granite, and walls lined with rare imported marbles, including French polished limestone and Italian marble.
* **Vertical Transportation**: The entrance lobby featured high-speed escalators clad in mirror-finish metal and black glass, transporting visitors directly up to the main banking hall.
* **Custom Appointments**: Custom furnishings, lighting fixtures, stainless steel teller counters, and signage were crafted specifically for the building. Special timepieces created by the French jewelry house Cartier were integrated into every elevator lobby and major public space.
* **Air Conditioning Systems**: It was engineered as the first large-scale skyscraper featuring fully integrated central air conditioning, utilizing custom ductwork hidden within structural ceiling plenums.

#### The Rooftop Penthouse and Neon Sign
The tower is surmounted by a multi-level mechanical penthouse that originally contained an executive solarium, dining room, and boardroom suite. Resting atop the penthouse structure is the building's signature feature: a 27-foot-tall red neon sign displaying the letters **PSFS**. Arranged vertically and horizontally on a steel frame, the sign was visible for tens of miles across the Delaware Valley, serving as both a corporate logo and a beacon for low-flying aircraft.

### Cultural Significance

#### Pioneering International Style in America
The PSFS Building holds a foundational position in American architectural history as the first realized International Style skyscraper. Prior to its construction, American high-rise design was defined by historicist styles (such as the Woolworth Building's Gothic Revival motifs) or decorative Art Deco setbacks (such as the Chrysler Building and Empire State Building). Howe and Lescaze demonstrated that modern European avant-garde theories—advocated by figures like Le Corbusier and Walter Gropius—could be successfully applied to large-scale American commercial real estate.

In 1932, the building was prominently featured in the influential Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) exhibition *Modern Architecture: International Exhibition*, curated by Philip Johnson and Henry-Russell Hitchcock. The exhibition popularized the term "International Style" and cemented the PSFS Building’s reputation as the premier American exemplar of the movement.

#### Impact on the Philadelphia Skyline
For more than half a century, the PSFS Building stood as one of the defining silhouettes of the Philadelphia skyline, paired alongside the historic tower of Philadelphia City Hall. Until the construction of One Liberty Place in 1987 broke the informal gentleman's agreement restricting building heights above the statue of William Penn, the PSFS Building was one of the tallest commercial structures in the city. Its illuminated red neon sign became a cherished cultural icon for generations of Philadelphians.

#### Preservation Model for Corporate Modernism
The successful adaptation of the PSFS Building into the Loews Philadelphia Hotel in 2000 set an international standard for the historic preservation of mid-century corporate modernism. At a time when many mid-century structures faced demolition due to mechanical obsolescence or changing commercial tastes, the adaptive reuse demonstrated that functional modern skyscrapers could be rehabilitated for 21st-century commercial vitality while retaining their core historical and architectural fabric.

### 2. Lead section

During the nineteenth century, Philadelphia transformed from an Atlantic port city into a central hub of early American industrialization, often termed the "Workshop of the World." This economic expansion attracted massive waves of foreign-born immigrants—primarily from Ireland, Germany, and Great Britain—alongside an expanding population of free African Americans. As the city's demographic makeup shifted rapidly, long-standing Anglo-Protestant elites and native-born working-class residents responded with intense hostility. Nativism—a political and social ideology seeking to preserve the dominance of native-born citizens over foreign immigrants—became a defining force in Philadelphia's social, political, and spatial landscape throughout the antebellum and post-Civil War eras.

Tensions escalated dramatically in the 1830s and 1840s, driven by economic panic, labor competition, and acute sectarian friction between Protestants and Roman Catholics. A major flashpoint emerged over public education when Roman Catholic leaders, led by Bishop Francis P. Kenrick, challenged mandatory readings of the Protestant King James Bible in municipal public schools. The resulting anti-Catholic hysteria culminated in the Philadelphia Nativist Riots of May and July 1844. These violent conflicts in the outlying districts of Kensington and Southwark involved armed battles, the arson of Catholic churches and institutions, and multiple fatalities, necessitating the imposition of martial law by state militia forces under Brigadier General George Cadwalader.

The political fallout of this anti-immigrant unrest permanently reconfigured local and state governance. Nativists organized formally under political banners such as the American Republican Party and the American Party (popularly known as the "Know-Nothings"). Leaders like newspaper editor Lewis C. Levin, sheriff and later mayor Morton McMichael, and Mayor Robert T. Conrad leveraged anti-Catholic and anti-foreign rhetoric to capture municipal offices and seats in the United States Congress. Conrad and his allies co-opted Revolutionary iconography—using spaces like Independence Hall to project an ideology of "virtuous" native citizenship—and implemented patronage policies that filled municipal posts with nativist sympathizers.

Crucially, the lawlessness of the 1844 riots exposed the vulnerability of Philadelphia County’s administrative fragmentation, which was divided among twenty-nine independent townships, boroughs, and districts. In response, Pennsylvania lawmakers enacted the Consolidation Act of 1854, expanding the boundaries of the City of Philadelphia to match those of the entire county. This administrative overhaul was followed in 1855 by the creation of one of the nation's earliest unified, professional police forces. As the century progressed, nativist sentiment adapted to new waves of arrivals, shifting focus toward anti-Asian discrimination and Southern and Eastern European restriction, leaving an indelible imprint on the city's institutional development.

### 3. Origins

The historical antecedents of nineteenth-century nativism in Philadelphia trace back to colonial Pennsylvania. Although founder William Penn established the colony on principles of religious tolerance, the arrival of non-English populations quickly sparked ethnic anxieties among the city's ruling elites. By the 1720s, substantial numbers of German-speaking immigrants had settled in Germantown and surrounding areas, eventually accounting for nearly one-third of the region's total population. English elites expressed acute fear over the political and cultural influence of German voters, who frequently formed alliances with pacifist Quakers. Prominent Philadelphians voiced explicit anti-German prejudice; Benjamin Franklin famously argued in his 1751 essay, *Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind*, that German arrivals were of inferior stock who would "Germanize us instead of our Anglifying them." Political opposition turned violent during colonial elections, such as an incident in 1742 when Anglican-aligned sailors physically assaulted German voters to prevent them from casting ballots.

Political and ideological anti-immigrant sentiment intensified following the American Revolution. In the 1790s, political instability in Europe—including the French Revolution and the suppressed 1798 Irish Rebellion—sent thousands of foreign exiles seeking asylum in Philadelphia, which then served as the national capital. Federalist politicians viewed incoming French radicals and Irish refugees as subversive elements aligned with Democratic-Republican opponents. National security concerns during the Quasi-War with France prompted the Federalist-dominated United States Congress to pass the Naturalization Act of 1798 as part of the controversial Alien and Sedition Acts. This law dramatically increased the residency requirement for U.S. citizenship from five years to fourteen years and temporarily barred newly arrived immigrants from voting, codifying nativism into federal law.

To manage the physical entry of immigrants and protect the city from tropical disease outbreaks such as yellow fever, the Pennsylvania Board of Health established the Lazaretto quarantine station in 1799. Situated along the Delaware River in Tinicum Township, roughly eight miles south of Philadelphia, the Lazaretto required incoming ships to submit to rigorous medical inspections, holding suspected cases in isolation hospitals until its closure in 1893. 

Despite its early prominence as a primary port of arrival, Philadelphia’s direct role in maritime immigration began to wane after the War of 1812. Transatlantic trade increasingly shifted toward New York City, whose deep-water harbor offered direct access to Europe and, following the completion of the Erie Canal in 1825, a streamlined route to the American interior. Consequently, Philadelphia's share of total national immigrant arrivals fell from approximately 12 percent in the 1820s to just 4.5 percent by the 1850s. Nevertheless, overall numbers of foreign-born residents within Philadelphia swelled as immigrants arrived overland via expanding regional canal systems and newly built trunk lines such as the Philadelphia and Columbia Railroad (1832) and New Jersey's Camden & Amboy Railway (1834).

### 4. Development

Between 1830 and 1860, Philadelphia experienced explosive population growth driven by European economic pressures, the Irish Potato Famine (1845–1850), and the political upheavals of the Revolutions of 1848. The population within Philadelphia County expanded from approximately 100,000 in 1815 to over 400,000 in 1850, reaching 565,000 by 1860 following political consolidation. By 1860, foreign-born residents constituted a massive portion of the city's populace, with Irish-born immigrants accounting for 16.7 percent and German-born residents making up 7.5 percent. Because early-to-mid nineteenth-century Philadelphia operated largely as a "walking city," where workers resided within walking distance of employment sites, diverse socioeconomic and ethnic groups settled in close spatial proximity.

This rapid demographic realignment exacerbated existing labor, racial, and religious rivalries. Unskilled Irish labor competed directly with native-born white workers and the city's growing free Black population—which had expanded dramatically following Pennsylvania's gradual abolition act of 1780 and southern migration. Tensions erupted into violent street conflicts, including the anti-Black Lombard Street Riot of August 1842, in which predominantly Irish working-class mobs attacked an African American temperance parade. Concurrently, intra-Irish sectarian divisions surfaced. In July 1831, street fighting erupted between Irish Protestants celebrating the Battle of the Boyne and Irish Catholic counter-demonstrators, establishing a pattern of public violent contestation between religious groups. German communities also experienced internal divides over language and assimilation; disputes within Lutheran congregations over English usage led to litigation in 1815, while organizations such as the German Society of Pennsylvania actively advocated for the preservation of German language and culture, petitioning the state legislature to publish laws in German.

The most severe breakdown of civil order occurred in the spring and summer of 1844, sparked by a dispute over public education. In late 1842, Roman Catholic Bishop Francis P. Kenrick addressed a letter to the Philadelphia school board requesting that Catholic public school students be permitted to read from the Catholic Douay Bible rather than the Protestant King James Version, and that Catholic pupils be excused from Protestant religious instruction. Protestant clergy and nativist organizations, including the American Protestant Association and the American Republican Party, seized upon the appeal to fan anti-Catholic fear, claiming that foreign Papists were attempting to subvert American republican institutions and remove the Bible from schools.

On May 3, 1844, the American Republican Party staged an outdoor political rally in Kensington, an industrial suburb with a dense Irish Catholic population. Heckling and physical fights escalated into gunshots. Three days later, on May 6, renewed rallies degenerated into open violence; nineteen-year-old nativist George Shifler was killed, becoming an instant martyr for the nativist movement. Over the next three days, nativist mobs rampaged through Kensington, burning dozens of homes, a Catholic school, and two major Catholic churches: St. Michael's and St. Augustine's (the latter located at Fourth and Vine Streets). Order was temporarily restored only after Sheriff Morton McMichael and county militia units intervened. 

Violent unrest reignited two months later in Southwark. On July 5, 1844, a nativist crowd discovered that weapons were being stored for defense inside the Church of St. Philip Neri. A mob besieged the building, eventually acquiring cannon and exchanging artillery and small-arms fire with state militia troops commanded by Brigadier General George Cadwalader. The Southwark riot resulted in roughly a dozen deaths and scores of injuries before militia reinforcements quelled the rebellion on July 8 under a mandate of martial law.

### 5. Impact

The destruction and loss of life during the 1844 riots catalyzed far-reaching municipal administrative reforms. Before 1854, Philadelphia County was fractured into twenty-nine distinct self-governing municipalities, boroughs, and townships, each maintaining its own understaffed constabulary. This fragmentation routinely allowed mobs to escape across jurisdictional boundaries, leaving police forces incapable of handling major urban unrest. To restore public order, the Pennsylvania General Assembly enacted the Consolidation Act of 1854, which subsumed all outlying districts—such as Kensington, Southwark, Moyamensing, and Northern Liberties—into one single corporate entity under the City of Philadelphia. In 1855, Mayor Robert T. Conrad established a centralized, professional police department empowered to patrol immigrant enclaves and systematically enforce municipal authority.

Politicized nativism enjoyed substantial electoral success during the mid-nineteenth century. In the immediate wake of the 1844 riots, voters elected American Party candidates Lewis C. Levin and John Hull Campbell to the United States House of Representatives. Levin, editor of the *Daily Sun*, used his congressional platform to advocate for extending the naturalization requirement for U.S. citizenship to twenty-one years and banning foreign-born residents from holding public office. In 1854, Whig and Know-Nothing coalition candidate Robert T. Conrad was elected Mayor of Philadelphia, while Jacob Broom secured a U.S. House seat on an explicit nativist ticket. Conrad actively utilized patronage to staff city departments with nativist supporters, convening City Council in its existing chambers on the second floor of Independence Hall (which had housed council meetings since 1799) to visually link the nativist platform with the legacy of the American Revolution. By the late 1850s, as the Know-Nothing party dissolved over national slavery debates, many Philadelphia nativists migrated into the newly established Republican Party, which incorporated anti-immigrant planks into its local political platforms to secure working-class white voters. Former sheriff Morton McMichael won election in 1866 as Philadelphia’s first Republican mayor, maintaining traditional nativist sensibilities throughout his administration.

In the post-Civil War era, nativist hostility adapted to new target groups. As Chinese immigration to the United States increased during the mid-to-late nineteenth century, local nativists shifted focus toward Chinese arrivals. Congress passed the Page Act of 1875 and the landmark Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882, prohibiting the entry of Chinese laborers. Displaced from western states by anti-Asian violence, a small Chinese community established an enclave along the 900 block of Race Street in Philadelphia, establishing laundries, restaurants, and grocery stores. Philadelphia police subjected Chinatown to frequent raids and intense surveillance under the pretense of code enforcement and moral control. 

These nineteenth-century dynamics established structural patterns of exclusion, municipal policing, and ethnic political organizing that continued to shape Philadelphia throughout the twentieth century, setting precedent for later federal restriction laws such as the Johnson-Reed Act of 1924.

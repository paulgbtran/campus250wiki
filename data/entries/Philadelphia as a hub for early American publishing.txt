## Lead section

Between 1750 and 1800, Philadelphia established itself as the preeminent center for book printing, publishing, and periodical production in the United States, surpassing the earlier publishing strongholds of Boston and New York. Supported by its growing port economy, rapid population expansion, and a high concentration of intellectual and political organizations—such as the American Philosophical Society and the University of Pennsylvania—the city served as the informational and cultural capital of the early American republic. Philadelphia’s printers and publishers transformed the regional print culture from small, multi-purpose artisan shops into a highly specialized, industrialized market that shaped national public discourse and literary taste.

The foundations of this industry were laid in the late seventeenth century by pioneer printers like William Bradford, but it was Benjamin Franklin who created the prototype for early American publishing networks. In the eighteenth century, Franklin used his print shop and bindery on Market Street, along with his widely circulated *Pennsylvania Gazette* and *Poor Richard’s Almanac*, to finance a system of printer-apprentices throughout the Atlantic colonies. Following the American Revolution, ambitious projects such as Thomas Dobson's first American edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* demonstrated Philadelphia's technical and financial capacity for large-scale production.

In the early nineteenth century, the distinct roles of printer, bookseller, and publisher began to separate. Innovators like Irish immigrant Mathew Carey established modern publishing models, introducing specialized trade catalogs, national distribution networks, and door-to-door subscription peddling. Concurrently, technological advances—including steam-powered presses, stereotyping, and rotary presses—fueled an explosion of mass media. Philadelphia became a national trendsetter in penny daily newspapers such as the *Public Ledger* and influential literary and women's magazines, including *Godey’s Lady’s Book*, *Graham’s Magazine*, *Lippincott’s Magazine*, and later, Cyrus H.K. Curtis’s *Ladies’ Home Journal* and *Saturday Evening Post*.

Although New York eventually surpassed Philadelphia in trade publishing during the mid-nineteenth century, Philadelphia retained its dominance in specialized sectors such as medical publishing, religious texts, subscription reference works, and academic literature. Surrounding Washington Square, the district known as "Publishers Row" housed industry giants like J.B. Lippincott & Co. and Lea & Febiger well into the twentieth century. Philadelphia's early history as a publishing hub laid the groundwork for the modern American print industry, leaving a legacy preserved today by scholarly presses, independent imprints, and digital literary media.

## Origins

The printing industry in the Delaware Valley originated in the late seventeenth century when English emigrant William Bradford established Philadelphia’s first printing press in 1685. For several decades, Bradford was the city's sole press operator. In colonial British America, early printers performed multiple roles to remain solvent: they operated print shops, bound volumes, published local broadsides, pamphlets, newspapers, and almanacs, and ran retail stores selling stationery and imported British books. Publishing original books was rare and financially risky due to high paper and labor costs and an uncertain return on capital. Consequently, colonial printers primarily imported bound volumes from Great Britain while relying on fee-based job printing for local government and merchant clients to supply their steady income.

Benjamin Franklin revolutionized this business model after arriving in Philadelphia in the 1720s. Operating a print shop and bindery at 320 Market Street, Franklin recognized that long-term commercial success required steady revenue streams and access to broader geographic markets. In 1729, he acquired the *Pennsylvania Gazette*, expanding its news coverage and entertainment value to build a reliable advertising base. In 1732, he began issuing *Poor Richard’s Almanac*, which gained a vast audience across the Atlantic seaboard for its weather forecasts, maxims, and practical wisdom. Franklin reinvested his profits into an early colonial publishing franchise, training apprentices and financing their print shops in distant towns. This network distributed books and periodicals financed or printed by Franklin, including Samuel Richardson’s novel *Pamela* (1744)—the first novel printed in America—and James Logan’s translation of Cicero’s *Cato Major* (1744).

Other regional printers also carved out niche markets in the eighteenth century. In Germantown, Christopher Saur established a German-language press in 1738 to serve the influx of German immigrants, publishing German Bibles, devotional materials, and periodicals. In Burlington and later Trenton, New Jersey, Philadelphia-trained apprentice Isaac Collins built a press specializing in religious literature and educational textbooks. 

The end of the Revolutionary period brought even more ambitious publishing endeavors to Philadelphia. From 1789 to 1798, Scottish immigrant printer Thomas Dobson undertook the printing and publishing of the first American edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. Comprising eighteen multi-volume texts with custom-engraved copper plates, the project relied on a national subscription drive that included prominent patrons such as George Washington and Thomas Jefferson. Although financial difficulties prevented Dobson from securing large profits, the enterprise proved that American pressmen could successfully execute complex, multi-volume publishing efforts without relying on European imports.

## Development

Following the American Revolution, Philadelphia experienced a rapid surge in press operators, growing from nine printing shops in 1740 to fifty-one by 1785. The city's geography provided easy access to coastal shipping lines and river systems along the Delaware and Schuylkill, while expanding road networks linked it directly to interior markets in the American South and West. Furthermore, Philadelphia’s temporary role as the federal capital from 1790 to 1800 catalyzed the growth of political journalism and partisan daily newspapers, such as Benjamin Franklin Bache's *Philadelphia Aurora*.

The early nineteenth century marked a structural shift as the formerly unified roles of printer, bookseller, and publisher separated into distinct professions. Mathew Carey is widely regarded as Philadelphia’s first modern trade publisher. An Irish political refugee who arrived in 1784, Carey stepped away from manual presswork to concentrate on editorial acquisition, financial capitalization, and national distribution. Carey capitalized on popular British and American fiction, publishing Susanna Rowson’s bestseller *Charlotte Temple* in 1794. He published the first American atlas and the first American Roman Catholic Bible, and he pioneered the use of itinerant book peddlers—most notably Mason Locke Weems—to sell volumes door-to-door in southern and western states. In the 1820s, Carey passed his firm to his son Henry Charles Carey and son-in-law Isaac Lea. Facing competition in popular fiction from New York houses like Harper Brothers, Carey & Lea shifted their focus toward medical and scientific publishing, an area well suited to Philadelphia’s growing medical schools and research hospitals. The enterprise eventually evolved into the medical publishing house Lea & Febiger.

Institutional, religious, and subscription publishing also flourished during this era. Organizations funded by national donations established headquarters in the city: the Philadelphia Bible Society formed in 1809, and the American Sunday School Union followed in 1824, distributing millions of religious tracts and children's books across the country. Commercial firms like J.B. Lippincott & Co. (founded in 1836 by Joshua B. Lippincott) published religious books, Bibles, and legal texts before expanding into medical literature, gift books, and wholesale book distribution. By the late nineteenth century, Lippincott operated a production plant at 715 Market Street capable of printing and binding two thousand volumes daily. Door-to-door subscription houses, including T. Ellwood Zell, Gebbie and Barrie, and the National Publishing Company, also made Philadelphia a national center for subscription sales.

Women made notable contributions to the early Philadelphia book trade, often stepping in to run businesses after the deaths of their husbands. Jane Aitken operated a press in the early 1800s and published the first American translation of the Septuagint Bible in 1808. Lydia Bailey served as the official printer for the City of Philadelphia for decades, executing major printing contracts for Mathew Carey and municipal agencies while employing dozens of pressmen. Later in the nineteenth century, female publishers established focused imprints, such as Florence I. Duncan and Mary R. Heygate Hall's firm (Duncan & Hall), and Louise C. Boname, who published educational language manuals.

Technological innovations during the 1820s through the 1850s transformed the capacity of Philadelphia presses. The introduction of steam-powered iron presses and stereotyping—the process of casting solid metal plates from papier-mâché or plaster molds of set type—allowed publishers to store plates for future printings. This innovation reduced composition costs and minimized capital risk. Steam presses enabled the rise of cheap daily penny papers, starting with Dr. Christopher Columbus Conwell’s short-lived *The Cent* (1830) and William M. Swain’s highly successful *Public Ledger* (1836). By the 1850s, Swain had installed Richard March Hoe's type-revolving rotary press, capable of producing more than twenty thousand newspaper copies per hour.

These printing capabilities also powered a golden age for American magazines. Early literary miscellanies like Joseph Dennie's Federalist-leaning *Port Folio* (1801–1827) relied on a small group of educated contributors known as the Tuesday Club. By the 1840s, larger commercial magazines emerged. George Rex Graham’s *Graham’s Magazine* hired full-time editors like Edgar Allan Poe and paid generous rates for original work by authors like Bryant, Cooper, and Longfellow. Louis A. Godey and long-time editor Sarah Josepha Hale built *Godey’s Lady’s Book* into a dominant national monthly magazine. Combining literature, art, advice columns, and hand-tinted fashion plates, *Godey’s* reached a pre-Civil War circulation exceeding 150,000. 

By the late nineteenth century, Cyrus H.K. Curtis elevated magazine publishing into an international enterprise. Founded in 1883 and edited by his wife Louisa Knapp and later Edward Bok, Curtis's *Ladies’ Home Journal* integrated low subscription pricing with heavy corporate advertising, becoming the first American magazine to hit one million subscribers in 1904. Curtis reinvested these profits to buy the *Saturday Evening Post* in 1897, turning it into a popular general-interest weekly known for its fiction, journalistic reporting, and cover illustrations by Norman Rockwell. In 1910, Curtis completed a massive Georgian Revival headquarters overlooking Washington Square, anchoring Philadelphia's "Publishers Row."

## Impact

Philadelphia’s rise as an early publishing hub shaped the intellectual, cultural, and political landscape of the new nation. By creating a commercial market for American literature, Philadelphia publishers helped free native writers from reliance on European models and patron funding. Periodicals published in the city established national standards for journalism, literary criticism, and popular culture, helping forge a shared American identity across diverse regional populations.

The concentration of major print operations around Washington Square led to the development of "Publishers Row." Located along South Sixth Street and its surrounding blocks, this district brought together editorial offices, print shops, binderies, and distribution centers for firms like J.B. Lippincott & Co., Lea & Febiger, W.B. Saunders, David McKay, and the Curtis Publishing Company. Industrial advancements continued through the mid-twentieth century; in 1946, Curtis constructed a production plant in Sharon Hill, Pennsylvania, featuring four-color "perfecting" presses that could print paper rolls on both sides simultaneously.

Philadelphia’s press culture also supported a diverse range of ethnic and specialized publications catering to the region's immigrant communities. Following Franklin's early *Philadelphische Zeitung* (1732), the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw the rise of long-running foreign-language papers, including the Italian *Voce Della Colonia*, the Polish *Patryota*, the German *Philadelphia Tageblatt*, and the Yiddish *Idishe Welt*. The *Philadelphia Tribune*, founded in 1884 by Christopher James Perry, became a vital voice for the region's African American community and is the nation's oldest continuously published African American newspaper.

In the post-World War II period, national media consolidation and changing demographics altered Philadelphia’s publishing industry. New York City gradually became the nation's primary capital for general trade publishing. Local newspapers faced increased competition from suburban dailies—such as the *Bucks County Courier Times*, the *Burlington County Times*, and the Camden *Courier-Post*—which captured suburban readers and advertising revenues. This shift contributed to the closure of notable metropolitan dailies, including the *Evening Bulletin* in 1982. Many historic commercial publishing houses merged with or were acquired by international media conglomerates during the late twentieth century; Wolters Kluwer acquired J.B. Lippincott and Lea & Febiger, merging them into Lippincott Williams & Wilkins.

Despite these shifts, Philadelphia’s print heritage endured through university presses and specialized independent publishing houses. The University of Pennsylvania Press (established in 1890 and expanded in 1927 under editor Phelps Soule), Temple University Press (1969), Rutgers University Press (1936), and Saint Joseph’s University Press (1997) became key publishers of academic scholarship and regional history. Independent publishers like Running Press (founded in 1972) and Quirk Books (2002) built global audiences for non-fiction, trade titles, and best-selling fiction. Additionally, poetry and literary journals such as the *American Poetry Review* (founded in 1972), *Painted Bride Quarterly*, *Philadelphia Stories*, and various digital academic journals continue the city's long-standing tradition of literary publication.

## Lead section

**John Brendan Kelly Sr.** (October 4, 1889 – June 20, 1960), known colloquially as "Jack Kelly," was an American Olympic champion rower, self-made bricklaying entrepreneur, and prominent Philadelphia civic and political figure. Recognized as one of the most decorated scullers in the history of American rowing, Kelly became the first rower in history to win three Olympic gold medals, dominating the single and double sculls events during the 1920 Antwerp and 1924 Paris Olympic Games.

Beyond his athletic achievements, Kelly constructed a vast commercial empire in Philadelphia. Rising from working-class origins in the city's East Falls neighborhood, he established John B. Kelly, Inc., which grew to become the largest bricklaying and masonry contracting enterprise on the East Coast. His ubiquitous business signboards bearing the slogan "Kelly - Brickwork Contractor" became a staple across construction sites in the Mid-Atlantic region, cementing his status as an industrial powerhouse.

Kelly was equally active in public service and local political life. A major figure in the revitalization of the Philadelphia Democratic Party during the New Deal era, he ran a high-profile campaign for Mayor of Philadelphia in 1935, mounting a strong challenge in a historically Republican stronghold. He later served as President of the Fairmount Park Commission and was appointed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt as the National Director of Physical Fitness during World War II.

As the patriarch of the prominent Kelly family of Philadelphia, his legacy extended through his children, including Olympic medalist and sports administrator John B. Kelly Jr. and Academy Award–winning actress Grace Kelly, who became Princess of Monaco upon her marriage to Prince Rainier III in 1956. Kelly is commemorated in Philadelphia by a prominent bronze sculpture situated along the Schuylkill River near Boathouse Row, commissioned by the city and maintained under the stewardship of the Association for Public Art.

## Early life and education

John Brendan Kelly was born on October 4, 1889, in the working-class East Falls neighborhood of Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. He was one of ten children born to John Henry Kelly and Mary Anne Costello, Irish Roman Catholic immigrants who had emigrated from County Mayo, Ireland. Raised in a modest household, Kelly was taught the values of physical labor, self-reliance, and fierce personal ambition from an early age. 

He attended the local public schools of Philadelphia, though his formal education was limited as he entered the workforce early to help support his large family. At the age of sixteen, Kelly secured an apprenticeship as a bricklayer under his uncle, Patrick Kelly. While learning the trade on construction sites throughout the city, he developed exceptional physical strength and endurance.

Outside of his working hours, Kelly threw himself into athletic pursuits. He excelled in track and field, basketball, and football, but found his true passion on the waters of the Schuylkill River. Joining the Vesper Boat Club—one of the historic rowing organizations comprising Philadelphia’s renowned Boathouse Row—Kelly began rigorous training routines that would quickly elevate him to the pinnacle of competitive sculling.

During World War I, Kelly put his athletic and business ambitions on hold to enlist in the United States Army. He served as a lieutenant and was stationed at Camp Meade in Maryland. While in uniform, he engaged in military boxing tournaments, winning 12 out of 13 fights before receiving an honorable discharge at the conclusion of the war and returning to Philadelphia.

## Career

### Athletic Achievements and Olympic Triumph

Kelly’s rowing career was defined by unmatched dominance in single and double sculls. Between 1919 and 1920, Kelly achieved an extraordinary streak, winning 126 consecutive races in the single sculls, setting a record in modern amateur rowing. 

Despite his dominant national standing, Kelly was embroiled in a famous international sports controversy in 1920. He applied to compete in the prestigious Diamond Challenge Sculls at the Henley Royal Regatta in England, but his entry was rejected by the regatta’s governing body. The official reasoning cited the Stewards’ strict amateur rules, which barred anyone "who is or has been by trade or employment a mechanic, artisan, or labourer"—a class-based restriction that disqualified Kelly due to his early work as a bricklayer. Additionally, the rules excluded members of the Vesper Boat Club due to a prior dispute over amateur status involving the club’s 1905 crew. 

Undeterred by the rejection, Kelly redirected his focus to the 1920 Summer Olympics in Antwerp, Belgium. In the single sculls event, he advanced to the final and defeated his British rival, Jack Beresford (the recipient of the Henley title that year), in one of the closest and most grueling races in Olympic history, winning by less than a second. Barely a half-hour after his single sculls victory, Kelly teamed up with his cousin Paul Costello to win the gold medal in the double sculls event, achieving an unprecedented double victory.

At the 1924 Summer Olympics in Paris, Kelly and Costello successfully defended their Olympic title in the double sculls, taking the gold medal once again. With this victory, Kelly became the first person to win three gold medals in rowing.

### Business and Enterprise

Utilizing a $7,000 loan secured from his brothers, Kelly founded his own masonry contracting firm, **John B. Kelly, Inc.**, in 1919. Combining a fierce work ethic with shrewd business acumen, he built the company into an industrial powerhouse during the Mid-Atlantic construction boom of the 1920s and 1930s.

The firm's signature blue-and-white signs—reading simply "Kelly - Brickwork Contractor"—appeared on major commercial, institutional, and residential building sites throughout Philadelphia, New York, and Washington, D.C. Under Kelly's management, the business grew into the largest bricklaying enterprise on the East Coast, turning Kelly into a self-made multi-millionaire. His enterprise provided structural masonry for landmark buildings, hospitals, university facilities, and government structures.

### Politics and Public Service

Kelly was a transformative figure in Philadelphia’s political landscape during the mid-20th century. Historically, the city had been dominated by a powerful Republican political machine. Kelly emerged as a central leader within the Philadelphia Democratic Party, bringing organizational discipline and financial backing to the party apparatus.

In 1935, Kelly accepted the Democratic nomination for Mayor of Philadelphia. Running on a platform of municipal reform and New Deal-style civic investments, he came remarkably close for a Democrat at the time, losing to Republican candidate S. Davis Wilson by approximately 4,600 votes (receiving 374,570 votes to Wilson's 379,216).

Despite the defeat, Kelly remained a trusted political heavyweight. He served as Chairman of the Philadelphia Democratic County Committee and cultivated a close working relationship with President Franklin D. Roosevelt. In 1941, Roosevelt appointed Kelly as the National Director of Physical Fitness under the Office of Civilian Defense, a wartime post in which he promoted physical education, youth sports, and readiness across the nation.

In local governance, Kelly served for years as the President of the Fairmount Park Commission, where he championed the expansion, preservation, and public recreation amenities of Philadelphia’s vast park system, particularly along his beloved Schuylkill River.

## Personal life

In 1924, Kelly married Margaret Katherine Majer (1898–1990), a former competitive swimmer, model, and physical educator who was the first female faculty member to direct women's athletics at the University of Pennsylvania. The couple established their primary residence at a grand estate on Henry Avenue in East Falls, Philadelphia. 

The couple had four children:
1. **Margaret "Peggy" Kelly** (1925–1991)
2. **John B. Kelly Jr.** (1927–1985), who followed in his father's footsteps as a world-class sculler, winning the Diamond Challenge Sculls at Henley in 1947 and 1949, an Olympic bronze medal in 1956, and serving as President of the United States Olympic Committee.
3. **Grace Patricia Kelly** (1929–1982), who achieved international fame as an Academy Award–winning Hollywood actress before marrying Prince Rainier III of Monaco in 1956 to become Princess Grace of Monaco.
4. **Elizabeth "Lizzanne" Kelly** (1933–2009).

Kelly was known for his charismatic personality, deep devotion to civic causes, and disciplined approach to family life. He maintained an active lifestyle throughout his adulthood. 

Kelly died on June 20, 1960, at the age of 70, following surgery for intestinal cancer at Graduate Hospital in Philadelphia. His funeral was attended by civic leaders, sports figures, and international dignitaries. He was interred at Holy Sepulchre Cemetery in Cheltenham Township, Pennsylvania.

## Legacy

John B. Kelly Sr.'s legacy endures across multiple spheres of American cultural, athletic, and civic life. He remains a central figure in the athletic heritage of Philadelphia and the broader history of rowing in the United States. In 1956, Kelly was inducted into the National Rowing Hall of Fame, and he was later named to the U.S. Olympic Hall of Fame.

Kelly's impact on Philadelphia’s physical and cultural infrastructure remains visible today. In 1967, the city honored his contributions by dedicating a life-sized bronze memorial sculpture depicting Kelly seated in his single sculls rowing shell. Executed by the prominent Philadelphia sculptor Harry Rosin, the artwork was installed along East River Drive near the grandstand area of the Schuylkill River regatta course—a stretch of road subsequently renamed **Kelly Drive** in honor of the Kelly family's legacy to the city and the sport of rowing. The monument remains preserved under the custodial care of the Association for Public Art.

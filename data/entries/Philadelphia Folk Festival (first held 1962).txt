## 2. Lead section

The **Philadelphia Folk Festival** is an annual four-day music event held near Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, celebrated as the longest continuously-run outdoor music festival in North America. Established in 1962 by folklorist Kenneth S. Goldstein (1927–1995) and radio disc jockey Gene Shay (1935–2020), the festival was created to contribute to the American folk music revival of the mid-1960s. Taking advantage of a temporary hiatus of Rhode Island's Newport Folk Festival, Goldstein and Shay organized the event to demonstrate the breadth of traditional and contemporary folk music while introducing emerging musicians to audiences in the Greater Philadelphia region.

From its inception, the festival has been sponsored by the Philadelphia Folksong Society, a non-profit organization dedicated to music and arts education. During the event's early years, proceeds directly supported local cultural initiatives, including the funding of a folklore library, concerts hosted at community centers across Philadelphia, and academic fellowships for students enrolled in the folklore program at the University of Pennsylvania. What began as a modest grassroots assembly quickly expanded into a major multi-day cultural institution featuring main-stage performances, participatory workshops, square dancing, hootenannies, artisan crafts, and extensive camping facilities.

Musically, the Philadelphia Folk Festival encompasses a diverse array of traditional and contemporary genres. Over six decades, its stages have hosted acoustic genres such as Appalachian folk, blues, Cajun, murder ballads, and traditional oral music, alongside cross-genre styles including Celtic, country, Creole zydeco, and Ashkenazi Jewish klezmer music. Landmark folk and blues artists such as Pete Seeger, the Reverend Gary Davis, Taj Mahal, and Bonnie Raitt have performed at the event, as have contemporary indie and folk-rock acts including Steve Earle, Iron & Wine, and The Decemberists.

Originally hosted at Wilson Farm in Paoli, Pennsylvania, from 1962 through 1964, the festival moved briefly to Spring Mountain in 1965 before relocating to Old Poole Farm in Schwenksville, Montgomery County, in 1966 following zoning controversies over crowd sizes and municipal noise ordinances in Paoli. In the decades since, Old Poole Farm has served as the festival's permanent home, accommodating up to 35,000 attendees annually. Through its preserved camping traditions, children's educational programming, and environmental sustainability initiatives, the Philadelphia Folk Festival remains a cornerstone of North American folk culture and a primary driver of folk music preservation in the Mid-Atlantic region.

## 3. Background

The emergence of the Philadelphia Folk Festival in 1962 occurred during the height of the mid-twentieth-century American folk music revival. Across the United States, audiences developed a heightened interest in traditional regional sounds, oral storytelling, and acoustic songcraft. In the Greater Philadelphia region, this movement found a receptive audience. Local venues such as the 2nd Fret in Center City Philadelphia and the Main Point in Bryn Mawr established themselves as premier folk clubs, maintaining regular schedules of traditional and contemporary acoustic acts.

Seeking to channel this regional enthusiasm into a permanent event, folklorist Kenneth S. Goldstein and broadcast personality Gene Shay conceptualized a major festival for the Philadelphia region. Goldstein, an academic and record producer, and Shay, a prominent local folk radio DJ, recognized a distinct opportunity in 1962 when the established Newport Folk Festival in Rhode Island entered a temporary hiatus. The duo envisioned an event that would educate the public on the diverse historical dimensions of folk music while providing a national platform for both established traditional artists and newly emerging musicians.

To institutionalize the festival, Goldstein and Shay partnered with the Philadelphia Folksong Society, a non-profit cultural organization focused on arts education and traditional music preservation. The Folksong Society agreed to sponsor the event, establishing an operational model where festival earnings were reinvested directly into regional education and scholarship. During the festival's first four years, revenues funded the creation of a specialized folklore library, underwrote free public concerts in Philadelphia community centers, and provided graduate fellowships for students studying folklore at the University of Pennsylvania. This institutional backing anchored the festival to Philadelphia's academic and civic communities from its inception.

### Initial Years at Wilson Farm (1962–1964)
The inaugural Philadelphia Folk Festival took place in late August 1962 on the property of C. Colket Wilson Jr., known as Wilson Farm, located in Paoli, Chester County, Pennsylvania. The maiden lineup featured prominent figures in American folk music, headlined by veteran activist and songleader Pete Seeger (1919–2014). Joining Seeger were the bluegrass ensemble the Greenbriar Boys, Piedmont blues guitarist and gospel singer Reverend Gary Davis (1896–1972), and Appalachian banjo player and vocalist Obray Ramsey (1913–1997). The first event attracted an estimated audience of 2,500 people.

The festival experienced rapid growth over the next two years. By 1963, attendance reached approximately 10,000 attendees, expanding further to 15,000 by 1964. The program broadened to encompass a wide spectrum of roots traditions, including Appalachian traditional music, roots revival, blues, Cajun tunes, and traditional murder ballads passed down through oral tradition.

### Zoning Disputes and Relocation to Schwenksville
As attendance scaled rapidly at Wilson Farm, local residents and township officials in Tredyffrin Township raised concerns regarding the event's strain on local infrastructure. Neighbors submitted complaints citing traffic congestion, public alcohol consumption, unauthorized camping on private properties, excessive litter, and general noise disruption. The tension culminated during the 1964 festival when a Saturday night concert ran until 2:00 a.m. Sunday morning, violating Tredyffrin Township's municipal carnival ordinance.

In January 1965, the Tredyffrin Township Zoning Board of Adjustment formally revoked the festival’s permanent zoning variance. Although the Philadelphia Folksong Society mounted a legal challenge arguing that a permanent variance could not be summarily rescinded, organizers simultaneously searched for a new venue capable of supporting the event's logistical needs. 

For the August 1965 event, festival directors moved the festival to Spring Mountain in Montgomery County. The following year, in 1966, organizers secured an agreement with Old Poole Farm, a working farm situated in Schwenksville, Montgomery County, approximately 35 miles northwest of Philadelphia. The expansive agricultural property offered the physical capacity required for dedicated stage areas, extensive parking, and regulated campgrounds, becoming the festival's permanent operational site from 1966 onward.

### Genre Expansion and Performance History
Following the move to Old Poole Farm, festival management continued to expand the musical palette featured across its multiple stages. While maintaining its core commitment to traditional acoustic folk, the festival incorporated related traditional genres, including Creole-influenced zydeco, Celtic traditional music, and Ashkenazi Jewish klezmer music.

Over the decades, the event billed major figures in popular music who drew upon folk traditions. Notable acts included blues musician Taj Mahal, singer-songwriter Bonnie Raitt, country-rock artist Steve Earle, and local multi-instrumentalist Andrew Lipke. In the early 2000s, the festival incorporated contemporary indie-folk and folk-rock groups such as Iron & Wine and The Decemberists, attracting younger audiences while maintaining its traditional foundation.

## 5. Aftermath

### Cultural Continuity and Camping Traditions
When the broader national folk revival began to wane in the 1970s, many contemporary music festivals folded; however, the Philadelphia Folk Festival retained its audience through operational adaptation and community building. In the twenty-first century, annual attendance stabilized at approximately 35,000 attendees per year. 

A defining feature of the festival's long-term sustainability is its extensive camping culture. Approximately 6,500 patrons reside on-site at the Old Poole Farm campgrounds throughout the festival weekend. The camping grounds developed distinct internal communities, featuring self-governed "neighborhoods" organized around specific preferences, such as quiet family zones, RV lots, and active tent-camping communities. Impromptu jam sessions, hootenannies, and open pickin' circles occur around the clock within the campgrounds. To accommodate this culture, organizers formally added a "Campers Only" concert held on the Thursday evening prior to the public opening of the main stages.

### Educational and Environmental Programs
To cultivate successive generations of folk enthusiasts, festival organizers established targeted family programming. In the wooded area connecting the main campgrounds to the primary concert meadow, organizers designated "Dulcimer Grove" as an area for children and family activities. Programmatic offerings include the Great Groove Band, an interactive music workshop where school-age children learn fiddle, acoustic guitar, and folk music techniques over the course of the weekend, culminating in a performance on the main stage.

In 2010, the festival instituted ecological initiatives to reduce its environmental footprint. Partnering with the Illinois-based folk ensemble The Giving Tree Band, festival management introduced multi-stage recycling, composting programs, and sustainable waste-management protocols across the farm grounds. 

Through six decades of uninterrupted operation, the Philadelphia Folk Festival established the Greater Philadelphia region as an essential national sanctuary for the preservation, performance, and evolution of American folk music.

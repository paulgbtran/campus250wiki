**Margaret "Peggy" Shippen Arnold** (July 11, 1760 – August 24, 1804) was an American-born socialite and accomplice in Benedict Arnold's treasonous plot during the American Revolutionary War. Born into one of colonial Philadelphia’s most affluent and politically prominent families, she became a central figure in upper-class society during both the Patriot administration and the British military occupation of Philadelphia. She is historically most famous as the second wife of Major General Benedict Arnold and for her active complicity in his treasonous plot to surrender the American fortification at West Point to the British Crown.

In April 1779, Shippen married Arnold while he served as the Military Governor of Philadelphia. Capitalizing on her pre-existing personal relationship with British intelligence officer Major John André—whom she had met during the British occupation of the city in 1777–1778—Shippen served as the primary intermediary in secret communications between Arnold and British military authorities. Her social connections, political acumen, and intelligence operations facilitated the negotiations that led Arnold to attempt the surrender of West Point in exchange for money and a commission in the British Army.

Following the discovery of the plot and the capture of Major André in September 1780, Shippen famously feigned a fit of hysterical madness when confronted by General George Washington and his staff at West Point. Her performance successfully convinced Washington and his aides of her total innocence, earning her safe passage to her family home in Philadelphia and subsequently to British-controlled New York City. Pennsylvania authorities later discovered incriminating correspondence and banished her from the state.

Shippen spent the remainder of her life in exile in Great Britain and New Brunswick, managing her husband’s turbulent financial affairs and securing British pensions and military appointments for her children. While nineteenth-century historians often depicted her as an innocent victim of her husband's dark ambitions, modern historical consensus recognizes her as an astute, politically active co-conspirator who played a direct and decisive role in one of the most notorious espionage episodes in American history.

## Early life and education

Margaret "Peggy" Shippen was born on July 11, 1760, in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, then the largest and wealthiest city in British North America. She was the youngest daughter of Edward Shippen IV and Margaret Francis Shippen. The Shippen family was a foundational pillar of Pennsylvania’s colonial aristocracy; her great-grandfather, Edward Shippen, had served as the first elected Mayor of Philadelphia in 1701. Her father was a distinguished lawyer, judge, and member of the Pennsylvania Provincial Council who sought to maintain a position of strict political neutrality during the escalating imperial crisis between Great Britain and the American colonies, though the family maintained strong Loyalist leanings.

Growing up in the family’s grand residence on Fourth Street, Shippen enjoyed an upbringing characterized by material luxury and high social standing. Unlike many women of her era, she received an advanced informal education supervised by her father. She was tutored in literature, history, French, penmanship, and fine arts, alongside practical instruction in financial accounting, household management, and political discourse. Observers noted her intelligence, charm, sharp wit, and striking beauty, which quickly established her as a preeminent figure within Philadelphia’s elite social circles.

The outbreak of the Revolutionary War disrupted Philadelphia's social dynamics. When British forces under General Sir William Howe occupied Philadelphia in September 1777, the Shippen household became a frequent gathering place for British officers. During this period, seventeen-year-old Shippen met Captain (later Major) John André, an accomplished and charismatic officer who served as an aide-de-camp to Major General Charles Grey and as a prominent social organizer for the British army. André became a regular guest at the Shippen home, painting portraits of Peggy and her sisters and exchanging poetry. In May 1778, André orchestrated the *Mischianza*, an elaborate, day-long extravagant festival and pageant held to honor the departing General Howe. Although André designed costumes for Peggy and her sisters as "Ladies of the Blended Rose," her father forbade them from attending the event, though her social connection to André remained strong.

## Career

While women in eighteenth-century America were formally barred from holding public office or military command, Shippen operated effectively within the political and intelligence spheres through her position as a high-society hostess and diplomatic intermediary. Her informal "career" as a political agent began in earnest following the British evacuation of Philadelphia in June 1778 and the subsequent reoccupation of the city by Continental forces.

Major General Benedict Arnold was appointed Military Governor of Philadelphia by General George Washington in June 1778. Arnold, a hero of the Battles of Saratoga who was recovering from severe leg wounds, established a lavish lifestyle in the city that quickly drew criticism from Pennsylvania’s Patriot leadership. Arnold began courting Shippen shortly after his arrival. Despite initial reluctance from Judge Edward Shippen due to Arnold’s age (he was nineteen years older than Peggy), his status as a widower with three children, and his volatile political standing, the couple was married on April 8, 1779.

```
[British Military Intelligence] ◄──► [Peggy Shippen Arnold] ◄──► [Gen. Benedict Arnold]
    (Major John André)                (Intermediary / Ciphers)       (West Point Command)
```

Shortly after their marriage, Arnold’s growing resentment toward the Continental Congress over missed promotions, court-martial charges, and financial disputes culminated in his decision to change allegiances. Shippen played a structural role in initiating and facilitating her husband's treasonable negotiations with the British army:

1. **Reestablishing Contact**: Shippen utilized her existing social correspondence with Major John André, who had become the head of British secret intelligence under Sir Henry Clinton in New York, to open a clandestine channel.
2. **Coded Communication**: She aided in drafting and transmitting coded messages, using invisible ink (referred to as "sympathetic stain") and numerical ciphers hidden within innocent letters discussing fashion, social news, and luxury goods.
3. **Financial Negotiation**: Shippen actively participated in setting the financial terms for Arnold’s betrayal, insisting on compensation for lost property and a substantial pension for herself and her children.

When Arnold obtained command of the key fortress at West Point, New York, in August 1780, Shippen accompanied him to his headquarters at the Beverly Robinson house on the Hudson River. From this position, Arnold prepared to hand over the garrison’s blueprints and defense plans to the British for £20,000—a plot directly facilitated by Shippen’s intelligence network.

## Personal life

Shippen’s personal life was defined by her devotion to her family, her navigate of political upheaval, and her resilience during years of international exile. Her marriage to Benedict Arnold produced seven children, five of whom survived to adulthood:

* **Edward Shippen Arnold** (1780–1813): Served as a lieutenant in the British Army in India.
* **James Robertson Arnold** (1781–1854): Attained the rank of Lieutenant-General in the Royal Engineers.
* **George Arnold** (1787–1828): Served as a lieutenant-colonel in the Royal Bengal Cavalry.
* **William Fitch Arnold** (1794–1846): Served as a captain in the 9th Queen's Royal Lancers.
* **Sophia Matilda Arnold** (1785–1828): Married Colonel Ponsonby Phipps of the British Army.

The turning point of Shippen's personal life occurred on September 25, 1780, when British Major John André was captured near Tarrytown, New York, carrying the papers revealing the West Point plot. Upon receiving advance warning of André's capture, Arnold fled down the Hudson River to the British warship HMS *Vulture*. When General George Washington, Alexander Hamilton, and the Marquis de Lafayette arrived at headquarters shortly thereafter, Shippen executed a calculated act of hysteria. She threw herself into bed, claimed her husband had been murdered, declared that General Washington intended to kill her child, and pretended to be utterly insane.

The performance convinced Washington and his staff of her complete innocence. Hamilton famously wrote to his fiancée, Elizabeth Schuyler, describing Shippen as a frantic, heart-broken victim of her husband's deceit. Washington granted her safe passage back to her family home in Philadelphia.

However, once in Philadelphia, the Supreme Executive Council of Pennsylvania seized Arnold’s papers and discovered written evidence directly implicating Shippen in the conspiracy with André. In October 1780, the Council issued an order banishing her from Pennsylvania. She was forced to join her husband behind British lines in New York City.

Following the British defeat in 1781, the Arnold family resettled in London. They briefly moved to Saint John, New Brunswick, between 1785 and 1791, where Arnold engaged in commercial shipping ventures that generated intense local hostility. The family returned to London in 1791. Throughout Arnold's subsequent failing business investments and military misadventures, Shippen maintained control of the household finances, successfully petitioned the British Crown for land grants in Canada and an annual pension of £500, and managed the education of her children.

Benedict Arnold died in London in June 1801. Left with considerable debts, Shippen spent her remaining years liquidating assets, settling claims, and securing career postings for her sons. She died of cancer in London on August 24, 1804, at the age of 44, and was buried alongside her husband at St Mary's Church, Battersea.

## Legacy

Peggy Shippen's historical legacy has undergone a profound reevaluation over two centuries. In the immediate aftermath of the Revolution and throughout the nineteenth century, American popular history framed Shippen through prevailing gender conventions, portraying her as an innocent, delicate young wife who was entirely unaware of her husband's treachery or who had been manipulated by his darker motives.

This romanticized narrative persisted until the mid-twentieth century, when archival research by historians such as Carl Van Doren unearthed the lost Clinton Papers and the British Secret Intelligence ledgers. These documents revealed letters in Shippen's handwriting containing invisible ink messages, code keys, and financial demands addressed to John André, confirming her central role as co-architect of the West Point conspiracy.

|                     manipulated by her husband.                  |

Modern scholarship views Shippen as one of the most effective covert operatives of the Revolutionary era. Her ability to leverage contemporary societal assumptions about female innocence enabled her to operate an intelligence network under the eyes of both Patriot and Loyalist authorities and escape immediate prosecution.

In the physical landscape of Philadelphia, her legacy remains tied to Mount Pleasant, a Georgian mansion in East Fairmount Park purchased by Benedict Arnold as a wedding present for Peggy in 1779. Managed by the Philadelphia Museum of Art, Mount Pleasant stands as a major historic landmark illustrating the high-society world of late-eighteenth-century Philadelphia in which Shippen rose to prominence.

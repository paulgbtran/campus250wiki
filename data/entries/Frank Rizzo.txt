## Lead Section

Francis Lazzaro "Frank" Rizzo Sr. (1920–1991) was an American police officer and politician who served as the Police Commissioner of Philadelphia from 1967 to 1971 and subsequently as the 93rd Mayor of Philadelphia for two consecutive terms from 1972 to 1980. Rising through the ranks of the Philadelphia Police Department over several decades, Rizzo cultivated a tough, uncompromising public persona that resonated with working-class residents while drawing intense criticism from civil rights organizations, activists, and minority communities.

Rizzo's governance and law enforcement philosophy were defined by an explicit "law-and-order" approach. During his tenure as police commissioner and mayor, his administration expanded the police force and emphasized visible, aggressive policing tactics. Supporter bases viewed him as a dedicated public servant who maintained urban stability during a era marked by nationwide unrest, whereas detractors viewed his tenure as a period characterized by widespread police brutality, systemic discrimination, and institutional harassment against Black citizens, the LGBTQ+ community, and political dissidents.

The enduring controversy surrounding Rizzo’s public career persisted long after his death in 1991. In 1998, a 9-foot bronze memorial statue of Rizzo, created by sculptor Zenos Frudakis and privately funded by the Frank L. Rizzo Monument Committee, was installed on the steps of Philadelphia's Municipal Services Building Plaza across from City Hall. For over two decades, the statue served as a focal point for intense civic debates regarding public memory, racial justice, and historical commemoration in the city.

In June 2020, amidst widespread global and local Black Lives Matter demonstrations following the death of George Floyd, the City of Philadelphia officially removed the monument in the early morning hours of June 3. Mayor Jim Kenney characterized the statue as a symbol of racism, bigotry, and police brutality, noting that the treatment of minority communities under Rizzo’s leadership represented one of the most painful periods in Philadelphia’s modern history. The statue was subsequently placed into permanent secure storage.

## Early Life and Education

Francis Lazzaro Rizzo Sr. was born in Philadelphia in 1920 into an Italian-American family. Raised in South Philadelphia, a neighborhood characterized by strong ethnic enclave identity and working-class traditions, Rizzo developed an early orientation toward municipal service and physical discipline. His father was a police officer, providing a direct family precedent for a career in public law enforcement.

Rizzo attended local Philadelphia public schools, though he left formal secondary education prior to graduation to enlist in the United States Navy. Following his military service, he returned to Philadelphia to join the municipal police force, embarking on a long career within the city's uniformed services. Throughout his early adult years, Rizzo built a reputation for personal physical assertiveness and an aggressive policing style on the streets of Philadelphia, which accelerated his advancement within the department.

## Career

### Police Department and Police Commissioner (1967–1971)
Rizzo’s rise within the Philadelphia Police Department was swift. Joining the department as a patrolman, he advanced through the supervisory ranks to become Deputy Commissioner and eventually Police Commissioner in 1967, serving in that capacity until 1971. As commissioner, Rizzo gained national prominence for his visible presence at crime scenes, his confrontational rhetoric, and his willingness to personally direct police interventions during civil disturbances.

Under Rizzo’s leadership, the department adopted high-visibility tactics, aggressive stop-and-frisk policies, and heavy enforcement measures. While celebrated by many conservative Philadelphians as a champion of public safety during the turbulent late 1960s, his methods drew severe condemnation from civil rights leaders. Allegations of unchecked police brutality, unlawful arrests, and targeted intimidation against Black activists and LGBTQ+ individuals became defining characteristics of public debate regarding his commissionership.

### Mayor of Philadelphia (1972–1980)
Capitalizing on his popularity among white working-class voters, Rizzo ran for Mayor of Philadelphia as a Democrat and was elected in 1971, taking office in January 1972. He secured reelection in 1975, serving two full terms until 1980. His mayoral administrations were marked by an unwavering focus on municipal law enforcement funding, opposition to court-ordered school desegregation busing, and political conflicts with progressive and minority civic groups.

Rizzo’s political dominance reshaped Philadelphia's electoral landscape, entrenching deep racial and ideological divisions across the city. His governance style was direct and autocratic, frequently alienating political opponents while maintaining strong loyalty among his base. Attempts by his administration to amend the city charter in 1978 to allow him to run for a third consecutive term were defeated by a coalition of Black, progressive, and liberal voters, effectively capping his continuous tenure as mayor at eight years.

## Personal Life

Frank Rizzo was married to his wife, Mary, with whom he had two children, including Frank L. Rizzo Jr., who later entered Philadelphia politics as a member of the Philadelphia City Council. Rizzo maintained strong ties to South Philadelphia throughout his life, residing in the city and remaining active in local political circles until his death.

Following his mayoralty, Rizzo remained a frequent candidate and media presence in Philadelphia politics. He died suddenly of a heart attack on July 16, 1991, at the age of 70, while actively campaigning for a third non-consecutive mayoral term as the Republican nominee. Following his death, family members, friends, and political allies formed the Frank L. Rizzo Monument Committee to honor his memory and oversee private fundraising efforts for a public memorial.

## Legacy

### Historical Assessment and Cultural Impact
Frank Rizzo remains one of the most polarizing political figures in Philadelphia history. To his supporters, he represented an unwavering defender of neighborhood stability and traditional authority during a period of urban crisis. To his critics, his career embodied institutional racism, police misconduct, and political demagoguery that damaged race relations and civic unity in Philadelphia for generations.

### The Frank L. Rizzo Monument Controversy
Following Rizzo's death, the Frank L. Rizzo Monument Committee commissioned renowned figurative sculptor Zenos Frudakis—an alumnus of the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts (PAFA)—to create a 9-foot bronze statue depicting Rizzo walking and waving. Funded entirely through private contributions from family, friends, and supporters, the statue was donated to the City of Philadelphia and erected in 1998 on the steps of the Municipal Services Building Plaza, directly across from Philadelphia City Hall.

Ironically, the bronze monument was situated near Jacques Lipchitz's modern public sculpture *Government of the People*, a artwork that Rizzo had famously dismissed during his lifetime as a "load of dumped plaster."

From its installation, the monument generated continuous public conflict:

* **Relocation Agreement (2017):** After years of petitions, protests, and municipal reviews, the City of Philadelphia formally committed in 2017 to relocate the statue to a non-public site during a planned 2021 renovation of the Municipal Services Building Plaza.
* **Protests and Removal (2020):** In late May and early June 2020, mass Black Lives Matter demonstrations swept Philadelphia following the murder of George Floyd. The statue became a focal site for anti-racism protests, suffering vandalism and repeated attempts at dismantling by demonstrators.
* **Final Disposition:** On June 3, 2020, under orders from Mayor Jim Kenney, municipal workers removed the 9-foot bronze statue in the early morning hours. Mayor Kenney issued an official statement declaring:
  > *"The statue is a deplorable monument to racism, bigotry, and police brutality for members of the Black community, the LGBTQ community, and many others. The treatment of these communities under Mr. Rizzo's leadership was among the worst periods in Philadelphia's history... The removal of this statue today is but a small step in that process."*

Ownership of the statue was subsequently transferred back to the Frank L. Rizzo Monument Committee, and the object was placed in long-term secure storage.

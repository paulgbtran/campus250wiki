## 2. Lead section

**Cliveden**, also known as the **Benjamin Chew House**, is an 18th-century historic estate located in the Germantown neighborhood of Northwest Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Built between 1763 and 1767 as a summer retreat for Benjamin Chew (1722–1810)—the last Chief Justice of colonial Pennsylvania under Penn family proprietorship—the mansion stands as one of the finest surviving examples of Mid-Atlantic Georgian country house architecture. Situated on six landscaped acres along Germantown Avenue, the site represents a major architectural landmark of colonial British America.

The property gained international military renown during the American Revolutionary War when it served as the focal point of the Battle of Germantown on October 4, 1777. During General George Washington’s tactical counteroffensive against the occupying British garrison in Philadelphia, approximately 120 soldiers of the British 40th Regiment of Foot, commanded by Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Musgrave, barricaded themselves inside Cliveden's heavy stone walls. The mansion functioned as an impromptu fortress, resisting repeated assaults and heavy artillery bombardments by Continental forces. The tactical delay caused by the siege at Cliveden proved pivotal, contributing to the confusion and eventual retreat of the American troops.

Following the Revolutionary War, Cliveden was repaired and remained in the Chew family for seven generations across more than two centuries. The long tenure of family ownership preserved not only the mansion's original architectural features and decorative arts, but also an extraordinary archive of household documents, legal records, and correspondence. These records have provided modern historians with crucial documentation regarding the social structure, political climate, and economic networks of colonial and early republic Pennsylvania.

In 1961, Cliveden was designated a National Historic Landmark and was formally listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1966. In 1972, the Chew family transferred ownership of the property to the National Trust for Historic Preservation. Today, Cliveden operates as a historic house museum and community educational site, offering programming that explores Revolutionary War military history, historic preservation, and the complex historical realities of indentured servitude and enslavement on the estate.

## 3. History

### Construction and Colonial Context
In the early 1760s, Benjamin Chew, a prominent Philadelphia lawyer, politician, and jurist, purchased land along the Germantown Great Road to construct a summer residence away from the heat and periodic yellow fever epidemics of downtown Philadelphia. Chew commissioned local master carpenter and builder Jacob Knorr to construct the stone mansion, drawing design inspiration from European architectural pattern books such as James Gibbs's *A Book of Architecture* (1728). Construction began in 1763 and was completed in 1767. 

Cliveden was built as the center of a gentleman's country estate, supported by outbuildings including a detached kitchen, washhouse, carriage house, and barn. During the pre-Revolutionary era, the estate functioned both as a venue for high-society entertaining and as an agricultural operational site maintained by enslaved workers, indentured servants, and wage laborers.

### The Battle of Germantown (1777)
During the British occupation of Philadelphia in the autumn of 1777, General Sir William Howe stationed a significant portion of his army in Germantown. On the foggy morning of October 4, General George Washington launched a coordinated four-column surprise attack against the British positions. As the Continental Army pushed the British infantry back along the Germantown road, Lieutenant Colonel Thomas Musgrave and approximately 120 soldiers of the 40th Regiment of Foot fell back and barricaded themselves inside Cliveden's main residence.

When the lead Continental units encountered the improvised garrison, American commanders held a brief council of war. Brigadier General Henry Knox argued forcefully that it was contrary to military doctrine to leave a fortified "castle" occupied by the enemy in the Army's rear. Consequently, Washington ordered William Maxwell’s brigade and Continental artillery under Knox to reduce the fortress. For over an hour, Continental forces fired field artillery pieces at the mansion and launched direct infantry charges against the barricaded doors and shuttered windows.

Cliveden's thick stone masonry successfully absorbed the impact of light field artillery, while British soldiers firing from the upper story windows inflicted heavy casualties on the exposed Continental infantry. The prolonged engagement at Cliveden generated dense smoke and cannon fire, contributing to the disorganization of neighboring American units operating in the heavy fog. The delay allowed British commanders to rally their troops and launch a counterattack that eventually forced Washington to order a general retreat.

### Post-Revolutionary Era and Chew Family Tenure
The Battle of Germantown left Cliveden severely damaged. The exterior stone façade was pockmarked by cannonballs and musket shot, the roof and chimneys were partially destroyed, and the interior plasterwork and woodwork suffered extensive scarring. Benjamin Chew, whose political stance during the conflict was categorized as neutral or non-juror, was temporarily held under house arrest in New Jersey during the British occupation due to his former royal appointments.

In 1779, owing to financial uncertainties and the home's damaged state, Chew sold Cliveden to Blair McClenachan, a prominent Philadelphia merchant and politician. However, in 1797, Chew repurchased the property. The house remained in the Chew family continuously until 1972, serving as a primary or seasonal residence for generation after generation of Chew descendants.

### Slavery, Servitude, and Labor History
Historical research conducted in the late 20th and early 21st centuries broadened the understanding of Cliveden's operational history by highlighting the lives of the enslaved individuals and indentured laborers who built, maintained, and worked on the estate. Benjamin Chew owned multiple plantations across Pennsylvania, Delaware, and Maryland, holding dozens of men, women, and children in bondage.

Enslaved laborers worked at Cliveden both before and after the Revolutionary War, serving as domestic staff, agricultural workers, carriage drivers, and groundskeepers. Notable archival documents, including Chew family financial ledgers, letters, and run-away ads, detail the human complexity of the site. Notable figures include Richard Allen, founder of the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church, who was born into slavery under Benjamin Chew in Philadelphia before being sold to a Delaware plantation owner, where he eventually purchased his freedom.

## 4. Architecture

### Exterior Design
Cliveden is recognized as an exemplary monument of American Georgian architecture. Designed in the classic English Palladian tradition as adapted for the Mid-Atlantic colonies, the main residence features a symmetrical, five-bay two-and-a-half-story façade built from locally quarried Wissahickon schist ashlar stone with dressed granite accents and quoins.

The central feature of the front façade is a grand pedimented entrance pavilion flanked by classical pilasters and framed by a rusticated stone surround. The gabled roofline is punctuated by pedimented dormers and crowned by carved stone decorative urns along the parapet and roof ridge—an unusual and sophisticated ornamental feature for colonial American domestic structures.

### Interior Plan and Detailing
The interior of Cliveden is organized around a grand central hallway that spans the width of the main house. The entry hall is divided into front and rear sections by a formal screen of paired wooden Tuscan columns carrying an elaborate entablature, creating an impressive sequence of spaces for arriving guests.

To the left and right of the main hallway are double parlors, dining areas, and private study rooms equipped with carved wooden mantelpieces, deep window sills, and plaster cornices. The second floor contains sleeping chambers arranged around a central landing, while the third floor attic space historically housed servants and domestic staff. The thick interior partitions and heavy timber framing contributed to the structural integrity that allowed the building to survive the 1777 military bombardment.

### Outbuildings and Grounds
The estate originally encompassed over sixty acres of farmland, gardens, and orchard grounds. Today, six acres remain preserved around the main house. Surrounding the mansion are original 18th-century stone dependencies, including a detached kitchen building and a washhouse connected to the main block by stone walling, a configuration designed to separate domestic chores and heat from the principal living spaces.

## 5. Cultural Significance

### Military and Historical Legacy
Cliveden holds a permanent place in American Revolutionary War historiography. The physical scars of the Battle of Germantown—including pockmarked schist masonry, chipped woodwork, and bloodstains preserved beneath floorboards—turned the mansion into an early destination for historical tourism and Revolutionary remembrance as early as the 19th century.

Every autumn, Cliveden hosts the annual Reenactment of the Battle of Germantown, drawing thousands of visitors, historians, and military reenactors to Northwest Philadelphia to observe dramatizations of the 1777 tactical struggle around the house and grounds.

### Modern Interpretation and Public History
In the 21st century, Cliveden has pioneered innovative methodologies in public history and historic site interpretation. Moving beyond traditional "great man" narratives that focused exclusively on Benjamin Chew and Revolutionary military strategy, Cliveden launched comprehensive research initiatives aimed at illuminating the lives of all people who resided on the property.

Exhibitions such as *Liberty to Go to the See* draw heavily from the extensive Chew Family Papers (housed at the Historical Society of Pennsylvania) to explore stories of race, bondage, legal status, and social inequality in early Philadelphia. Through community dialogues, dramatic performances, and educational workshops, Cliveden addresses how the concepts of freedom and liberty coexisted with chattel slavery on the same historic ground.

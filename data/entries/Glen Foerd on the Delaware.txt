## 2. Lead section

**Glen Foerd on the Delaware** is an 18-acre historic estate, house museum, public park, and arboretum located at the confluence of the Poquessing Creek and the Delaware River in the East Torresdale neighborhood of Northeast Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Established in the mid-19th century as a riverfront country seat, Glen Foerd is the sole remaining intact Gilded Age river estate open to the public along Philadelphia's Delaware River waterfront. The site is listed on the National Register of Historic Places and is designated as a landmark within the local and regional park system.

The estate originated in 1850 when Charles Macalester (1798–1873), a prominent Scottish-American financier, merchant, and presidential advisor, purchased the riverfront parcel to build a summer retreat. Macalester named the Italianate villa "Glengarry" after his ancestral homelands in Scotland. During Macalester's residence, Glengarry served as a fashionable venue for high-society gatherings and hosted prominent national figures, including U.S. Presidents James Buchanan and Abraham Lincoln.

In 1893, the property was acquired by Robert H. Foerderer (1860–1903), a wealthy leather manufacturer and U.S. Congressman who made a vast fortune by inventing and mass-producing "Vici Kid," a revolutionary chrome-tanning process for goat skins. Foerderer renamed the estate "Glen Foerd," combining the original Scottish name with his family surname. Between 1895 and 1903, Foerderer commissioned architect William C. Pritchett Jr. to dramatically expand and remodel the villa into an expansive Beaux-Arts and Classical Revival mansion, complete with a two-story art gallery, grand portico, formal gardens, and modern estate infrastructure.

Following the death of Foerderer and his wife, Caroline, the property passed to their daughter, Florence Foerderer Tonner (1882–1971), an influential arts patron and collector. Tonner amassed an extensive collection of European and American fine art, decorative items, prints, and rare books, which she housed at Glen Foerd. Upon her death in 1971, Tonner bequeathed the estate to the public. Today, the property is owned by the City of Philadelphia through Philadelphia Parks & Recreation and operated by the non-profit Glen Foerd Conservation Corporation, providing educational programming, public parkland, historical tours, and arts access along the Delaware River corridor.

## 3. History

### Mid-19th Century and the Macalester Era (Glengarry)
In the mid-19th century, the banks of the Delaware River north of Philadelphia developed into a preferred sanctuary for the city's merchant and financial elites seeking relief from the urban summer heat and disease epidemics. In 1850, Charles Macalester, a prominent Philadelphia banker and advisor to U.S. presidents from Andrew Jackson to Abraham Lincoln, acquired land in what is now East Torresdale. Macalester commissioned a three-story Italianate villa facing the Delaware River and christened the residence "Glengarry" in homage to his Scottish ancestral roots.

Glengarry quickly became an important social hub for mid-Victorian political and industrial leaders. Macalester utilized the estate not only as a domestic refuge but as a site for diplomatic and economic networking. The surrounding area grew in prominent residential standing during this era, driven in part by Macalester's involvement in local land development and the expansion of the Pennsylvania Railroad along the river corridor, which enabled rapid transit between downtown Philadelphia and the riverfront estates.

### The Foerderer Era and Expansion (1893–1920s)
Following Macalester's death in 1873 and subsequent estate transitions, the property was purchased in 1893 by industrialist Robert H. Foerderer. Foerderer was one of Philadelphia's prominent Gilded Age figures, having built a major leather manufacturing empire centered in Frankford. His technological breakthrough—the commercialization of the "Vici Kid" chrome-tanning process—transformed leather production by yielding soft, water-resistant, durable kid skin leather, making his company one of the largest leather processors in the world.

Foerderer renamed the property "Glen Foerd" and embarked on an ambitious expansion program to align the residence with his family's wealth and public status. Between 1895 and 1903, he retained William C. Pritchett Jr., a notable Philadelphia architect, to redesign the house. Pritchett encapsulated the original Italianate villa inside a grand Classical Revival shell, expanding the structure into a 25-room mansion. The renovations introduced a monumental front portico, a sun parlor, expanded service wings, and a dedicated two-story art gallery featuring a pipe organ.

Concurrently, the grounds were transformed into a formal country estate. Foerderer added an ornate stone carriage house, a riverfront boathouse, a water tower, greenhouses, and terraced lawns sweeping down toward the Delaware River. However, Foerderer's enjoyment of the completed estate was brief; he died in 1903 at age 43, shortly after serving in the United States House of Representatives. His widow, Caroline Fischer Foerderer, maintained the property until her death in 1923.

### Florence Foerderer Tonner and Preservation (1923–1971)
In 1923, ownership of Glen Foerd transferred to Robert and Caroline's daughter, Florence Foerderer Tonner, who lived at the estate with her husband, Edward E. Tonner. Florence was a figure in Philadelphia's cultural life, serving as a benefactor to local arts organizations, hospitals, and charities. An enthusiastic collector, she filled Glen Foerd with thousands of art objects, including paintings attributed to European masters, American portraiture, prints by Rembrandt and Whistler, classical furniture, fine china, tapestries, and decorative glassware.

Throughout the mid-20th century, as industrial development and suburban housing expansion reshaped much of Northeast Philadelphia's riverfront, Florence maintained Glen Foerd as a quiet enclave of mid-nineteenth and early-twentieth-century residential land use. Recognizing the ecological and historic importance of her family's estate, she made provisions in her estate planning to preserve the property for civic enjoyment.

### Public Stewardship and Modern Era
Upon Florence Foerderer Tonner's death in 1971, the estate was placed in trust for public use and benefit. Following legal and administrative proceedings regarding the structure of the trust, ownership of the 18-acre property was officially conveyed to the Fairmount Park Commission (now Philadelphia Parks & Recreation) in the late 1970s.

To assist the municipality in preserving and maintaining the historic site, the **Glen Foerd Conservation Corporation** was formed in 1983 as a non-profit operating entity. The corporation works in partnership with the City of Philadelphia to manage restoration projects, maintain the accredited arboretum, host cultural events, and offer public educational access. In 1979, Glen Foerd was officially listed on the National Register of Historic Places, cementing its status as an architectural landmark of the Delaware Valley.

### Main Mansion Exterior
The main residence at Glen Foerd displays a layered architectural evolution, reflecting its growth from a mid-19th-century Italianate country residence to a Gilded Age Classical Revival manor. The three-story structure features stucco-rendered stone walls, symmetrical window fenestration, carved balustrades, and elaborate roof cornices supported by decorative brackets.

The primary architectural landmark of the facade is the monumental two-story classical portico overlooking the Delaware River. Added during William C. Pritchett Jr.'s turn-of-the-century expansion, the portico is supported by large, fluted Ionic columns topped with carved stone capitals. The second-story balcony nestled beneath the portico offers panoramic views of the riverfront. High-style Beaux-Arts detail work, including stone sills, copper gutter fittings, and multi-pane sash windows, unifies the original Macalester core with Pritchett's additions.

### Interior Layout and Art Gallery
The interior of Glen Foerd contains approximately 25 rooms arranged around a grand central hall:

* **Central Hallway and Staircase:** Features rich hardwood floors, intricate carved oak paneling, plaster ceiling moldings, and a sweeping main staircase lit by stained-glass fenestration.
* **Art Gallery:** Added during the 1895–1903 Foerderer additions, the two-story art gallery was built specifically to display the family's art collection. It features an overhead glass skylight, dark wood wainscoting, an integrated Haskell pipe organ, and a secondary balcony walkway that overlooks the main floor of the hall.
* **Drawing Room and Dining Room:** Adorned with period-appropriate woodwork, classical fireplaces featuring imported marble mantels, gilded chandeliers, and decorative plaster friezes depicting floral and mythic motifs.
* **Sun Parlor and Conservatory:** A bright, south-facing glass-enclosed space designed to bridge the indoor living area with the surrounding estate gardens, featuring tiled flooring and views of the grounds.

### Outbuildings and Landscape Architecture
The 18-acre estate retains a intact ensemble of late-19th and early-20th-century outbuildings and designed landscape elements:

* **The Carriage House and Stables:** A stone building designed in harmony with the main mansion's style. Originally housing horses, carriages, and groomsmen, it was later adapted for early automobiles and now serves as an event space and administrative facility.
* **The Boathouse:** Positioned along the tidal shoreline of the Delaware River, this functional structure provided water access for rowing, yachting, and river recreation during the late 19th and early 20th centuries.
* **Water Tower and Gatehouse:** Utility structures built during the Foerderer era that incorporated stone masonry and decorative trim, reflecting the aesthetic integration of utility buildings on historic country estates.
* **Grounds and Arboretum:** The estate grounds feature terraced lawns, winding gravel walkways, stone retaining walls, and a formal rose garden with a central fountain. Glen Foerd's grounds are recognized as an accredited arboretum, containing specimen trees such as mature American beeches, English oaks, ginkgos, weeping hemlocks, and historic magnolias.

## 5. Cultural significance

Glen Foerd on the Delaware occupies a prominent role in the cultural, architectural, and environmental history of the Philadelphia region:

* **Delaware River Country Estate Heritage:** Glen Foerd is the sole remaining publicly accessible Gilded Age river estate along Philadelphia's Delaware waterfront. It stands as a physical document of the 19th-century "river seat" movement, when elite Philadelphians converted agrarian riverfront lands into estate corridors.
* **Industrial and Industrialist Legacy:** The estate highlights the historical narrative of Philadelphia's rise as the "Workshop of the World." Robert H. Foerderer's fortune, derived from Frankford's leather manufacturing industry, illustrates how industrial innovation fed directly into regional philanthropy, fine arts patronage, and country estate development.
* **Community Arts, Education, and Environmental Stewardship:** Under the stewardship of the Glen Foerd Conservation Corporation and Philadelphia Parks & Recreation, Glen Foerd functions as a community cultural center. The estate hosts environmental education workshops focusing on river ecology and Poquessing Creek watershed conservation, artist-in-residence programs, concert series, theatrical performances, and public history tours.

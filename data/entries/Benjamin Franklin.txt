### Lead Section

Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790) was an American polymath, publisher, scientist, inventor, diplomat, and Founding Father whose life and civic endeavors were inextricably linked with the city of Philadelphia. Arriving in Philadelphia as a runaway apprentice in 1723, Franklin transformed the burgeoning colonial port city into a center of intellectual inquiry, civic reform, and scientific innovation. His work as a printer and writer established his financial independence and gave him a powerful public platform to advocate for civic improvement and intercolonial unity.

As a statesman, Franklin played a pivotal role in the founding of the United States. He was the only Founding Father to sign all four major documents that established the new nation: the Declaration of Independence (1776), the Treaty of Alliance with France (1778), the Treaty of Paris ending the Revolutionary War (1783), and the United States Constitution (1787). His diplomatic service in London and Paris secured critical international recognition and military support for the American Revolutionary cause.

Beyond his political achievements, Franklin was world-renowned for his scientific investigations into electricity and his practical inventions, including the lightning rod, bifocals, and the Franklin stove. In Philadelphia, he mobilized community cooperation to establish foundational institutions, including North America's first subscription library, a volunteer fire company, a civic academy, a public hospital, and a learned society for scientific inquiry. 

Franklin's enduring legacy permeates Philadelphia's physical and cultural landscape. From major thoroughfares such as the Benjamin Franklin Parkway to educational and cultural repositories like the Library Company of Philadelphia and the American Philosophical Society, his contributions laid the institutional groundwork for modern Philadelphia and shaped early American national identity.

### Early Life and Education

Benjamin Franklin was born in Boston, Massachusetts, on January 17, 1706, the fifteenth of seventeen children born to Josiah Franklin, a tallow chandler and soap boiler, and Abiah Folger. Despite showing an early aptitude for reading and writing, Franklin received only two years of formal schooling at Boston Latin School and a private writing academy before financial constraints forced him to work in his father’s shop at age ten.

At age twelve, Franklin was apprenticed to his older brother James, a printer who produced *The New-England Courant*. Recognizing the power of the printed word, the young Franklin taught himself prose composition, arithmetic, and basic philosophy by reading volumes in his brother’s shop. Under the pseudonym "Silence Dogood," sixteen-year-old Franklin secretly authored a series of satirical essays critiquing Bostonian society and politics, which were published to popular acclaim in the *Courant*.

Tensions between the brothers escalated when James discovered Benjamin was the author. Seeking independence, seventeen-year-old Franklin broke his indentures and fled Boston in 1723. He traveled first to New York City and then to Philadelphia, arriving in the Pennsylvania capital virtually penniless. Franklin quickly found employment as a journeyman printer under Samuel Keimer, making an impression on local political figures, including Governor William Keith, who encouraged Franklin to travel to London in 1724 to acquire printing equipment. Though Governor Keith's promised letters of credit failed to materialize, Franklin spent two years working in prominent London printing houses, mastering trade techniques and broadening his exposure to Enlightenment thought before returning to Philadelphia in 1726.

### Career

Upon his return to Philadelphia, Franklin established his own printing office. In 1729, he acquired *The Pennsylvania Gazette*, which he built into one of the most widely read and influential newspapers in the American colonies. Through the *Gazette*, Franklin published news, opinion, and civic proposals, cultivating a reputation for pragmatic journalism and editorial acumen. In 1732, he began publishing *Poor Richard's Almanack* under the pseudonym Richard Saunders. Renowned for its aphorisms, practical advice, and witty commentary on thrift and industry, the *Almanack* became a major commercial success across British North America.

Franklin’s business success allowed him to retire from active management of his printing enterprise at age forty-two, devoting his time to scientific research and public service. In the late 1740s, his pioneering experiments with electricity earned him international renown among European scientists. He formulated the single-fluid theory of electricity, introduced terms such as "charge," "conductor," and "battery," and demonstrated the electrical nature of lightning through his famous kite experiment, leading to the invention of the lightning rod.

Concurrently, Franklin spearheaded numerous civic initiatives that reshaped Philadelphia. In 1727, he gathered local tradesmen and intellectuals to form the "Junto," a mutual-improvement club dedicated to debating ethics, philosophy, and politics. In 1731, members of the Junto organized the Library Company of Philadelphia, creating the first successful subscription library in North America to provide public access to literature and scientific texts. Franklin subsequently assisted in organizing the Union Fire Company (1736), the American Philosophical Society (1743), the Academy and College of Philadelphia (1751, later the University of Pennsylvania), and the Pennsylvania Hospital (1751).

In public affairs, Franklin served as Deputy Postmaster General for the colonies starting in 1753, drastically improving postal efficiency and communication networks. During the French and Indian War, he presented the Albany Plan of Union (1754), an early framework for intercolonial governance. Representing the Pennsylvania Assembly, Franklin moved to London in 1757 to contest the tax-exempt status of the Penn family's proprietary lands, remaining in Great Britain for much of the next eighteen years as a representative for several American colonies.

Returning to Philadelphia in 1775 amid escalating conflict with Great Britain, Franklin was elected to the Second Continental Congress and appointed to the Committee of Five that drafted the Declaration of Independence. In late 1776, Congress dispatched Franklin to Paris as a commissioner to secure French assistance. His diplomatic skill, charm, and popularity in French society culminated in the Treaties of Alliance and Commerce (1778) and substantial financial loans that proved vital to the American victory. He subsequently negotiated the 1783 Treaty of Paris, securing British recognition of American independence. Franklin returned to Philadelphia in 1785, serving as President of the Supreme Executive Council of Pennsylvania and participating as a senior delegate in the 1787 Constitutional Convention.

### Personal Life

In September 1730, Franklin established a common-law marriage with Deborah Read, the daughter of his former Philadelphia landlady. The couple raised Franklin's illegitimate son, William (born c. 1730), alongside their own children, Francis Folger (born 1732, who died of smallpox at age four) and Sarah "Sally" (born 1743). William Franklin eventually served as the Royal Governor of New Jersey, which caused a permanent estrangement between father and son when William remained a Loyalist during the American Revolution.

Franklin maintained an extensive network of intellectual and personal correspondences across the transatlantic world. His religious views evolved over time; raised in a Calvinist household, he adopted Deism in his youth, emphasizing moral virtue, a benevolent Creator, and tolerance among religious sects. Throughout his life in Philadelphia, he supported various religious institutions and championed civic harmony among the city’s diverse ethnic and religious populations.

Late in life, Franklin addressed the institution of slavery. Although he had owned enslaved domestic servants earlier in his career and printed advertisements for slave sales in *The Pennsylvania Gazette*, his views changed significantly over the decades. By the 1780s, Franklin became an ardent abolitionist. In 1787, he was elected President of the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery, writing essays condemning human bondage and submitting a petition to the new United States Congress in 1790 calling for the abolition of the slave trade and the emancipation of enslaved people.

---

### Legacy

Benjamin Franklin died on April 17, 1790, at his home in Philadelphia at the age of eighty-four. An estimated 20,000 people attended his funeral procession to Christ Church Burial Ground in Center City Philadelphia, where he was interred beside his wife, Deborah. His death prompted national mourning and eulogies across the United States and Europe, recognizing him as a archetype of Enlightenment intellect and civic virtue.

Franklin’s physical and cultural imprint remains central to Philadelphia's urban identity. In the early twentieth century, city leaders designed a grand boulevard in the City Beautiful style to link Center City with Fairmount Park. Named the Benjamin Franklin Parkway in 1937, this thoroughfare became a cultural corridor housing institutions such as the Free Library of Philadelphia, the Franklin Institute, the Rodin Museum, the Barnes Foundation, and the Philadelphia Museum of Art.

Furthermore, the institutions Franklin founded—such as the Library Company of Philadelphia, the American Philosophical Society, the University of Pennsylvania, and Pennsylvania Hospital—continue to operate as major research, educational, and medical organizations. His voluminous personal and official correspondence, preserved in collections such as *The Papers of Benjamin Franklin* project, offers scholars a comprehensive window into eighteenth-century transatlantic history, politics, and culture.

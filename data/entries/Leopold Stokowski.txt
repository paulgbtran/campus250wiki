### Lead section

Leopold Anthony Stokowski (April 18, 1882 – September 13, 1977) was a British-born American orchestral conductor, organist, and arranger who stood as one of the most prominent classical music figures of the twentieth century. Best known for his transformative quarter-century tenure as music director of the Philadelphia Orchestra from 1912 to 1938, Stokowski molded the ensemble into one of the world's premier symphonic bodies, celebrated internationally for its lush, theatrical, and acoustically rich performance style often designated as the "Philadelphia Sound."

Stokowski was a showman and a relentless innovator who reshaped orchestral traditions. He discarded the conventional conductor’s baton early in his career, choosing instead to guide orchestras with expressive, free-form hand movements and fluid arm gestures. His performance techniques extended to altering traditional seating arrangements on stage, encouraging free bowing among string players, and experimenting continuously with acoustic design, recording technology, and broadcast radio.

Beyond his institutional achievements in Philadelphia, Stokowski gained broad popular fame through his pioneering work in film and popular media. Most notably, he collaborated with animator Walt Disney on the groundbreaking 1940 animated feature film *Fantasia*, conducting the Philadelphia Orchestra for the soundtrack and appearing on screen in a famous silhouette sequence shaking hands with Mickey Mouse. Throughout his seven-decade career, Stokowski introduced American audiences to dozens of landmark twentieth-century compositions and remained an active recording artist until shortly before his death at age 95.

### Early life and education

Leopold Stokowski was born on April 18, 1882, in London, England. He was the son of Kopernik Joseph Stanislaus Stokowski, a cabinetmaker of Polish heritage, and Annie Marion Moore, an Englishwoman of Irish descent. Though Stokowski later cultivated a stylized, enigmatic European accent and occasionally understated his English birth to enhance his artistic persona, his early upbringing and musical training were rooted in Victorian London.

Demonstrating exceptional musical aptitude as a child, Stokowski began studying the violin and piano at an early age. In 1896, at age 13, he was admitted to the Royal College of Music in London, becoming one of the youngest students ever accepted into the institution. There, he studied organ performance, harmony, and composition under distinguished faculty, including Sir Charles Hubert Hastings Parry and Sir Charles Villiers Stanford. He gained valuable practical experience serving as organist and choir director at St. James's Church, Piccadilly, starting in 1902.

In 1905, Stokowski emigrated to the United States after securing a position as organist and choirmaster at St. Bartholomew's Church in New York City. His expressive organ playing and charismatic personality quickly attracted attention within American musical circles. Seeking to transition from church music to orchestral conducting, Stokowski traveled to Europe during summers to observe leading European conductors and secure guest-conducting opportunities, making his professional orchestral conducting debut in Paris in 1909.

### Career

#### Cincinnati and Arrival in Philadelphia (1909–1912)
Stokowski’s orchestral conducting career began in earnest in 1909 when he was appointed music director of the Cincinnati Symphony Orchestra. Over three seasons in Cincinnati, he modernized the orchestra’s programming, instituted rigorous rehearsal standards, and gained recognition as a dynamic young talent. However, disputes with the orchestra’s board of directors led to his resignation in 1912.

#### The Philadelphia Orchestra Era (1912–1938)
Later in 1912, Stokowski accepted the post of music director of the Philadelphia Orchestra, beginning a legendary partnership that redefined American classical music. Over twenty-six seasons, he elevated the ensemble from a regional group to an international powerhouse. 

Stokowski fundamentally altered orchestral techniques to cultivate the trademark "Philadelphia Sound." He instructed string players to use "free bowing"—allowing individual musicians to draw their bows independently rather than in synchronized motion—which created a warmer, more continuous, and opulent string timbre. He reorganized the physical layout of the orchestra on the stage of the Academy of Music, moving woodwinds, brass, and percussion to maximize tonal warmth and projection. 

A fierce advocate for contemporary music, Stokowski presented the American premieres of numerous groundbreaking masterworks, including Gustav Mahler’s Symphony No. 8 ("Symphony of a Thousand") in 1916, Igor Stravinsky’s *The Rite of Spring* in 1922, and works by Sergei Rachmaninoff, Arnold Schoenberg, Alban Berg, and Edgard Varèse. He also popularized his own grand symphonic transcriptions of keyboard works by Johann Sebastian Bach, including the famous *Toccata and Fugue in D minor*.

#### Film, Broadcasts, and Later Orchestras
Stokowski recognized early the power of mass media to democratize classical music. He led the Philadelphia Orchestra in nationwide radio broadcasts and extensive commercial recordings for the Victor Talking Machine Company (later RCA Victor). In the late 1930s, he transitioned toward film projects, collaborating with Walt Disney on *Fantasia* (1940), which introduced multi-channel stereophonic sound reproduction (*Fantasound*) to cinema.

After stepping down as music director in Philadelphia in 1938 (succeeded by Eugene Ormandy), Stokowski formed the All-American Youth Orchestra in 1940, touring Latin America and the United States. He subsequently co-founded the New York City Symphony, conducted the NBC Symphony Orchestra alongside Arturo Toscanini, served as Music Director of the Hollywood Bowl Symphony Orchestra, and led the Houston Symphony from 1955 to 1961. In 1962, at age 80, he founded the American Symphony Orchestra in New York City, serving as its director for a decade.

### Personal life

Stokowski’s personal life was frequently highlighted in the press due to his high-profile relationships and marriages to prominent social figures:

1. **Olga Samaroff** (m. 1911; div. 1923): An acclaimed American concert pianist born Lucy Hickenlooper. She was influential in encouraging his early career move to Philadelphia. They had one daughter, Sonya.
2. **Evangeline Brewster Johnson** (m. 1926; div. 1937): Artist and heiress to the Johnson & Johnson pharmaceutical fortune. They had two daughters, Luba and Sadja.
3. **Gloria Vanderbilt** (m. 1945; div. 1955): Socialite, artist, and heiress. Despite an age gap of more than forty years, their marriage lasted a decade and produced two sons, Leopold Stanislaus and Christopher.

In the late 1930s, between his second and third marriages, Stokowski was involved in a widely publicized international romance with Hollywood actress Greta Garbo, traveling with her throughout Europe.

Stokowski maintained a vigorous physical regimen and continued conducting, recording, and editing scores well into his nineties. He died of a heart attack on September 13, 1977, at his home in Nether Wallop, Hampshire, England, at the age of 95.

---

### Legacy

Leopold Stokowski left an indelible mark on orchestral performance practice, sound recording history, and Philadelphia's cultural identity. His twenty-six years in Philadelphia established the city as an international capital of orchestral music and defined a sonic standard that influenced orchestral performance worldwide.

His willingness to embrace technological innovation made classical music accessible to vast new audiences through radio, long-playing records, stereophonic sound, and cinema. By championing contemporary composers alongside timeless classical masters, he broadened the modern symphonic repertoire and proved that an orchestral conductor could be both a serious artistic innovator and a major public figure.

### Lead Section

Live Aid was a massive dual-venue benefit concert held on Saturday, July 13, 1985, organized primarily by Irish musician Bob Geldof and Scottish musician Midge Ure to raise funds for the alleviation of the 1983–1985 Ethiopian famine. Billed as the "global jukebox," the event ran simultaneously at Wembley Stadium in London, United Kingdom, and John F. Kennedy (JFK) Stadium in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States. Combined with satellite performances and contributions in countries such as Australia, Japan, Austria, Yugoslavia, and the Soviet Union, the concert represented one of the largest satellite link-ups and television broadcasts in human history, reaching an estimated global audience of 1.9 billion people across 150 nations—nearly 40 percent of the Earth's population at the time.

The American leg of Live Aid, staged at Philadelphia’s historic JFK Stadium, attracted nearly 90,000 spectators and ran for more than 15 hours continuously. Promoted and managed in the United States by legendary concert impresario Bill Graham, the Philadelphia concert featured an extraordinary lineup of pop, rock, soul, and hip-hop royalty. Legendary acts performative of several eras took the stage, ranging from contemporary chart-toppers like Madonna, Duran Duran, and Run-D.M.C. to historic classic rock figures such as Bob Dylan, Eric Clapton, Neil Young, and Mick Jagger.

Beyond its musical accomplishments, the Philadelphia Live Aid event was a monumental technological and logistical feat. Using complex satellite relays, television networks around the globe broadcast synchronized performances between London and Philadelphia. A defining highlight of the event’s global coordination occurred when British musician Phil Collins performed at Wembley Stadium early in the day, boarded a Concorde supersonic airliner to fly across the Atlantic Ocean, and arrived in Philadelphia to perform a second set at JFK Stadium that evening.

The concert raised tens of millions of dollars directly for famine relief in East Africa and galvanized worldwide political and humanitarian awareness surrounding chronic hunger and international aid policy. Historically, the Philadelphia concert stood as the largest single musical event held in the region, cementing the city's place in popular music history and serving as a high-water mark for the charity super-concert movement of the late 20th century.

### Background

The impetus for Live Aid stemmed from international news reports highlighting the harrowing severity of the famine in Ethiopia between 1983 and 1985. In October 1984, BBC Television broadcast news reports by Michael Buerk with footage captured by cameraman Mohamed Amin, depicting severe starvation that claimed hundreds of thousands of lives. Moved by the tragedy, Bob Geldof of the Boomtown Rats and Midge Ure of Ultravox co-wrote the charity single "Do They Know It's Christmas?" recorded by a supergroup of British and Irish music stars under the name Band Aid in November 1984. The record became the fastest-selling single in UK history up to that point and raised millions for famine relief.

Inspired by Band Aid’s success, American artists led by Harry Belafonte, Quincy Jones, Michael Jackson, and Lionel Richie formed USA for Africa and recorded "We Are the World" in January 1985, which similarly dominated worldwide charts and raised vast humanitarian capital. Recognizing that continuous funding and heightened global attention were necessary, Geldof envisioned an unprecedented, live transatlantic charity concert spanning two major stadiums in the English-speaking world.

Selecting the American venue required a stadium capable of hosting nearly 100,000 fans with established infrastructure for intense broadcast engineering. After considering sites in New York City and Washington, D.C., organizers selected John F. Kennedy Stadium in South Philadelphia. Built in 1926 as the Stadium of the Sesquicentennial International Exposition, JFK Stadium possessed a vast 105,000-seat open-air capacity and a rich history of hosting massive rock festivals and Army–Navy college football games. American concert promoter Bill Graham took charge of coordinating the U.S. venue, working alongside local authorities, municipal emergency management teams, and network executives from ABC and MTV to secure security, transit, and continuous live broadcast transmission.

### Course of Events

The Philadelphia leg of Live Aid commenced at 8:51 AM Eastern Daylight Time on July 13, 1985, nearly two hours after the opening festivities began at Wembley Stadium in London (which started at 12:00 PM BST / 7:00 AM EDT). Under a sweltering July sun with temperatures exceeding 90°F (32°C), nearly 90,000 spectators packed into JFK Stadium. To keep the dense crowd safe from severe heat exhaustion, municipal fire crews periodically sprayed fans with fire hoses, while volunteers distributed free drinking water throughout the day.

The concert opened with a performance by local Philadelphia act Bernard Watson, followed by folk singer Joan Baez, who greeted the audience with "This is your Woodstock, and it's long overdue." Local Philadelphia rock band The Hooters delivered an energetic early set, capitalizing on their rapid commercial rise. Over the next 15 hours, dozens of iconic acts delivered succinct 15- to 20-minute performances using an innovative revolving stage designed to minimize set changeover times between groups.

Highlights from the afternoon included sets by hip-hop pioneers Run-D.M.C., metal pioneers Black Sabbath (reunited with original vocalist Ozzy Osbourne), acoustic supergroup Crosby, Stills & Nash, Judas Priest, Bryan Adams, The Beach Boys, and Simple Minds. As the afternoon progressed, Santana, Ashford & Simpson, and Tom Petty and the Heartbreakers kept the momentum building. Pop superstar Madonna performed a high-energy three-song set featuring "Holiday" and "Into the Groove," accompanied by dancers and backup singers.

The evening set brought several legendary moments. Phil Collins arrived at JFK Stadium via helicopter after his transatlantic Concorde flight, performing solo and sitting in on drums for Eric Clapton and later Led Zeppelin. The reunion of Led Zeppelin’s surviving members—Jimmy Page, Robert Plant, and John Paul Jones—was one of the most anticipated moments of the night, though plagued by technical and tuning issues. 

Soul legend Patti LaBelle delivered an extraordinary, gospel-infused performance of "Imagine." Philadelphia native duo Daryl Hall & John Oates performed a classic soul revue set alongside Temptations members Eddie Kendricks and David Ruffin. Mick Jagger performed a theatrical solo set, duet-ing with Tina Turner on "State of Shock" and "It's Only Rock 'n Roll (But I Like It)," during which Jagger playfully tore off Turner's skirt mid-performance. 

The concert approached its climax with Bob Dylan performing acoustic selections alongside Rolling Stones guitarists Keith Richards and Ronnie Wood. Dylan stirred minor controversy on-stage by suggesting that a portion of the funds raised ought to assist struggling American family farmers—a remark that directly inspired Willie Nelson and John Mellencamp to organize the first Farm Aid concert later that year. Finally, the entire American lineup joined on-stage to close the night with a massive group rendition of "We Are the World," matching London’s closing performance of "Do They Know It's Christmas?".

### Aftermath

Live Aid was an unprecedented financial, cultural, and political success. Initial broadcast estimates indicated that over $70 million had been pledged during the broadcast; total funds raised eventually exceeded $127 million ($350+ million in contemporary inflation-adjusted figures). The funds were distributed via the Band Aid Trust for emergency relief, grain delivery, medical equipment, and long-term agricultural development programs in Ethiopia and neighboring East African nations.

Despite its success, Live Aid faced logistical and political criticisms in subsequent years. Some international policy analysts argued that relief supplies were occasionally diverted or taxed by Ethiopian military dictator Mengistu Haile Mariam to support government counterinsurgency efforts. However, independent audits confirmed that the vast majority of aid reached vulnerable civilian populations, saving an estimated hundreds of thousands of lives.

For the city of Philadelphia, hosting Live Aid was a watershed moment in event management and cultural recognition. The concert demonstrated the municipality's capacity to host international megagatherings, laying groundwork for future large-scale events such as the 2005 Live 8 concert (held along the Benjamin Franklin Parkway) and the 2015 Papal visit. 

John F. Kennedy Stadium, however, was near the end of its structural lifespan. Aging infrastructure and safety concerns surrounding the concrete grandstands led to the stadium being condemned in 1989 and demolished in 1992. The site of JFK Stadium now forms part of the South Philadelphia Sports Complex, home to Lincoln Financial Field, Citizens Bank Park, and the Wells Fargo Center.

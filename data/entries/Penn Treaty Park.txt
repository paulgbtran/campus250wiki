### Lead Section

Penn Treaty Park is a historic seven-acre public park located along the Delaware River in the Fishtown neighborhood of Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Situated on land historically known as Shackamaxon, the park occupies the site where Quaker founder William Penn is traditionally believed to have entered into a peace treaty with Chief Tammanend (Tammany), a sachem of the Lenape Nation, under the canopy of a prominent great elm tree in late 1682 or early 1683. Although no physical written treaty from this exact meeting survives, the encounter established a foundational pact of friendship, non-aggression, and land co-existence that defined early relations between European settlers and indigenous peoples in colonial Pennsylvania.

The land on which the park sits underwent dramatic transformations across three centuries. Prior to European colonization, Shackamaxon served as an essential council ground and meeting venue for the Lenape clans of the Delaware Valley. During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, as Philadelphia expanded rapidly into an industrial metropolis, the surrounding riverfront evolved into a bustling center for shipbuilding, lumber yards, and maritime commerce. In 1893, in response to growing urban density and a desire to preserve the historic legacy of Penn’s peace accord, the City of Philadelphia officially acquired and dedicated the tract as a public municipal park.

Today, Penn Treaty Park features expansive green lawns, mature shade trees, a riverfront promenade offering panoramic views of the Delaware River and the Benjamin Franklin Bridge, and several historic monuments. Among these is the 1827 Penn Treaty Monument, an obelisk erected by the Penn Society to mark the location of the famous Treaty Elm, which fell during a storm in 1810. The park is managed by Philadelphia Parks & Recreation in partnership with the local community organization, the Penn Treaty Park Association.

Beyond its physical landscape, Penn Treaty Park holds international cultural and philosophical significance. The treaty signed at Shackamaxon was lauded throughout Enlightenment Europe—most famously by French philosopher Voltaire—as a rare historical exemplar of a peaceful pact made without religious oaths or military coercion. The site remains a powerful regional symbol of peace, reconciliation, Quaker heritage, and ongoing Lenape cultural identity.

### History

#### Indigenous Council Ground and Shackamaxon
Long before the founding of Pennsylvania, the site of Penn Treaty Park was known as *Shackamaxon* (often translated from the Unami Lenape dialect as "place of chiefs" or "place of eels"). Situated along the western bank of the river known to the native inhabitants as the *Lenapewihittuk* (the Delaware River), Shackamaxon occupied a strategic location between northern and southern Lenape territories. Native leaders routinely converged at the site to deliberate on governance, trade, alliance, and dispute resolution. The natural landscape was dominated by mature hardwood forests, including an exceptionally large American elm (*Ulmus americana*) that provided shade and served as a recognizable landmark for diplomatic assemblies.

#### Penn's Peace Accord (1682–1683)
Following the royal grant of the Pennsylvania charter to William Penn by King Charles II in 1681, Penn sought to institute a governance model rooted in Quaker principles of pacifism, religious freedom, and human equality—an initiative he termed his "Holy Experiment." Recognizing the sovereignty of the Lenape inhabitants, Penn dispatched commissioners to negotiate land purchases and established personal correspondence with indigenous leaders prior to his own arrival.

In late November 1682 or early 1683, shortly after arriving in the colony aboard the ship *Welcome*, Penn met with Chief Tammanend and other Lenape delegates beneath the Shackamaxon elm tree. Rather than negotiating a standard real estate transaction, the gathering produced an unwritten treaty of perpetual peace and mutual respect. According to oral tradition and historical records, the parties exchanged wampum belts and pledged that the Lenape and English settlers would "live in love as long as the creeks and rivers run, and while the sun and moon endure."

#### The Great Elm and Industrial Transformation
Throughout the eighteenth century, the "Great Elm" or "Treaty Elm" remained a revered site of pilgrimage for locals and foreign visitors. During the American Revolutionary War, British military forces occupied Philadelphia; despite widespread deforestation around the city for firewood, British general Simcoe ordered a guard to protect the Treaty Elm from damage out of respect for Penn's legacy.

On March 3, 1810, a violent storm overturned the historic tree. Tree-ring analysis indicated that the elm was approximately 283 years old at the time of its fall, measuring over twenty-four feet in circumference. Prior to its destruction, cuttings and scions were preserved, leading to the propagation of direct descendant elms that were subsequently planted across college campuses, parks, and historical grounds nationwide.

Following the fall of the tree, the surrounding riverfront became heavily commercialized. By the mid-nineteenth century, the area was dominated by shipyards, coal depots, iron foundries, and lumber yards, characteristic of the industrial growth that transformed Fishtown and Northern Liberties into manufacturing centers.

#### Creation of the Municipal Park (1893)
Recognizing the rapid loss of historical terrain to industrialization, public interest mounted in favor of creating a permanent memorial park at Shackamaxon. In 1827, the Penn Society had erected a marble obelisk on private industrial ground near the former location of the Great Elm. Decades later, through municipal land acquisitions in the late nineteenth century, the City of Philadelphia condemned and purchased the waterfront parcels surrounding the monument. Penn Treaty Park was officially opened to the public in 1893, securing seven acres of open green space for urban residents and establishing a permanent memorial to Penn and Tammanend.

### Architecture

The physical spatial design of Penn Treaty Park combines late nineteenth-century municipal park layout with modern riverfront open-space planning. Situated at the terminus of East Columbia Avenue and Delaware Avenue, the park transitions from the dense urban grid of Fishtown to an expansive, open waterfront lawn oriented toward the Delaware River.

#### Monuments and Sculptures
The architectural and sculptural centerpiece of the park is the **Penn Treaty Monument**, erected in 1827 by the Penn Society. The monument consists of a four-sided white marble obelisk standing on a square pedestal base. Each face of the obelisk bears distinct historical inscriptions detailing Penn's birth and death dates, the founding of Pennsylvania in 1682, the treaty under the Great Elm, and the dedication by the Penn Society.

Near the park entrance stands a full-length bronze **Statue of William Penn**, created by sculptor Frank Gaylord and dedicated in 1982 to mark the tricentennial of Penn's arrival in Pennsylvania. The statue depicts Penn in traditional seventeenth-century Quaker attire holding a scroll representing the charter of liberties. Surrounding the plaza are bronze interpretive plaques outlining the history of the Lenape nation, Chief Tammanend, and the diplomatic traditions of Shackamaxon.

#### Landscape and Built Features
The landscape architecture emphasizes unobstructed sightlines to the Delaware River. Key physical elements include:

*   **The Waterfront Promenade:** A paved pedestrian pathway lining the river wall, equipped with historicist iron railings and benches, providing views of shipping traffic, the Camden waterfront, and the Benjamin Franklin Bridge.
*   **The Central Gazebo:** A Victorian-style wooden bandstand pavilion situated near the park's central lawn, constructed to facilitate community gatherings, theatrical performances, and musical concerts.
*   **Tree Canopy and Scions:** The park features a varied tree canopy composed of native hardwoods, including descendant scions of the original Treaty Elm planted to maintain the biological continuity of the historical landmark.

### Cultural Significance

Penn Treaty Park holds a unique place in early American history, art, and political philosophy. The meeting between William Penn and Tammanend provided an enduring model for peaceful cross-cultural diplomacy, contrasting sharply with the violent conflicts that characterized colonial frontiers elsewhere in North America.

#### Philosophical and International Legacy
The Treaty of Shackamaxon achieved international renown during the Age of Enlightenment. French philosopher Voltaire penned a famous tribute to the event, calling it "the only treaty between those people [Native Americans] and the Christians that was not ratified by an oath, and that was never broken." Voltaire praised the Quaker principle of eschewing formal religious oaths while maintaining inviolable moral commitments to indigenous sovereignty.

#### Representation in Fine Arts
The scene of the treaty under the Great Elm became one of the most iconic subjects in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century American visual art:
*   **Benjamin West (1771):** Penn's son, Thomas Penn, commissioned American painter Benjamin West to produce *Penn's Treaty with the Indians*. West's large-scale oil painting depicted Penn, Lenape leaders, and Quaker merchants assembled beneath the sprawling elm tree. Although historically inaccurate regarding costume and age, the painting popularized the romantic iconography of the event across Europe and the United States.
*   **Edward Hicks:** Quaker folk painter Edward Hicks produced dozens of renditions of the treaty scene throughout the early nineteenth century, frequently incorporating the image into his famous *Peaceable Kingdom* series as an earthly realization of Isaiah’s biblical prophecy of harmony.

#### Contemporary Community and Indigenous Remembrance
In contemporary Philadelphia, Penn Treaty Park serves both as an active neighborhood park for the Fishtown community and as a ceremonial gathering place for regional Native American organizations. Annual observances, including Indigenous Peoples' Day celebrations, Lenape cultural festivals, and peace commemorations, are routinely held on the grounds, reaffirming the site's enduring legacy as a sanctuary of intercultural dialogue and co-existence.

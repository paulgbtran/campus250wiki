Betsy Ross

Betsy Ross (born Elizabeth Griscom) was an American seamstress and upholsterer who became a central figure in American folklore. According to popular tradition, she fashioned and helped design the first official national flag of the United States, the "Stars and Stripes," at the request of George Washington. While historical records confirm that she manufactured flags for the Pennsylvania navy and ran a successful upholstery business for decades, there is no contemporary documentary evidence confirming she designed or created the first American flag. Her story was first made public in 1870 by her grandson, William Canby, and has since become a deeply ingrained aspect of American revolutionary legacy.

Betsy Ross

Early life and education

Elizabeth Griscom was born on January 1, 1752, in Gloucester City, New Jersey. She was the eighth of seventeen children raised in a strict Quaker household as a member of the Society of Friends. She received her education in Quaker schools before becoming an apprentice to a Philadelphia upholsterer, where she mastered the skills of sewing, furniture stuffing, and textile work that would define her career.

Career and Marriages

By 1775, Betsy and her fellow upholstery apprentice, John Ross, whom she married in 1773, opened their own small shop in the commercial district of Philadelphia. Because John Ross was an Episcopalian, Betsy was disowned by the Society of Friends for marrying outside her faith.

Her life was heavily impacted by the American Revolutionary War:

  • First Marriage: Her husband John Ross joined a local militia company to fight in the Revolution and was killed in January 1776. Betsy continued operating the upholstery business independently.
  • Second Marriage: In June 1777, she married Joseph Ashburn, a merchant marine. His ship was captured during the war, and he died in an English prison in 1782.
  • Third Marriage: In 1783, Betsy married John Claypoole, a fellow prisoner of Ashburn who had delivered the news of his death to Betsy. Together, they joined the Free Quakers and ran the upholstery business, later maintaining it alongside Betsy's daughters, granddaughters, and nieces. Records note that she and Claypoole performed various jobs, including covering a desk at the State House (Independence Hall) with green fabric. She continued producing flags and upholstery goods until her later years.

The Flag Legend and Historical Analysis

The narrative that Betsy Ross designed and crafted the first American flag remained entirely unknown until 1870. Her grandson, William Canby, presented a paper titled "The History of the Flag of the United States" to the Historical Society of Pennsylvania. Canby claimed that in June 1776, George Washington, accompanied by a committee from the Continental Congress, visited Ross's back parlor. They allegedly presented her with a rough sketch of a flag, which Ross improved by suggesting a circular arrangement of stars and demonstrating how to cut a crisp, five-pointed star with a single snip of her shears, rather than the six-pointed stars initially proposed.

Modern historians classify this account as a national myth rather than verified fact. While the Continental Congress officially adopted the Stars and Stripes on June 14, 1777, there are no congressional logs, letters, or receipts connecting Ross to the creation of that initial flag. Archival evidence proves she was paid for making navy flags for Pennsylvania, but the legend of her inventing the national design relies entirely on family oral histories and affidavits recorded decades later by her descendants, such as her daughter Rachel Claypoole Fletcher.

The Betsy Ross House

The Betsy Ross House, located on Arch Street in Philadelphia, operates as a prominent museum and is one of the city's most visited historic sites, ranking third in popularity behind the Liberty Bell and Independence Hall. Historical records confirm that Betsy Ross rented property on this block between 1773 and 1786, though it remains a matter of historical debate whether she lived in the exact structure standing at 239 Arch Street or an adjacent property that now serves as the museum garden.

In 1898, the Betsy Ross Memorial Association launched a nationwide campaign where two million Americans donated dimes to rescue the building from disrepair and convert it into a national shrine. The building was officially donated to the city of Philadelphia in 1937 and fully restored with financial backing from millionaire Atwater Kent. Today, the courtyard houses the gravesite of Betsy Ross and her third husband, John Claypoole, after their remains were relocated there from previous burial sites.

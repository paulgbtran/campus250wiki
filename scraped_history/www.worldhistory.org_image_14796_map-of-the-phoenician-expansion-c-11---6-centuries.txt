The Phoenician expansion between the 11th and 6th centuries BCE grew out of the maritime
, whose skilled sailors and merchants created one of the ancient
’s most dynamic trading networks. As these
-states developed advanced shipbuilding and navigational techniques, they extended their commercial reach across
, and the western Mediterranean. Their
flourished through the exchange of timber, purple dye, metalwork, glass, and luxury goods, linking Near Eastern markets with
, and Anatolian spheres.
Over time, this commercial web evolved into a pattern of settlement and
. From the 10th century BCE onward, Phoenician communities established outposts and later urban colonies,most famously
(traditionally founded c. 814 BCE, during the reign of Pygmalion of Tyre,) along the coasts of North Africa,
, Sardinia, and the Iberian Peninsula. These colonies served as staging points for
, cultural exchange, and resource extraction, helping Phoenician influence span thousands of kilometers. By the 6th century BCE, shifting regional powers, including the
(c. 911–609 BCE) and the Neo-Babylonian
, 605–562 BCE), gradually curtailed Phoenician independence in the Levant. Yet their colonies continued to thrive, carrying forward Phoenician language,
, and commercial practices long after the homeland fell under imperial domination.

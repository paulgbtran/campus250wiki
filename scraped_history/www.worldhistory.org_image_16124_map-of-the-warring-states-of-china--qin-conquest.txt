Warring States period
(c. 475–221 BCE) represents the final and most transformative phase of the Eastern
(770–256 BCE), marked by political fragmentation, sustained interstate
, and profound institutional change. As
royal authority weakened, former vassal states asserted full sovereignty, adopting the title of “king” and pursuing territorial expansion through military
and diplomatic maneuvering. This era was not merely one of violence but of systemic restructuring: rulers implemented administrative centralization, codified laws, reformed taxation, and strengthened standing armies, accelerating the transition from aristocratic feudal networks to territorial states governed through bureaucratic mechanisms.
By the 4th century BCE, power had consolidated around seven major kingdoms: Qin, Chu, Zhao, Wei,
, Yan, and Qi, whose rivalry reshaped the political map of northern and central
. The state of Qin, strengthened by Legalist reforms under Shang
(4th century BCE), developed a highly centralized and militarized structure that proved decisive. In 221 BCE, King Zheng of Qin (reign 247–221 BCE) completed the conquest of his rivals and proclaimed himself

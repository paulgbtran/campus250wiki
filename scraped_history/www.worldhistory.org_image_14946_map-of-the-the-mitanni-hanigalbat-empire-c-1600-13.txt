Mitanni’s core lay in the Khabur River basin (modern northeastern Syria), where a predominantly Hurrian population was ruled by an elite whose royal names, treaties, and religious vocabulary reveal Indo-
influences, particularly in horse training and warrior ideology. This hybrid political
underpinned Mitanni’s military strength but also exposed its fragility. From the 14th century BCE, sustained pressure from the resurgent
(notably under Šuppiluliuma I, reign c. 1344–1322 BCE) and the expanding Middle Assyrian state eroded Mitanni authority. Internal dynastic struggles further weakened the kingdom, leading to its fragmentation and eventual absorption into Assyrian and Hittite spheres by c. 1300 BCE. Mitanni’s rise and fall illustrate the volatility of Late
Age geopolitics, where power depended less on territorial size than on diplomacy, military specialization, and control of strategic corridors.
